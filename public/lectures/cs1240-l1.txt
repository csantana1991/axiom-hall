[[BEAT 0]]
The board in front of you is titled worst case is a tax, not a lifestyle. Leave that sentence up while we count. A doubling array sometimes copies every stored element. You already hold n items, the capacity is full, and the next insert allocates a larger block and walks the old block so those items have new addresses. That walk is linear in n. If you bill every later insert as if it were that walk, you will refuse a structure that is cheap on almost every call. Amortized analysis refuses the tax. It bills the sequence.

Start with aggregate reasoning, because a quotient does not yet need a potential function. Aggregate analysis takes the total cost of n inserts and divides by n. The quotient is the average actual cost per insert. It says nothing reliable about one call. It says something about the bill for the whole run.

Use a doubling rule whose unit costs you can add by hand. A quiet insert, when the number of elements is strictly below the capacity, writes one new cell and costs 1. A growth insert, when the number of elements already equals a positive capacity, copies those elements into a block twice as large and then writes the new cell. Copying n elements and writing one more costs n plus 1. The first insert is different. The allocation is empty, there is nothing to copy, and you create a block of capacity 1 and write one cell. Charge that first insert a cost of 1. The notes call the empty allocation the special case. The ledger will show why.

Run eight inserts. Insert 1 costs 1. The table holds one element at capacity 1. Insert 2 finds the table full, copies one element, writes the new one, and costs 2. Capacity is now 2. Insert 3 finds the table full again, copies two elements, writes one, and costs 3. Capacity is now 4. Insert 4 finds room and costs 1. Insert 5 finds four elements in a block of four, copies four, writes one, and costs 5. Capacity is now 8. Inserts 6, 7, and 8 each find room and cost 1. Add the column. 1 plus 2 plus 3 plus 1 plus 5 plus 1 plus 1 plus 1 equals 15. Eight inserts cost 15 in total. The average is 15 divided by 8, which is 1.875.

Separate the 15 into writes and copies. The writes contribute 8, one per insert. The copies contribute 1 plus 2 plus 4, which is 7. The copy sizes form a geometric series. Each doubling copies a block twice as large as the previous copy, and the newest copy is larger than all earlier copies put together. For a run that stops on a full power of two, the copy sizes are 1, 2, 4, and so on, up through half the final population. Their sum is one less than that population. Writes equal the population. Total actual cost is twice the number of inserts, minus 1.

Check the pattern on the powers you can do without a machine. One insert costs 1, and twice 1 minus 1 is 1. Two inserts cost 3. Four inserts cost 7. Eight cost 15. Sixteen cost 31. Thirty-two cost 63. On each of those stopping times the average is 2 minus 1 over n. For eight inserts, 1.875. For sixteen, 31 divided by 16, which is 1.9375. For thirty-two, 63 divided by 32, which is 1.96875. The average on these rows climbs toward 2. It does not climb toward n.

That is the aggregate fact. The expensive inserts are real, and they are rare enough that the copies add up to less than the writes once the empty start is behind you. If you had billed each of the eight inserts at the worst cost in the run, which was 5, you would have written 40. The sequence cost 15. The tax would have been more than twice the actual bill. Stretch the same comparison to thirty-two inserts. The worst insert in that run copied sixteen elements and wrote one, so its cost was 17. Billing every insert at 17 produces 544. The sequence cost 63. The tax is no longer a rounding choice. It is a different budget.

Now change one growth rule, because a single policy can look artificially kind. Suppose you grow by one slot on every insert instead of doubling. Insert number k copies k minus 1 old elements and then writes the new one. Across eight inserts the copies are 0 plus 1 plus 2 plus 3 plus 4 plus 5 plus 6 plus 7, which is 28. Writes add 8. Total cost 36. Average 4.5, already worse than doubling's 1.875, and the shape is the problem. Across sixteen inserts the copies sum to 120 and the total with writes is 136, average 8.5. Across thirty-two inserts the total is 528, average 16.5. The average now tracks n over 2. You removed the rare spike by making every insert copy, and the sequence became quadratic. The sum of the first n counting numbers is n times n minus 1, all over 2. That is the copy bill. Doubling looks reckless if you stare at one spike and disciplined if you add the column. Growing by one looks gentle on the largest single copy and careless on the sum.

The slide names a third telling, and you should hear it before the potential arrives. Accounting charges part of a cheap step now so a later copy is already paid for. Each insert pays 1 for its own write and deposits something toward the move it will suffer later. Under doubling, the deposits taken during the quiet stretch are sized to cover the next copy. I am not closing that ledger on this board. Aggregate, accounting, and potential are three refusals of the same tax. Aggregate is the division you just performed, total cost divided by the number of inserts. Accounting is a story about tokens deposited on cheap steps. Potential is a per-step invariant that implies a bound of the same kind. The next board builds the invariant. This board only stops you from billing the worst insert as if it were the ordinary one.

You might ask whether an average of 1.875 means the structure is safe on a path that can afford only a few memory operations per call. It does not. Insert 5 in the run of eight cost 5 by itself. Whoever called that insert waited for the copy of four cells. The average describes the eight-call bill. It does not describe the fifth call. If the constraint you actually have is a deadline on every call, amortized reasoning has not met it. It has answered whether the sequence is expensive.

Here is what the aggregate claim does not mean. It does not mean every insert is cheap, and it does not mean the worst insert is order 1. A single quotient hides a spike. We just watched 15 over 8 look calm while the fifth summand was 5. It does not mean that growing by one slot is the serious response to the spike. You saw that response go quadratic. It also does not mean the constant 3, which the later boards will hold fixed, has already shown up in the average. On a full power of two the aggregate average sits just under 2. The 3 will be an upper bound manufactured by a savings account, with slack still sitting in that account at the end of the run. We have not defined the account.

One more reading of the same totals, so the geometric series is not a trick tied to the number 8. Let n be a power of two, and stop when the table is exactly full. Copies sum to n minus 1. Writes sum to n. Total cost is 2n minus 1. Divide by n. The average is 2 minus 1 over n. The worst insert inside that run is the growth that carried the table from n over 2 up to n. It copied n over 2 elements and wrote one, at cost n over 2 plus 1. For n equal to 1024, that growth costs 513, the whole run costs 2047, and the average is 2047 divided by 1024, which is just under 2. The caller who hit the growth from 512 to 1024 did not experience an average. That caller copied 512 cells. Aggregate analysis tells you the run was cheap per insert. It does not tell you the unlucky insert was cheap.

Worst case is a tax, not a lifestyle. The lifestyle of this structure, measured by the sum and then divided, is a small constant on the stopping times we computed. The tax, measured by the most expensive insert you can point at, grows without a fixed cap as the table grows. Amortized analysis is the choice to bill the lifestyle of the sequence. The next board builds a savings account so the same choice can be checked one insert at a time, including on the insert that copies.
[[BEAT 1]]
The board in front of you is titled the potential is a savings account. A potential is a number you attach to the state of the structure. You pick it so that an expensive step spends savings and a cheap step makes a deposit. The potential is not a field in the array. The running program does not store it, and it does not change the instructions that copy or write. It is an accounting device in the proof, and the lab prints it so you can audit the device.

Write the definition in words, because this is the formula the rest of the hour stands on. The amortized cost of step i, written a hat sub i, equals c sub i plus phi sub i minus phi sub i minus 1. Here c sub i is the actual cost of the step, the number of cells you really wrote or copied. Phi sub i is the potential after the step. Phi sub i minus 1 is the potential before the step. The difference phi sub i minus phi sub i minus 1 is the deposit if it is positive and the withdrawal if it is negative. Actual cost plus that difference is the amortized cost. A quiet step with a rising potential is charged more than it actually spent. An expensive step with a falling potential is charged less than it actually spent, because it is spending earlier deposits.

The telescope is an identity, and you should expand it on four steps before you trust it. Suppose the potential starts at phi sub 0. Then a hat sub 1 equals c sub 1 plus phi sub 1 minus phi sub 0. A hat sub 2 equals c sub 2 plus phi sub 2 minus phi sub 1. A hat sub 3 equals c sub 3 plus phi sub 3 minus phi sub 2. A hat sub 4 equals c sub 4 plus phi sub 4 minus phi sub 3. Add them. Every intermediate potential appears once with a plus and once with a minus. They cancel. The sum of the amortized costs equals the sum of the actual costs plus phi sub 4 minus phi sub 0. Rearrange that. The sum of the actual costs equals the sum of the amortized costs plus phi sub 0 minus phi sub 4. Nothing in that rearrangement asked the potential to be clever. Any numeric score you attach to the states will telescope. The modeling choice is which score makes the amortized costs small and keeps the rearranged bound pointed the right way.

The bound you want is an upper bound on the actual sum. Look at the rearranged identity. Sum of c sub i equals sum of a hat sub i plus phi sub 0 minus phi sub n. If phi sub n is at least 0 and phi sub 0 equals 0, then phi sub 0 minus phi sub n is less than or equal to 0. Dropping it only makes the right-hand side larger. Therefore the sum of the actual costs is less than or equal to the sum of the amortized costs. That is the sentence on the notes. If the potential starts at zero and never finishes negative, billing every step at its amortized cost covers the true total, with something left over exactly equal to the potential still on the books.

Put numbers on a toy account so the cancellation is visible. Let phi start at 0. Step 1 has actual cost 1 and leaves the potential at 2. The amortized cost is 1 plus 2 minus 0, which is 3. Step 2 has actual cost 1 and leaves the potential at 4. The amortized cost is 1 plus 4 minus 2, which is 3. Step 3 has actual cost 5 and leaves the potential at 2. The amortized cost is 5 plus 2 minus 4, which is 3. The expensive step withdrew 2 of savings. Step 4 has actual cost 1 and leaves the potential at 4. The amortized cost is 1 plus 4 minus 2, which is 3. The actual costs add to 1 plus 1 plus 5 plus 1, which is 8. The amortized costs add to 12. Ending potential is 4, starting potential is 0. The identity says the actual sum equals 12 plus 0 minus 4, which is 8. It checks. The inequality says 8 is at most 12. The slack of 4 is not a missing cost. It is savings still on deposit, and those savings are real in the ledger even though no cell stores them.

Change the starting potential, since the notes state the hypothesis phi sub 0 equals 0 for a reason. Keep the same four actual costs and the same later potentials, but suppose you began with phi sub 0 equal to 3 rather than 0. Then the first amortized cost becomes 1 plus 2 minus 3, which is 0, and the other three amortized costs stay 3, 3, and 3. The amortized sum is 9. The identity says the actual sum equals the amortized sum plus 3 minus 4, which is 9 minus 1, which is 8. Still true. The clean upper bound actual sum at most amortized sum is false, because 8 is not at most 9 is still true in this particular case, so pick the numbers so it fails. Let me adjust the ending. Suppose the four steps end at potential 0, and they start at potential 5, and the actual sum is 8 while the amortized sum, by the identity, equals actual sum minus phi sub 0 plus phi sub n, which is 8 minus 5 plus 0, which is 3. Then the actual sum 8 is larger than the amortized sum 3. Starting above zero put old savings on the books that this run was allowed to spend. If you forget those old savings and claim that the amortized column alone upper-bounds the run, you will underbill. The fix in the statement is either to start at zero or to add the starting potential back onto the amortized sum. The notes choose a start at zero.

You also need the ending potential to be nonnegative, and a small counterexample shows the direction of the failure. Suppose phi sub 0 equals 0, two steps have actual costs 1 and 1, and the potentials after the steps are 2 and then negative 5. Amortized cost of the first step is 1 plus 2 minus 0, which is 3. Amortized cost of the second is 1 plus negative 5 minus 2, which is negative 6. The amortized sum is negative 3. The actual sum is 2. Two is not at most negative 3. The identity still holds: actual sum equals negative 3 plus 0 minus negative 5, which is 2. The inequality died because the ending potential was negative. A potential that wanders below zero can make an amortized column look wonderfully small while the actual work was not. When you design phi, nonnegativity is not a style preference. It is the direction of the bound.

You might ask whether any formula can be called a potential as long as you write it on the board. The telescope will accept any formula. The bound will not. You need a start at zero, or an explicit starting balance added back. You need an ending value that is not negative, and you usually need that nonnegativity at every prefix if you want every prefix of the run to obey the same inequality. You also need the value to fall when the actual cost spikes, or the amortized cost of the spike stays large and you have learned nothing. A constant potential, for example, never falls and never rises. Every amortized cost equals the actual cost. The telescope is true and useless. Potential analysis begins when the choice of phi moves cost off the spike and onto the quiet steps without going broke.

Here is what this claim does not mean. It does not mean the savings account is money, cache, or spare capacity sitting in the allocator. It does not mean a negative amortized cost on a single row is a refund the caller receives. In the broken example the second amortized cost was negative 6, and that number did not make the copy cheaper. It meant the ledger had been allowed to go into debt. It does not mean that once you have a potential, every data structure with an occasional expensive step is cheap on average. The potential has to fit the step. A stack that charges 1 to push and occasionally scans the whole stack still needs a phi that is large exactly when a scan is imminent. Copying the formula from a doubling table onto an unrelated structure is not a proof. And the inequality does not say the actual cost and the amortized cost are equal on each row. They differ by the deposit or the withdrawal. Equality of the sums holds only after you put the starting and ending potentials back, and the one-sided bound holds only under the sign conditions.

Keep the identity in one sentence you can say without looking down. The sum of the actual costs equals the sum of the amortized costs plus the starting potential minus the ending potential. If you start at zero and end nonnegative, the actual sum is at most the amortized sum. The doubling table on the next board is only this sentence with a particular phi, chosen so each amortized cost is the constant 3 after a special first row, and so the account is full just before a copy and nearly empty just after it.
[[BEAT 2]]
The board in front of you is titled dynamic table, phi equals two n minus m. Here n is the number of elements stored, and m is the capacity. The potential of a state is phi of n, m, equal to two n minus m. Read it as a fill score. When the table is full, n equals m, so phi equals n. The account holds one unit per stored element. When the table has just been doubled, the old elements occupy about half the new capacity, and phi is near 0. The slide says the potential is zero just after growth. Take that as the instant the new block exists and the old elements fill exactly half of it, before the new element is written. In the one-step bill we will use, the copy and the write are packaged together, so the row ends at phi equal to 2 rather than at 0. Both descriptions are the same growth. The packaged row is the one whose amortized cost you can check against the constant 3.

A quiet insert raises n by 1 and leaves m alone. The potential goes from two n minus m to two times n plus 1, minus m. The change is 2. The actual cost is 1, the write. Amortized cost equals 1 plus 2, which is 3. That arithmetic does not depend on how full the table was, as long as you did not grow. Every quiet insert deposits 2 and is billed 3.

Try it on a state that is not about to grow. Let n equal 5 and m equal 8. Phi equals two times 5 minus 8, which is 2. After the quiet insert, n equals 6 and m is still 8. Phi equals 12 minus 8, which is 4. The change is 2. Actual cost 1. Amortized cost 3. One more quiet insert, from n equal to 6, ends at n equal to 7 and phi equal to 14 minus 8, which is 6. Again the change is 2 and the amortized cost is 3. The account is filling because the table is filling. Nothing expensive has happened. You are prepaying.

A doubling insert is the withdrawal. Just before it, n equals m, so phi equals n. You copy n elements and write the new one, so the actual cost is n plus 1. After the step, the capacity is two m, which is two n using the old n, and the population is the old n plus 1. Phi equals two times the new population minus the new capacity, which is two times old n plus 1, minus two times old n, which is 2. The potential falls from n down to 2, provided n is greater than 2. The change is 2 minus n. Amortized cost equals n plus 1, plus 2 minus n, which is 3. The n cancels. The copy was paid for by the savings that equaled n, and the leftover arithmetic is the same 3 you charged on a quiet step.

The cancellation is the reason for the factor 2 in two n minus m. Any score that is on the order of n just before growth and on the order of 1 just after it can pay for a linear copy. The specific factor 2 makes the quiet deposit equal 2, so quiet bill and growth bill land on the same constant. If you had used three n minus m, a quiet insert would change the potential by 3 and the amortized cost would be 4, while the value just after a doubling would be three times the new n minus twice the old n, which still grows with n. The account would not reset. The factor 2 is the one that resets the packaged row to the constant 2 and prices every ordinary row at 3.

Amortized cost is 1 plus 1, which is 2. Not 3. Do not force the constant onto the empty start. The notes isolate this row for that reason. After this row, the amortized column is 3.

Insert 2 finds n equal to m equal to 1. Actual cost is 1 plus 1, which is 2. New capacity 2, new population 2, phi equal to 4 minus 2, which is 2. The potential moved from 1 to 2, a change of 1. Amortized cost is 2 plus 1, which is 3. Here the phrase falls from n to 2 does not describe a drop, because n was 1. The general formula still bills 3, since n plus 1 plus 2 minus n equals 3 even when the potential rises.

Insert 3 finds the table full at 2. Actual cost is 2 plus 1, which is 3. New capacity 4, new population 3, phi equal to 6 minus 4, which is 2. The potential was already 2, so the change is 0. Amortized cost is 3 plus 0, which is 3. Insert 4 is quiet inside capacity 4. Actual cost 1. Population 4, phi equal to 8 minus 4, which is 4. Change 2. Amortized cost 3. Insert 5 doubles from 4 to 8. Actual cost 5. New population 5, phi equal to 10 minus 8, which is 2. The potential falls from 4 to 2. Amortized cost is 5 plus 2 minus 4, which is 3. Inserts 6, 7, and 8 are quiet. Each costs 1, each raises phi by 2, and each is billed 3. After insert 8 the table is full, n equals 8, m equals 8, and phi equals 8. The account is full again, one unit per element, exactly when the next insert will be expensive.

Check the telescope on this prefix of eight. Actual costs are 1, 2, 3, 1, 5, 1, 1, and 1. They sum to 15. The notes tell you to change the growth factor to 3 and expect a new constant, with the method left intact. Do that as a fresh ledger, not as a guess. Capacity becomes three times the old capacity when you grow. The potential that resets is no longer two n minus m. Use phi equal to three n minus m, all over 2. You might ask why the quiet steps are asked to pay 3 rather than 2, since the write is the only work a quiet step performs. [[BEAT 3]]
The board in front of you is titled what the bound does not say. The bound says that if the potential starts at zero and stays nonnegative, the sum of the actual costs is at most the sum of the amortized costs. After the special first insert, each amortized cost on the doubling table is 3, so a run of n inserts is billed at most something like 3 n once you grant the first row its 2. That is a statement about a sum. It is not a statement about the next call, and the notes are explicit. Amortized order 1 does not mean every call is order 1.

Put the spike back on the table at a size where nobody can shrug. Suppose the doubling table is full with n equal to 1024 and m equal to 1024. Phi equals 1024. The next insert copies 1024 elements and writes one. Actual cost 1025. After the step the capacity is 2048 and the population is 1025. Phi equals two times 1025 minus 2048, which is 2050 minus 2048, which is 2. The change in potential is 2 minus 1024, which is negative 1022. Amortized cost is 1025 minus 1022, which is 3. The algebra is the same cancellation you already trust. The caller does not get to call the cost 3. The caller copies 1024 cells on this call. The 1022 of savings are a proof artifact that reconciles the sum. They do not shrink the copy.

The notes give the operational version of that gap. A trading gateway that copies a megabyte on the hot path once a second is still late. Amortized reasoning can truthfully say the average is fine and still leave you late once a second. If your listener cares about the late call, you use the potential to prove the average, and then you look at the tail separately. The tail here is not a mysterious distribution. It is the growth row, and you know exactly when it happens. It happens when n equals m.

Look at how the running average moves, because the lab draws it, and a careless reading of the drawing will confuse you. The brass line is the constant 3. The cream line is the average actual cost so far. They are on the same board because the potential was chosen so the amortized column is that constant. They do not sit on top of each other at a small n. After eight inserts the cream line reads 1.875. After nine, just after the growth to capacity 16, it reads 24 divided by 9, or 8 thirds, about 2.667. Inside the next epoch the quiet inserts pull the average down. After sixteen, with the table full again, the total actual cost is 31 and the average is 1.9375. The following insert, the growth to capacity 32, costs 17. Cumulative actual cost 48 over 17 inserts, which is about 2.824. The next full table, thirty-two inserts, has total cost 63 and average 1.96875. The insert after that costs 33, and the average becomes 96 divided by 33, which is about 2.909.

Read the shape, not a slogan. Just after each doubling, when the ending potential is 2, the slack in the telescope is only 2, so the cumulative actual cost sits 2 below the cumulative amortized cost. The special first row billed 2 instead of 3, which is another unit of slack if you compare against 3 n. Along this subsequence the cream line climbs toward the brass line: about 2.667, then 2.824, then 2.909, and if you carry the same arithmetic to the growth that passes 1024, the average just after that growth is 3 times 1024, divided by 1025, which is about 2.997. When you stop on a full table instead, the potential equals n, the slack is about n, and the cream line sits near 2. Within an epoch of quiet inserts the cream line falls, but it falls away from 3 and toward 2, because you are adding ones to a sum whose expensive term is already behind you. The note that the average falls toward 3 is the long-run pressure of those post-growth peaks up against the constant, together with the lab's choice to draw both lines. It is not a claim that every prefix is a smaller number than the prefix before it, and it is not a claim that a full table of thirty-two elements has already arrived at 3. Your ledger is right if it oscillates. The identity is what you should check, not a monotone cartoon.

The same tail survives the change of growth factor. Under tripling, the ordinary amortized cost is 2.5, which is a kinder average, and the growth that takes a full table of n still costs n plus 1 on that call. A smaller constant is not a smaller copy. If you came here hoping the potential would shave the megabyte, it will not. It only shows that you are not paying a megabyte on the quiet calls, and that the quiet calls have been prepaying.

You might ask whether the right engineering move is to grow by one slot, since that policy has no geometric spike. You already computed the bill, and it is worth setting next to this board. For n inserts under growth by one, the copies sum to n times n minus 1, over 2. For 32 inserts that copy bill is 496 and the total with writes is 528, average 16.5, while doubling those 32 inserts cost 63, average just under 2, and hid a single insert of cost 17. Growing by one does not remove work. It spreads a quadratic amount of copying into every call. If the spike of doubling is unacceptable, the honest responses sit outside the equality. You can pre-size the table to a capacity you are willing to hold. You can grow earlier, in a place that is not the hot call. You can choose a larger growth factor and accept a different constant, remembering that the spike is still a full copy. None of those sentences is the potential. The potential proves the average for the policy you actually run.

Take one more prefix as a fully worked check, so the slack is a number you have divided yourself and not a shape you only heard described. If a drawing of the cream line looks like it is trying to meet the brass line and then walking away, you are watching the stop time move from a just-grown table to a full one. Neither drawing has repealed the identity.

Here is what the bound does not mean, said in the narrow way the gateway example requires. It does not mean the hot path is safe because the brass line is flat. A flat amortized column can sit beside an actual column that prints 1025. It does not mean order 1 describes latency, jitter, or a deadline. Those words ask about one call or about the bad calls. The inequality asks about a sum. It does not mean the slack is waste you should try to optimize away inside the proof. The slack is the ending potential. On a full table it is large because you have finished prepaying the next copy and have not spent it yet. Zeroing it by hand would break the next growth's cancellation. It does not mean a structure with amortized order 1 is the wrong structure for a gateway. It may be the right structure for the throughput of the session and the wrong structure for the call that copies a megabyte. Those two judgments use the same ledger and answer different questions. You prove the average with phi equal to two n minus m. Then you look at the tail separately, on purpose, because the bound did not look at it for you.

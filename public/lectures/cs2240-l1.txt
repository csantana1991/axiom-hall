[[BEAT 0]]
The tape does not rewind. A stream is too long to store. You may keep a sketch whose size you chose, and you may not ask for the previous element again. Market data has this shape even when the file would fit on a disk, because the decision has to happen before the next packet. The contract is one pass, or a pass you can afford, and state that is little-o of the length, ideally polylogarithmic in that length. Failure probability is part of the answer, not a footnote you add after the number looks pretty.

Hear the constraint as a ban on rereading, not as a compliment to compression in general. If you can pause the tape, scan it twice, and keep every symbol, you are not in the setting this lecture is about. You are in ordinary storage. The stream setting starts when a second look is either impossible or too late to be the decision. A market packet that you needed yesterday is not repaired by a file that still holds it. The decision moved on. The sketch is what you had in hand when the packet arrived.

The lab makes the constraint visible by using an alphabet small enough to audit. Eight futures roots fit on the screen. They are small enough that you can know the true count of each one. The sketch is still run as if the alphabet were large. That is deliberate. Comparing an estimate to the truth is the privilege of a toy. If you could not see the true count, a sketch demonstration would be theater. Eight symbols fit, so every overestimate can be seen. The algorithm does not get easier when the alphabet grows. Your ability to audit it does. Remember which half is the privilege.

Do a first numeric pass with the lab’s own opening move. Stream the same symbol five times. The truth for that symbol is five. You have not kept the five events as five records. You have updated a sketch whose size you chose before the five arrived, or at least whose size does not grow with a longer tape. The estimate the sketch returns is at least the truth. Five is the floor for this symbol after this opening. If the estimate is five, some row of the sketch kept a clean look at those arrivals. If the estimate is larger, a collision in some row inflated a counter, and the minimum across rows is what kept the damage from being worse. You will take the mechanism apart in the next lesson. Here you only need the contract: one pass, a bounded sketch, an estimate that is not allowed to pretend it stored the tape, and a truth you can see because the alphabet is eight.

Change one input. Stream that same symbol seven times instead of five, and leave the alphabet at eight. The truth is now seven. The estimate is still at least the truth, so seven is the new floor. You still may not rewind to recount the first five and then add the last two by looking backward. The two extra arrivals are new updates. The sketch either absorbed them or it did not. An estimate of seven meets the floor. An estimate of nine is an overestimate of two, which you are allowed to notice because eight roots fit on the screen, and which you would have no business calling “about right” without a bound. The next beat is where the bound gets its symbols. This beat only insists that seven replaced five because the tape grew, and that the tape still does not rewind.

You might ask why you refuse to store the tape when eight symbols would fit in eight counters. Because the lab is a toy alphabet run as if the alphabet were large. Eight counters would be an exact histogram, and exact histograms are allowed only when the alphabet is tiny. The slide says that directly. The eight roots are tiny, which is why you can audit. The sketch is not granted an easier algorithm in exchange for your audit. If you store eight exact counters, you have left the exercise and solved a different problem, the problem of a tiny alphabet. That solution is honest when you name it as an exact histogram. It is not a sketch, and it will not survive the day the alphabet stops being eight.

What the one-pass contract does not mean is that two passes are immoral, or that a file which happens to fit must be deleted. It means that if your decision cannot wait for a second pass, your state has to be the sketch you chose, and the estimate has to come with a failure probability. It also does not mean that little-o of the length is a specific table you can cite without parameters. The slide’s ideal is polylogarithmic state. A sketch whose size tracks the stream has broken the contract even if the arithmetic of some particular afternoon stayed small. And it does not mean the eight roots are a market. They are a toy small enough for the truth to be visible. A real tape of trades can be dominated by a few symbols, which is why point queries matter more than a full histogram, and that domination is not a promise that eight will be the number.

Count what “too long to store” is refusing, with the five-arrival opening and the eight-symbol alphabet side by side. Five arrivals of one symbol, if stored raw, are five records. A mix that follows, say the lab’s mix after the five, adds more records. You do not get a budget for those records. You get a sketch of a size you chose. The exact alternative, eight counters, is constant in the length of the tape and linear in the alphabet. Constant in the length satisfies a memory bound. Linear in an alphabet of eight is cheap only because eight is tiny. The lecture’s warning is that you must not learn the wrong lesson from the cheapness. When the alphabet grows, eight counters become a dictionary of the alphabet, which is the storage the stream setting refused. The sketch’s job is to stay sized by the guarantee you ask for, not by the number of distinct roots that showed up.

Suppose you cheat once and rewind after the seven arrivals, recount, and fix the estimate down to the truth. You have produced a pretty number. You have also left the definition. An algorithm that returns a pretty number is not, by that prettiness, a streaming algorithm. The next beat will say what replaces prettiness: a named norm, an epsilon, a delta. Until then, notice that the rewind made the seven exact and taught you nothing about a packet that cannot be reread. Market data that must be decided before the next packet does not offer you that recount. The file fitting on disk does not offer it either, if the decision has already gone by.

A second small change of input, on the alphabet rather than the repetition count. Keep five identical arrivals, and imagine you audited only four of the eight roots because the screen was crowded. You can still see those four truths. You cannot claim the sketch behaved on the roots you did not look at. The privilege of comparing estimate to truth is limited to the symbols you actually counted by hand. The algorithm’s obligation is not limited that way. It runs as if the alphabet were large, including the roots you skipped. Partial audit is still a toy. It is a smaller toy. Do not promote it to a proof that collisions were absent in the half you ignored.

The sentence you should be able to say without a drawing is the contract, not a product name. One pass, because the tape does not rewind and the decision may precede the next packet. State smaller than the stream, ideally polylogarithmic, because the stream is too long to store. A sketch whose size you chose. An alphabet of eight, small enough that five arrivals, or seven, can be checked by eye, and still processed as if you did not have that privilege. Exact storage only if you admit the alphabet is tiny and you are keeping a histogram. Failure probability reserved for the bound, which is next, and not waved away because the five happened to look plausible.

[[BEAT 1]]
Approximation with a guarantee is the part that makes a sketch a streaming algorithm rather than a compression trick. You want a bound. With probability at least one minus delta, the estimate sits within epsilon of the truth, in a norm you named. Space then scales like the bound, not like the stream. An algorithm that returns a pretty number is not, by itself, a streaming algorithm. Prettiness has no delta in it.

Read the bound as three choices you owe the listener before you owe them a diagram. First, the norm. Within epsilon of the truth means nothing until you say whether you are talking about a point frequency, a vector of frequencies, or some other distance. Second, epsilon, the slack you are willing to tolerate in that norm. Third, delta, the probability you allow yourself to be wrong. One minus delta is the probability you are promising to be right. If you omit delta, you have stated a certainty the analysis does not give you. The slide is blunt: failure probability is part of the answer.

Put numbers in the three slots so the sentence cannot hide. Let epsilon be one tenth, and let delta be one tenth. Then the promise is: with probability at least nine tenths, the estimate sits within one tenth of the truth, in the norm you named when you chose epsilon. Nine tenths is one minus one tenth. The sketch’s memory is whatever the analysis says this epsilon and this delta require. It is not a function of how many packets were in the file. If the tape doubles and epsilon and delta stay put, the space stays put, under a guarantee of that form. That is the sense in which space scales like the bound and not like the stream. Little-o of the length, ideally polylogarithmic, is the shape you were already promised. Epsilon and delta are how you pick a point on that shape.

Change one input. Keep epsilon at one tenth and change delta from one tenth to one hundredth. One minus delta is now ninety-nine hundredths. You are asking to be right more often. You should expect the sketch to spend more state on that request, because depth, in the structure you will meet next, is the parameter that buys a smaller failure probability. This beat does not yet cash the formula that turns one hundredth into a row count. It does say the direction. A smaller delta is a stronger promise, and stronger promises are not free. If someone offers you the same sketch for both deltas, they have not changed the guarantee. They have only changed the sentence.

You might ask whether a demonstration that matched the truth on all eight futures roots has set delta to zero. It has not. Eight roots are small enough to know the truth, and a match on a toy tape is one tape. The guarantee is a probability over the algorithm’s randomness, or over the hashes it drew, for a bound you stated. A single matching afternoon does not drive delta to zero, and it does not let you delete epsilon from the claim. Comparing estimate to truth is the privilege of the toy. The privilege lets you see an overestimate. It does not promote the overestimate into a calibrated epsilon. The lab grid you will use later is a picture, and this lecture already warns you that pictures are not the bound. Here, the warning is earlier and simpler: do not read a successful audit as a proof that failure became impossible.

What the guarantee does not mean is that every estimate is within epsilon, full stop. The phrase “at least one minus delta” is doing the work. On some draws you are outside the slack, and that is not a broken theorem. It is the delta you budgeted. What it also does not mean is that epsilon is a relative error, or an additive error, until you name the norm. “Within epsilon of the truth” is incomplete on purpose in the opening statement. You complete it by naming the norm. A point-frequency norm and a norm on the whole vector are different promises, and a sketch built for one is the wrong tool for the other. What it also does not mean is that you may add epsilon to a pretty printout after looking at the truth. You state epsilon and delta when you size the sketch, before the tape flatters you.

Connect the bound back to the five arrivals, without pretending five is a general epsilon. The truth is five. An estimate of five sits at distance zero, which is inside any positive epsilon you named. An estimate of six sits at distance one. Whether one is “within epsilon” depends on the norm. If the norm is the raw count, distance one is within epsilon only if epsilon is at least one. If the norm scales by the length of the stream, a distance of one after a long tape is a small fraction, and epsilon might cover it. You do not get to say “close enough” in place of that sentence. After five identical arrivals and nothing else, the length is five. A later mix lengthens the tape and changes the fraction. The norm you named has to be stable enough that those two situations are not described by the same casual word.

Take the nine-tenths promise and ask what a hundred independent sketches would owe you, as arithmetic on the probability, not as a new theorem. If each sketch fails with probability at most one tenth, you do not add the tenths and declare that ten sketches must fail. The bound is per sketch, as stated. A hundred sketches each promising nine tenths are a hundred promises, not a single fused probability, unless you do the combination carefully and state it. The lecture does not ask you to fuse them. It asks you not to drop delta. If you report one number from one sketch, the delta that belongs next to it is the delta you sized, for example one tenth, and the probability you claim is nine tenths, not certainty.

Change the epsilon instead of the delta, so both knobs have moved once. Let delta stay at one tenth, and let epsilon go from one tenth to one twentieth. You are asking for a tighter neighborhood of the truth, with the same failure probability. Space has to answer that request. Width, in the structure that is coming, is the parameter that buys a smaller additive error. Again you are not cashing the ceiling formula yet. You are noticing that epsilon moved and the sketch is supposed to move with it. A picture with a fixed grid does not become a one-twentieth sketch because you wanted a tighter number on the day. The lab will eventually tell you its grid is too small to cash the bound. Believe that when you get there. For now, believe the weaker version: the bound, not the tape length and not your hope, sets the size.

A pretty number fails this beat in a specific way you can say out loud. Suppose the estimate prints as five, matching the five arrivals, and the writeup says nothing about epsilon or delta. The printout is compatible with a guarantee and also compatible with a lucky collision that did not happen to hurt. The listener cannot tell which. Adding the words “approximately five” does not introduce a delta. Adding “with probability at least nine tenths, within one tenth, in this named norm” does. That second sentence may be true or false of the sketch you built. It is at least the right kind of sentence. The first kind is how streaming algorithms get replaced by demonstrations.

You should leave able to refuse two confusions. The tape’s length is not the space. The space follows epsilon and delta, and the ideal shape is polylogarithmic in the stream, little-o of its length, not a second copy of it. And a perfect look at eight roots is an audit, not a zero failure probability. State the norm, the epsilon, and the delta. Then build something whose analysis can carry them. The something, for point frequencies, is next, and it is not a tool for every moment of the frequency vector.

[[BEAT 2]]
Frequency moments, said once, so you stop reaching for one sketch and calling it every question. The zeroth moment is the distinct count, how many different symbols appeared at least once. The first moment is the length, how many arrivals you saw, counting repeats. The second moment punishes heavy hitters. It is the moment that grows with the square of a count, so a symbol that appears often contributes far more than a rare symbol does. Sketches specialize. A count-min sketch estimates point frequencies. It is the wrong tool for a distinct-count brag.

Say the three moments on one short tape so the names become counts. Use the eight futures roots, and suppose the tape is seven copies of the first root, then one copy each of three other roots. You have named four distinct symbols, so the zeroth moment is four. The length is seven plus one plus one plus one, which is ten, so the first moment is ten. The second moment squares the counts before it adds them. Seven squared is forty-nine. Each of the three single arrivals squares to one. Forty-nine plus one plus one plus one is fifty-two. The heavy symbol contributed forty-nine of those fifty-two. That is what “punishes” means. The first moment treated the heavy symbol as seven out of ten. The second treated it as forty-nine out of fifty-two. A sketch built to track the second moment is answering a different question from a sketch built to track how many roots appeared, and both are different from a sketch that answers “how many times did this particular root appear.”

Point frequency is the question a count-min sketch is for. On this tape the first root has frequency seven, and each of the three rare roots has frequency one, and the four roots you did not send have frequency zero. A point query names one symbol and wants that number, or a guaranteed overestimate of that number. It does not want four, the distinct count, and it does not want fifty-two, the second moment, and it does not want ten except insofar as ten is the length that later scales an additive error. If you brag that the sketch “handled the tape,” say which moment you asked it about. The lecture’s specialization sentence is there because the brag is usually the zeroth moment, distinct count, which this sketch does not estimate.

Change one input. Keep the three rare roots at one arrival each, and change the heavy root from seven arrivals to two. Distinct count stays four, because the same symbols appeared. The length becomes two plus one plus one plus one, which is five. The second moment becomes four plus one plus one plus one, which is seven, because two squared is four. The heavy symbol no longer dominates the second moment the way forty-nine dominated fifty-two. Two out of five in the length, four out of seven in the second moment, is a milder punishment. The point frequency of the heavy root is now two, not seven. A count-min query for that root is aiming at two. A distinct-count question is still aiming at four, and you still do not get four out of a point-frequency sketch by asking it something clever. Changing seven to two moved the first and second moments and the point query. It did not move the zeroth moment. That independence is a good reason not to pretend one structure tracks all three.

You might ask whether, with only eight roots, you should skip sketches and compute every moment exactly. You may, and you must say that you did. Exact histograms are allowed only when the alphabet is tiny. Eight is the lecture’s example of tiny. An array of eight counters gives you every point frequency, the zeroth moment by counting how many counters are positive, the first moment by summing them, and the second moment by summing squares. That is a complete answer for a toy alphabet. It is not a streaming algorithm for a large alphabet, and the lab runs the sketch as if the alphabet were large so you do not learn the exact array as the method. If the alphabet later has millions of symbols and you still allocate a counter per symbol, you stored the problem. The moments are still defined. The space is no longer following a bound. It is following the alphabet.

What “the second moment punishes heavy hitters” does not mean is that a heavy hitter is a moral failure or a trading signal. It means the square amplifies large counts relative to small ones, as forty-nine against three singles showed. What the specialization also does not mean is that a count-min sketch is inaccurate on purpose about distinct count and therefore almost a distinct counter. It is the wrong tool. Wrong does not mean “usable if you squint.” A point-frequency overestimate, queried for every symbol you can name, does not become a distinct count. Symbols that never arrived can still pick up collisions and look positive, and symbols that arrived can share cells. You would be inventing a zeroth-moment algorithm out of a structure the lecture has already assigned to point queries. Distinct count has its own sketches. One of them will be named later so you do not reach for it by accident here. It is not this lab.

Compute the second moment another way, from the definition, so the square is not a story. Frequencies four, three, and three, on a tape of length ten, three distinct symbols. Zeroth moment three. First moment four plus three plus three equals ten. Second moment sixteen plus nine plus nine equals thirty-four. The largest single square, sixteen, is less than half of thirty-four. Compare with the earlier tape whose squares were forty-nine, one, one, and one, totaling fifty-two, where one symbol was almost the whole moment. Both tapes have first moment ten. Their second moments, fifty-two and thirty-four, tell the two shapes apart. The first moment cannot tell them apart, because you built them to have the same length. If your question is “are we dominated by a few symbols,” the second moment is the one that moves. If your question is “how long was the tape,” the first moment is the length, and both tapes answer ten. If your question is “how often did this one symbol appear,” you want a point query, and the count-min sketch is the tool this course gives you for that question.

A heavy-hitter story that stays inside the lecture is the slide’s own. A few symbols dominate a tape of trades. Point queries matter more than a full histogram. That is a reason to estimate frequencies of named symbols rather than to materialize every bin. It is not a reason to report the zeroth moment from a structure that never tracked distinctness, and it is not a reason to skip epsilon and delta. Domination explains why you care about a point. The guarantee from the previous beat explains what you are allowed to claim about the estimate. The one-pass contract explains why you do not store the tape in order to answer the point. Three beats, three different refusals.

Try a distinct-count brag in numbers and watch it fail honestly. Alphabet of eight. You query all eight roots in a count-min sketch after the tape of frequencies seven, one, one, and one, with four roots absent. Suppose collisions make two absent roots return an estimate of one, while the true absent count is zero. A naive “how many estimates were positive” returns six. The zeroth moment is four. Six is not four. The sketch did not malfunction as a point estimator merely because an absent root picked up a collision. Overestimation of a point is the behavior you will prove one-sided in the next lesson. Using those overestimates as a distinct count is the brag the lecture forbids. If both absent roots had stayed at estimate zero and the four present roots stayed positive, you might have printed four by luck. Luck on eight roots is the toy’s privilege. It is not a distinct-count algorithm.

Change the heavy frequency once more, from seven to seven on two different roots, so the second moment’s punishment is shared. Frequencies seven and seven. Zeroth moment two. First moment fourteen. Second moment forty-nine plus forty-nine, which is ninety-eight. Each heavy root is half the second moment, and a point query is still a query about one of them, aiming at seven, not at ninety-eight and not at two. A count-min sketch, asked about one root, is in its job. Asked “how many distinct,” it is out of its job. Asked for the second moment as a single scalar, it is out of its job as well. You said the moments once. You do not blend them back together in the last minute of the hour.

The lab’s five identical arrivals are the smallest case of the same split. Zeroth moment one, if nothing else arrived. First moment five. Second moment twenty-five. Point frequency five. The estimate is at least five. If it prints five, you matched the point frequency on this toy. You have not measured a distinct-count guarantee, because there was only one symbol to count and the alphabet was eight. Stream the mix the lab asks for after the five, recompute the three moments by hand, and only then look at the sketch. The sketch owes you a point estimate at least as large as the truth for the symbol you query. It does not owe you the zeroth moment, and it does not owe you the square sum. Those are different questions, said once, on purpose, so the later hour can be about one structure without pretending it is all of them.

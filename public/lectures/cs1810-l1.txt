[[BEAT 0]]
The idea in this opening block is a distinction you will use every time you fit a model. Risk is expected loss under the distribution you will be scored on. Empirical risk replaces that expectation with a sample average. The substitution is the entire method, and it is the entire opportunity to fool yourself.

Say the population object first, before any data. You have a parameter, call it theta. You have a prediction rule that depends on theta. You have a loss that takes the observed outcome and the prediction and returns a number. The risk of a parameter is the expectation of that loss when the pair you are scored on is drawn from the scoring distribution. In words, R of theta equals the expected value of the loss of y and f sub theta of x. Nothing in that sentence is a sum over a file. It is an average against a distribution. If you do not have the distribution in your hand, you do not have the number R of theta, even though you can write the symbol.

Empirical risk is the object you can compute from a finite list. With n training pairs, you replace the expectation by the sample mean. In words, the empirical risk is one over n times the sum of the loss on each training pair. The hat on R sub n is the whole warning. It marks a statistic, a function of the particular list you drew, not the expectation. The fitting rule in this lesson then chooses a parameter in the argmin of that average. In words, theta hat is an element of the argmin, over theta, of one over n times the sum of the losses. Read the order of the operations. You freeze the sample first. You vary the parameter second. You keep a parameter that makes this average as small as the model class allows. That is not the sentence "keep a parameter that makes the expected loss small."

A minimizer of the empirical risk is not a minimizer of the risk. Hold that as a refusal of an identification, not as a mood about models. The training procedure returns an argument of a sample average. The scoring problem asks for an argument of an expectation, when such an argument exists in the class. Those are different mathematical objects. They can land on the same point for a particular sample and a particular scoring distribution, and you still do not get the identification for free from the definition. This lesson does not prove a theorem that closes the gap. It names the gap, because the gap is where you fool yourself.

Let me put the average on numbers, using squared error, one of the two losses this lesson names as convex in the linear predictor. Take a one-dimensional parameter and let the loss on an outcome be the square of the outcome minus the parameter. The outcomes I am about to average are not a market tape and not a file in the lab. They are a list chosen so the definition has arithmetic you can check by hand. Let the training outcomes be 2, 4, and 6. Then n is 3.

The empirical risk is one third of the sum of those three squares. Differentiate before you search, because the derivative is what you will recompute when one outcome changes. The derivative of one third times the sum of squares, with respect to the parameter, is one third times the sum of 2 times the parameter minus the outcome. Set the derivative to zero. The factors one third and 2 are not zero, so they leave the stationarity condition. The sum of the parameter minus each outcome is zero. Three times the parameter equals the sum of the outcomes. The sum of 2, 4, and 6 is 12, so the parameter is 4. For squared error, empirical risk minimization on this list returns the sample mean, and the sample mean is 4.

Now compute the value, not only the location. At 4 the squared errors are 4, 0, and 4. The sum is 8. Divide by 3 and the empirical risk is 8/3, about 2.667. Check a neighbor. At 5 the squared errors are 9, 1, and 1, sum 11, average 11/3, about 3.667. At 3 the squared errors are 1, 1, and 9, the same average 11/3. At 0 the squared errors are 4, 16, and 36, sum 56, average 56/3, about 18.667. Among these candidates the training average is smallest at 4, and the derivative says that point is the unique minimizer on the line. If a program returns a different parameter for squared error on 2, 4, and 6, the program is wrong about the definition.

Change one input and recompute. Change the third outcome from 6 to 12 and leave 2 and 4 untouched. The new sum is 18. The new mean is 6. The empirical minimizer moves from 4 to 6. At 6 the squared errors are 16, 4, and 0, sum 20, average 20/3, about 6.667. At the old answer 4, the squared errors are 4, 0, and 64, sum 68, average 68/3, about 22.667. One substituted outcome moved the argmin by 2 and made the previous argmin look expensive on the new average. That is ordinary arithmetic of an average. Empirical risk is that average, so it moves when the list moves.

The same definition with four outcomes makes the factor one over n harder to skip. Let the outcomes be 1, 1, 1, and 9. The sum is 12, n is 4, and the sample mean is 3. Squared errors at 3 are 4, 4, 4, and 36, sum 48, empirical risk 12. At the parameter 1, which matches three of the four outcomes exactly, the squared errors are 0, 0, 0, and 64, and the average is 16. Sixteen is worse than 12. The single 9 pulls the minimizer of the average off the cluster at 1. A different functional of the same four numbers, for example a median, would have stayed at 1. This lesson is not claiming that the mean is the right summary for every scoring rule you will ever meet. It is claiming that once the loss is squared error and the objective is the sample average, the minimizer is the mean of the list, and that mean is a fact about the list.

Separate that fact from risk. For squared error, risk is the expectation of the square of y minus the parameter, under the distribution you will be scored on. Add and subtract the scoring mean, call it m. Then y minus the parameter splits into y minus m, plus m minus the parameter. Square the sum. The cross term has expectation zero because y minus m has mean zero. The expectation that remains is the variance of y under the scoring distribution, plus the square of m minus the parameter. The variance does not depend on the parameter. Every candidate is therefore ranked by its distance to m. The minimizer of risk is m. It is not, by anything in the definition, the mean of the training list.

Put a number on the excess. Suppose the scoring mean is 5, and suppose you still report the training minimizer 4 that you computed from 2, 4, and 6. The excess piece of risk, beyond the variance that every parameter pays, is the square of 5 minus 4, which is 1. The training average 8/3 being the best average on that list does not pay the 1 down. Change the supposition. If the scoring mean is 4, the excess of reporting 4 is 0, and for that particular scoring mean the two minimizers agree. The agreement belongs to the supposition, not to the hat. If the scoring mean is 6, the excess of reporting 4 is 4, and the excess of reporting 6 is 0. Same training list, different scoring mean, different verdict on the parameter you shipped.

You might ask whether a huge sample size makes the distinction dissolve. This lesson does not give you a rate, so I will not invent one. Empirical risk at a frozen parameter is a sample average of losses. A long average can sit near an expectation, and it can fail to, and neither sentence is proved on this board. Even if you grant that the average loss is close to its expectation at one fixed parameter, you did not evaluate one fixed parameter. You minimized. The parameter you return depends on the list. Closeness that holds uniformly over the parameters you might have returned is a stronger sentence, and it is not the sentence in the definition. A large n is not a license to erase the hat. What you may say is smaller, and it is exact. You computed an average. You minimized it. Risk is a different average. Its minimizer is a different object until some later argument, which you do not have yet, identifies the two.

Here is another list, so the gap is not tied to a single mean. Take the outcomes 10, negative 2, and 4. The sum is 12 and the mean is 4, the same location you got from 2, 4, and 6. The value of the empirical risk is not the same. Squared errors at 4 are 36, 36, and 0, sum 72, average 24. Earlier the average at the minimizer was 8/3. If you report only that the fitted parameter is 4, you have hidden which list you fit and what training loss you achieved. The formula in this lesson keeps the average, not only the identity of the argmin. Move one unit away, to 5. For squared error around a sample mean, a shift of size d raises the average squared error by d squared, because the residuals around the sample mean sum to zero and the cross term drops. A shift of 1 raises 24 to 25. Check it. Squared errors at 5 are 25, 49, and 1, sum 75, average 25. A shift of 3, to the parameter 7, raises the average by 9, from 24 to 33. Squared errors at 7 are 9, 81, and 9, sum 99, average 33. The algebra and the direct sum agree.

A one-point list shows the fooling in the smallest legal case of the definition. Let the only outcome be 5. Then n is 1, the average is the single squared error, and the minimizer is 5, with empirical risk 0. A training loss of 0 is not a risk of 0. Risk is still an expectation under the scoring distribution. If that distribution is not a point mass at 5, the variance term is positive at every parameter, including at 5. Memorizing the single training point drove the sample average to zero and did nothing, by itself, to that variance. This is the opportunity named at the start, with no extra machinery: the substitution of an average for an expectation can look perfect on the list and still be the wrong object.

What the claim does not mean needs a clean list of its own, said in sentences rather than slogans. It does not mean the sample average was mis-added. The averages above are the definition, carried out. It does not mean you should refuse to minimize training loss. That minimization is the method. It does not mean the training minimizer always misses the scoring minimizer by a fixed distance. You already saw a supposition where the distance was 0 and suppositions where it was 1 or 2. It does not mean squared error is the only loss the definition can host. The definition is one over n times a sum of whatever loss you wrote down. Logistic loss is the other loss this lesson names as convex in the linear predictor. The same hat would sit on an average of logistic losses. This lesson does not give a numeric logistic table, so I will not invent one. The structural warning transfers without that table: minimizing the average is still not the act of minimizing the expectation. It does not mean the surface you will walk in the lab is an empirical risk. That surface is a function of two coordinates and it contains no sample. There is no n in it. Do not narrate it later as a training average. We will use it because a failure on it cannot be blamed on a second basin.

One further worked average, because the factor one over n is where a 2 gets dropped and then repaired in the wrong place. Let the outcomes be 0, 0, and 3. The sum is 3, n is 3, and the sample mean is 1. Empirical risk at 1: squared errors 1, 1, and 4, sum 6, average 2. The residuals in the sign convention parameter minus outcome are 1, 1, and negative 2, and they sum to 0, so the gradient of the average is 0. At the parameter 0, which is a shift of 1, the average rises by 1 squared, from 2 to 3. Directly: squared errors 0, 0, and 9, average 3. At the parameter 1.5, the shift is one half, and the average rises by one quarter, from 2 to 2.25. Directly: each squared error is 2.25, the sum is 6.75, and 6.75 divided by 3 is 2.25. Look at the individual losses. At the minimizer they were 1, 1, and 4. At 1.5 they were equal, and the worst of them was 2.25, which is kinder than a worst loss of 4. Empirical risk did not choose the kinder worst loss. It chose the smaller average. If the quantity you will be scored on is a worst case rather than an expectation, you are not computing the risk written in this lesson, and you should say that before you fit.

There is a useful decomposition you should be able to say without notes. For any list and any parameter, the average squared error equals the average squared error at the sample mean, plus the square of the parameter minus the sample mean. On the list 0, 0, 3, that says the empirical risk equals 2 plus the square of the parameter minus 1. At the parameter 4, the formula gives 2 plus 9, which is 11. Directly, the squared errors are 16, 16, and 1, sum 33, average 11. The decomposition is why changing the parameter and changing an outcome are different edits. Changing the parameter moves you along a parabola whose bottom is fixed by the list. Changing an outcome moves the bottom. Empirical risk minimization, given the list, only does the first edit. It cannot reach into the scoring distribution and move m.

Carry one sentence into the next block. We are about to minimize a particular function by walking downhill one step at a time. The function is not a sample average. The reason this block came first is so you do not narrate the bottom of that surface as if it were automatically the parameter you will be scored on. The walk finds a minimizer of the function you differentiated. Whether the function you wrote down is the risk or the empirical risk is a choice that lives in the loss. If you put a hat on it, the hat stays in the claim.

[[BEAT 1]]
The idea in this block is that convex losses behave, and that on a convex bowl the failure mode of gradient descent is the step size, not a mysterious second basin. Squared error and logistic loss are convex in the linear predictor. A convex function has no spurious local minimum. If the walk on this lab misbehaves, you cut the step. You do not go looking for another valley.

The lab, with the penalty left at zero, is a weighted squared distance to a point. In words, the function of the two coordinates x and y is the square of x minus 1, plus 3 times the square of y plus 0.4. The unique minimizer is the point where both squares vanish, which is x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.4. The value there is 0. If your path is not heading toward that point, the sign on the update is wrong. We still walk, even though the gradient is linear and a single Newton step would finish the problem. The walk is the lesson. The Newton step is a check you should be able to perform, and then you should put it down and return to the slow update.

Write the partial derivatives before any step. The partial with respect to x is 2 times x minus 1. The partial with respect to y is 6 times y plus 0.4. The 6 is the 3 in front of the square, times the 2 that differentiation of a square produces. The curvature in y is three times the curvature in x, before that extra factor of 2 from the square, and after the factor the second derivatives are 2 in x and 6 in y. The y direction is the stiff direction. It is the direction that blows up first when the step gets long.

The update itself is the parameter vector moved by the step size times the gradient, with a minus sign. In words, the next theta is the current theta minus eta times the gradient of f at the current theta. The minus sign is the entire difference between descending and ascending. I will show both, with the same numbers, so the sign is not a slogan.

Start at the origin, x equals 0 and y equals 0, which is a legal point and an easy one to differentiate. The function value is the square of 0 minus 1, plus 3 times the square of 0.4. That is 1 plus 3 times 0.16, which is 1 plus 0.48, which is 1.48. The gradient is 2 times negative 1 in the first coordinate, and 6 times 0.4 in the second, so the gradient is negative 2 and positive 2.4. The squared length of that gradient is 4 plus 5.76, which is 9.76. You will want that number when you check the smoothness bound.

Take a modest step, eta equals 0.1. The x update adds 0.1 times 2, because of the minus sign in front of a negative partial, and x moves from 0 to 0.2. The y update subtracts 0.1 times 2.4, and y moves from 0 to negative 0.24. The new function value is the square of 0.2 minus 1, which is 0.64, plus 3 times the square of negative 0.24 plus 0.4. The inside is 0.16, its square is 0.0256, and 3 times 0.0256 is 0.0768. The function value is 0.7168, down from 1.48. The error in x, measured from 1, went from negative 1 to negative 0.8. The error in y, measured from negative 0.4, went from positive 0.4 to positive 0.16. Each coordinate is multiplying its error by a constant. For x the multiplier is 1 minus 2 eta, which at eta equals 0.1 is 0.8. For y the multiplier is 1 minus 6 eta, which is 0.4. The y error shrank more on this step, because y is stiffer, and the same step size is a larger fraction of the safe range in that direction.

Take the second step from 0.2 and negative 0.24, still with eta equals 0.1. The gradient is 2 times negative 0.8, which is negative 1.6, and 6 times 0.16, which is 0.96. Then x becomes 0.2 plus 0.16, which is 0.36. y becomes negative 0.24 minus 0.096, which is negative 0.336. The x error is now negative 0.64, which is 0.8 times negative 0.8. The y error is 0.064, which is 0.4 times 0.16. Continue in closed form rather than clicking the lab seven times and hoping. After k steps from the origin with eta equals 0.1, the x error is negative 1 times 0.8 to the k, and the y error is 0.4 times 0.4 to the k. At step 5, 0.8 to the fifth is 0.32768, so x is 1 minus 0.32768, which is 0.67232, and 0.4 to the fifth is 0.01024, so the y error is 0.004096 and y is negative 0.395904. The function value is about 0.107. At step 7, x is about 0.790 and y is about negative 0.399, and the function value is about 0.044. The y coordinate is visually done. The x coordinate is still walking. That is the curvature ratio showing up in the path, not a bug.

A smaller step makes the same geometry tedious, which is the remark that too small a step finishes after lunch. Set eta equals 0.05. The x multiplier is 1 minus 0.1, which is 0.9. The y multiplier is 1 minus 0.3, which is 0.7. From the origin, step 1 lands at x equals 0.1 and y equals negative 0.12, and the function value is 1.0452. Step 2 lands at 0.19 and negative 0.204, function value about 0.771. Step 5 lands at about 0.410 and negative 0.333, function value about 0.362. Step 10: the x error is still about negative 0.349, so x is only about 0.651, and the function value is still about 0.122. Step 20: x is about 0.878 and the function value is about 0.015. Step 40: x is about 0.985 and the function value is about 0.00022. Forty steps to get the function under a thousandth, starting from a value of 1.48, with a step that never once threatened to diverge. The path is correct and slow. Correct and slow is a different complaint from wrong.

Now lengthen the step until the y multiplier changes character. The second derivative in y is 6, so the multiplier 1 minus 6 eta has absolute value less than 1 only when eta is strictly less than 2 over 6, which is one third. The second derivative in x is 2, and the corresponding restriction is eta less than 1. The tighter restriction is the y restriction. Smoothness of this function, in the sense of a Lipschitz constant L for the gradient, is the larger curvature, L equals 6. A safe step lies below 2 over L, which is again one third. That is the same bound, read off the general descent rule and off the eigenvalues. Use it as a ceiling you stay under, not as a step you are required to take.

Try eta equals 0.2, which is under the ceiling. The x multiplier is 0.6. The y multiplier is 1 minus 1.2, which is negative 0.2. A negative multiplier means the y error flips sign every step and shrinks. From the origin, step 1 goes to x equals 0.4 and y equals negative 0.48. The y error is negative 0.08, which is negative 0.2 times the old error 0.4. The function value is the square of negative 0.6, plus 3 times the square of negative 0.08, which is 0.36 plus 0.0192, which is 0.3792. Step 2: x error is 0.6 times negative 0.6, so negative 0.36, and x is 0.64. y error is negative 0.2 times negative 0.08, so positive 0.016, and y is negative 0.384. The overshoot in y is already small. By step 5, x is about 0.922 and y is about negative 0.4001, and the function value is about 0.006. The sign flip did not prevent convergence. It is oscillation inside a shrinking envelope. Do not call every sign flip a divergence.

Try eta equals 0.25, still under one third. The x multiplier is 0.5. The y multiplier is negative 0.5. Step 1 from the origin: x equals 0.5, y equals negative 0.6, function value 0.37. Step 2: x equals 0.75, y equals negative 0.3, function value 0.0925. Step 3: x equals 0.875, y equals negative 0.45, function value 0.023125. Step 4: x equals 0.9375, y equals negative 0.375, function value about 0.0058. The y coordinate is bouncing around negative 0.4 by a half each time, and the bounce height is halved each step. The function values 1.48, 0.37, 0.0925, 0.023125, 0.005781 are monotone. You are allowed to overshoot the coordinate and still descend in the function.

The boundary step eta equals one third is the first place the picture stops descending toward the minimizer in y. The y multiplier is 1 minus 2, which is negative 1. The y error flips sign and keeps its magnitude forever. From the origin, y goes to negative 0.8, where the error is negative 0.4. The next step flips that error back to positive 0.4, so y returns to 0. Then it returns to negative 0.8. The y coordinate oscillates between 0 and negative 0.8 and never approaches negative 0.4. Meanwhile x is fine: its multiplier is 1 minus two thirds, which is one third, so the x error is crushed quickly. Step 1 lands at x equals two thirds and y equals negative 0.8, function value 1/9 plus 3 times 0.16, which is about 0.111 plus 0.48, about 0.591. Step 2 lands at x equals eight ninths and y equals 0, function value about 0.012 plus 0.48, about 0.492. Later steps drive x to 1 and leave the function value sitting at 3 times 0.16, which is 0.48, forever, because the y piece never dies. A step that is not strictly below 2 over L can park you in a cycle. The cycle is not a second local minimum of the function. The function still has only one minimizer. The iteration simply refuses to approach it.

Cross the ceiling. Set eta equals 0.4. The y multiplier is 1 minus 2.4, which is negative 1.4, and the absolute value is bigger than 1. The x multiplier is 0.2, still contracting, since 0.4 is well below 1. From the origin, step 1: x equals 0.8, y equals negative 0.96, function value 0.04 plus 3 times the square of negative 0.56. The square is 0.3136, times 3 is 0.9408, and the function value is 0.9808, which is still below 1.48. A single step decreased the function. That one decrease does not certify the step. Step 2: x equals 0.96, y equals 0.384, function value about 1.846, which is already above the start. Step 3: y equals negative 1.498, function value about 3.614. Step 4: function value about 7.084. Step 5: about 13.88. Step 7: about 53.3. The y coordinate is running away, alternating sign, with magnitudes growing by 1.4 each step. The iteration has left every sublevel set. The function did not grow a second basin. The step left the region where the descent inequality protects you.

The descent inequality is worth saying with these numbers, because "below 2 over L" is otherwise a charm. At a point where the gradient has squared length 9.76 and the function equals 1.48, the smoothness bound says the next function value is at most 1.48 minus eta times the quantity 1 minus eta times L over 2, times 9.76. Here L is 6, so L over 2 is 3, and the quantity in the middle is 1 minus 3 eta. At eta equals 0.1, 1 minus 0.3 is 0.7, and 0.1 times 0.7 times 9.76 is 0.6832. The bound is 1.48 minus 0.6832, which is 0.7968. The actual value was 0.7168, which sits under the bound. At eta equals 0.2, 1 minus 0.6 is 0.4, and 0.2 times 0.4 times 9.76 is 0.7808. The bound is 1.48 minus 0.7808, which is 0.6992. The actual value was 0.3792, again under the bound. At eta equals one third, 1 minus 1 is 0, and the bound says the next value is at most 1.48. It promises no decrease. At eta equals 0.4, 1 minus 1.2 is negative 0.2, and the formula no longer promises a decrease. The algebra gives an upper number 1.48 plus 0.7808, which is 2.2608, and an upper number above where you started is not a descent certificate. The actual first step happened to fall, to 0.9808, and the second step rose past the start. The certificate and the accident are different objects. You want the certificate when you choose eta, and you want the trajectory when you check it.

A step of 0.5 separates the two axes cleanly. The x multiplier is 1 minus 1, which is 0. The first update from the origin sets x exactly to 1 and never moves it again. The y multiplier is 1 minus 3, which is negative 2. Step 1 sets y to negative 1.2 and the function value to 1.92, already above 1.48. Step 2 sets y to 1.2 and the function value to 7.68. Step 3 sets y to negative 3.6 and the function value to 30.72. Each step multiplies the y error by negative 2, so the function's y piece, which is 3 times the squared error, multiplies by 4. The values 1.92, 7.68, 30.72, 122.88 are that factor of 4 after the first step. The x coordinate is perfect the whole time. A plot that looks stable in x can still be a divergent iteration. You have to read the stiff coordinate.

The y step is the one that blows up first in a precise sense. Start already optimal in y, at x equals 0 and y equals negative 0.4, and use the dangerous eta equals 0.4. The y partial is 0, so y stays at negative 0.4. The x partial is negative 2. The update sends x to 0.8, then 0.96, then 0.992, with function values 1, then 0.04, then 0.0016, then 0.000064. The same eta that exploded the two-dimensional start is a fast, correct contraction when the stiff error is already zero, because 0.4 is below the x ceiling of 1. The explosion is not a property of the number 0.4 in isolation. It is a property of 0.4 times the curvature you still have left in the path. If you only probe a path that has no y error, you will approve a step the bowl does not allow.

The opposite probe is a path with no x error. Start at x equals 1 and y equals 0, function value 0.48, gradient 0 and 2.4. With eta equals 0.1, y goes to negative 0.24, then negative 0.336, then negative 0.3744, then negative 0.38976. The function values are 0.48, then 0.0768, then 0.012288, then about 0.001966. The multiplier 0.4 on the error is the whole story, and x never moves. With eta equals 0.4 from the same start, y goes to negative 0.96, function value 0.9408, already worse, then to 0.384, function value about 1.844, then to negative 1.498, and onward by factors of negative 1.4. You do not need a bad x coordinate to see the failure. The stiff axis is sufficient.

You might ask why we walk at all if one Newton step finishes a quadratic. Do the Newton step out loud so the question has a real answer underneath it, and then leave it. The second-derivative matrix is diagonal, with 2 in x and 6 in y. Its inverse divides the x partial by 2 and the y partial by 6. The x partial is 2 times x minus 1, so dividing by 2 returns exactly x minus 1. The y partial is 6 times y plus 0.4, so dividing by 6 returns exactly y plus 0.4. Subtracting those from the current point lands on x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.4 in one update, from any start. From the origin, you subtract negative 1 from 0 in the adjustment that the inverse prescribes, and you land at 1, and you subtract 0.4 from 0 in the y adjustment and land at negative 0.4. From the far point x equals 3 and y equals 2, the function value is 4 plus 3 times the square of 2.4. The square of 2.4 is 5.76, times 3 is 17.28, and the function value is 21.28. The gradient is 4 and 14.4. Divide by 2 and by 6, subtract 2 from 3 and subtract 2.4 from 2, and you are at the minimizer with function value 0. Gradient descent with eta equals 0.1 from that same far point takes a first step to x equals 2.6 and y equals 0.56, function value still large, and keeps walking. The Newton update used the curvatures 2 and 6 as divisors. Gradient descent uses one eta for both, which is why the stiffer curvature sets the schedule. We walk because the walk is what you will have when the loss is no longer a known quadratic, and because a failure of the walk, on this bowl, cannot be blamed on anything except the step and the sign.

What the convexity claim does not mean should be said before you trust it outside the bowl. It does not mean every loss you will write down in a later course is convex. It does not mean a step below one third is the right step for a different function. The number one third is 2 divided by 6, and 6 is the y curvature of this particular f. Change the function and the ceiling changes. It does not mean a step below the ceiling is fast. You saw eta equals 0.05 take dozens of steps. It does not mean a decrease on the first iteration certifies the step. Eta equals 0.4 decreased once and then ran away. It does not mean oscillation is divergence. Eta equals 0.25 oscillates in y and converges. It does not mean the gradient formula is optional. If you descend with a plus sign from the origin and eta equals 0.1, x goes from 0 to negative 0.2 and y goes from 0 to positive 0.24, both errors larger than they were, function value the square of negative 1.2 plus 3 times the square of 0.64, which is 1.44 plus 3 times 0.4096, which is 1.44 plus 1.2288, which is 2.6688, up from 1.48. The path is not heading toward 1 and negative 0.4. The sign on the update is wrong. Convexity will not rescue a plus sign.

Leave the penalty at zero for every number in this block. A zero penalty is what keeps the minimizer at 1 and negative 0.4. The next lesson's lesson is what happens when that term is not zero. If you turn it on now, you are debugging a different fixed point and you will misread a correct walk as a failure to reach 1 and negative 0.4.

[[BEAT 2]]
The idea in this last block is that a trading objective with a threshold, a borrow fee, and a position cap is rarely the textbook bowl. You learn the bowl first so you can tell when a later network is no longer covered by the proof. The proof you have, in this lesson, is narrow and useful. Squared error and logistic loss are convex in the linear predictor. On a convex function there is no spurious local minimum. On this particular bowl the gradient is linear, the minimizer is unique, and a step strictly shorter than one third cannot be blamed on a hidden valley if the path fails. A threshold, a fee, or a cap can take you outside that sentence. The algebra does not announce the exit. You have to notice which hypothesis you dropped.

Start from the covered case and keep the numbers visible, so "covered" is a calculation and not a compliment. The function is the square of x minus 1, plus 3 times the square of y plus 0.4. The gradient is 2 times x minus 1, and 6 times y plus 0.4. The update subtracts eta times that gradient. With eta equals 0.2, which sits below one third, the error multipliers are 0.6 in x and negative 0.2 in y. Both have absolute value strictly below 1. From any start, both errors go to zero, and the path goes to 1 and negative 0.4. Take a start you have not used yet, x equals 1.5 and y equals negative 0.2. The function value is the square of 0.5, plus 3 times the square of 0.2, which is 0.25 plus 0.12, which is 0.37. The gradient is 1.0 and 1.2. One step with eta equals 0.2 moves x by minus 0.2, to 1.3, and moves y by minus 0.24, to negative 0.44. The new errors are 0.3 and negative 0.04. The old errors were 0.5 and 0.2. Multiply by 0.6 and by negative 0.2 and you get the same new errors. The function value is the square of 0.3, plus 3 times the square of negative 0.04, which is 0.09 plus 0.0048, which is 0.0948. The step descended. Nothing in that arithmetic asked whether a position was allowed to be larger than a cap, whether a fee was charged for holding it, or whether a threshold zeroed out the loss below some level. The bowl has none of those pieces. That is why the proof covers it.

A threshold is a different function, and this lesson does not give you a numeric trading threshold to pretend is a market fact. What it gives you is the warning that a threshold is how convexity goes away, so build the smallest threshold that makes the warning visible on this same surface. Suppose, as a toy and not as a desk rule, that the loss you actually minimize is the bowl only when the bowl exceeds one half, and is zero otherwise. Call that a threshold at one half. The bowl equals one half along a curve, and inside that curve the thresholded loss is flat. Every point inside is a minimizer of the thresholded loss, because the value is 0 there, and you cannot do better than 0. The unique minimizer of the bowl is still 1 and negative 0.4, where the bowl is 0, which is inside the flat region, since 0 is below one half. But the point x equals 0.5 and y equals negative 0.4 has bowl value 0.25, also below one half, so the thresholded loss is also 0 there. The point x equals 1 and y equals 0 has bowl value 0.48, still below one half, so it is also a minimizer of the thresholded loss. Three different parameters, one thresholded value. Gradient descent has nothing to follow on the interior of that set, because the slope is zero wherever the threshold has flattened the bowl. The failure mode is no longer "your step was longer than one third." The failure mode is that the objective no longer has a single minimizing point. A convex bowl cannot do that. The threshold did it by cutting the bowl off.

Check the boundary of the same toy with arithmetic, so the flat region is not a picture you only imagine. The bowl at the origin is 1.48, which is above one half, so the threshold has not yet flattened it. The bowl at x equals 0.4 and y equals negative 0.4 is the square of negative 0.6, which is 0.36, and the y piece is zero. Since 0.36 is below one half, the thresholded loss there is 0. A path that is descending the bowl toward the origin's x coordinate, with y already optimal, will eventually cross into the flat set and the gradient of the thresholded objective will drop to zero before the path has any reason, inside that new objective, to continue to x equals 1. Where it stops depends on where it entered the set. That dependence on the path is exactly what a convex function with a unique minimizer forbids. You have left the proof.

A borrow fee is a second way out, and again the lesson names it without quoting a market schedule, so the honest numerical version is a fee you write down as part of the objective and then differentiate. Suppose the objective becomes the bowl plus a fee of 0.5 times the absolute value of y, a charge for the size of the y position and not a claim about anyone's borrow market. Absolute value is not differentiable at zero, and it is not the penalty this lab studies. I am using it only to show what a kink does to the stationarity story you just learned. Away from y equals 0 the partial in y of the fee is 0.5 times the sign of y. The bowl's y partial is 6 times y plus 0.4. Set the sum to zero on the negative side, which is where the old minimizer lived. Then 6 times y plus 0.4, minus 0.5, equals zero, so 6 times y plus 0.4 equals 0.5, so y plus 0.4 equals 0.5 over 6, so y equals negative 0.4 plus about 0.0833, so y equals about negative 0.3167. The x equation is untouched, so x stays at 1. The new stationary point is not 1 and negative 0.4. Compute the bowl there, so you can see the original surface got worse. The y error is about 0.0833, and 3 times its square is 3 times about 0.00694, about 0.0208. The bowl rose from 0 to about 0.021 because you added a fee and re-solved. The objective you minimized is not the bowl. Saying the fee "only stabilizes the numerics" would be a way to avoid writing this new point. The point moved.

Change the fee and recompute, one input only. Replace 0.5 by 0.2. On the negative side, 6 times y plus 0.4 equals 0.2, so y plus 0.4 equals 0.2 over 6, about 0.0333, and y is about negative 0.3667. The shift away from negative 0.4 is smaller, about a third of a tenth rather than about eight hundredths. The bowl's extra value is 3 times the square of 0.0333, about 3 times 0.00111, about 0.0033. A smaller fee buys a smaller move. That sentence is ordinary calculus. It is not a proof that the fee lowered the risk you defined in the first block. Risk was an expectation of a loss under a scoring distribution. A fee inside the training objective changes which parameter you return. Whether the new parameter has smaller risk is a second number, computed on the scoring distribution, and this lesson does not hand you that number. Do not report the fee-adjusted bowl and call it risk.

A position cap is the third named exit, and it is a constraint rather than a term you add. Suppose the toy cap says y is not allowed below negative 0.2, while x is free. The unconstrained minimizer has y at negative 0.4, which is outside the cap. The constrained problem cannot go there. On the bowl, for a fixed x, the y piece is 3 times the square of y plus 0.4, which gets smaller as y moves toward negative 0.4. Inside the allowed set, the closest legal y to negative 0.4 is the boundary y equals negative 0.2. So the constrained minimizer is x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.2. The bowl value there is 3 times the square of 0.2, which is 3 times 0.04, which is 0.12. The unconstrained bowl minimum was 0. The cap forced you to pay 0.12 on the original surface. Gradient descent with a projection step makes the same point dynamically. Start at the origin, eta equals 0.1. The unconstrained first step wants y at negative 0.24, which violates the cap, so a projected step places y at negative 0.2 and x at 0.2. The unconstrained recipe would have continued toward negative 0.4. The projected recipe stays on the wall. After that, the y partial of the bowl at y equals negative 0.2 is 6 times 0.2, which is 1.2, still pushing downward, and the projection keeps discarding that push. The x walk continues. With the x multiplier 0.8, the x error from this new point goes from negative 0.8 toward zero, and x approaches 1 while y sits at negative 0.2. You converged. You converged to the constrained minimizer, not to the textbook minimizer. Calling the result "the minimum of the bowl" would be false. The value 0.12 is not 0.

Change the cap and recompute. If the floor on y is negative 0.5 instead of negative 0.2, the unconstrained minimizer y equals negative 0.4 is legal. The cap does not bind. The walk that was covered by the convex-bowl argument is covered again, and the minimizer is back at 1 and negative 0.4 with value 0. The difference between a cap that binds and a cap that does not bind is the difference between a new problem and the old problem. You have to check. A sentence like "we impose reasonable position limits" does not tell you which of those two problems you solved.

You might ask whether convexity survives the cap even though the minimizer moves. A cap of this box type keeps the feasible set convex, and the bowl is convex, so the constrained problem still has no spurious local minimum. The point y equals negative 0.2 at x equals 1 is the only minimizer on that half-plane, not one of several. What you lose is not uniqueness inside the constrained problem. What you lose is the right to quote the unconstrained location and the unconstrained step-size ceiling as if the constraint were not there. The safe step one third was about the iteration on the unconstrained function. A projected iteration is a different map. It can be analyzed, and this lesson does not do that analysis. If you quote "below 2 over L" as a reason your constrained trading walk is safe, you have used a certificate for a map you did not run.

The threshold toy is harsher than the cap, and the comparison is the one to keep. The cap kept a convex problem and moved the answer to the boundary. The threshold built a flat set of minimizers and erased the gradient on a whole region. The fee moved the stationary point by changing the derivative, and it introduced a kink at y equals 0 that the bowl did not have. Three different edits, three different reasons the writeup "we minimize a convex loss by gradient descent" can become a false sentence. The edits were toys, with the constants one half, 0.5, and negative 0.2 chosen so the arithmetic fits on a page. A real threshold, a real borrow fee, and a real position cap are whatever the book actually contains. The lesson's claim is not that those three constants are universal. The claim is that once any of those ingredients is in the objective, you re-derive the minimizer and you re-check the hypotheses. You do not inherit them from the bowl.

What the claim does not mean is as important as the claim, because this block is easy to overread. It does not mean finance cannot use a convex loss. If your actual objective is squared error or logistic loss in a linear predictor, with no threshold, no fee, and no cap, the convexity statement still applies, and the failure mode of gradient descent is still the step. It does not mean every nonconvex objective is a disaster you can see in the first step. It means the proof no longer covers you, so a bad step and a bad landscape can look the same from the outside. On the bowl, those were separable: the landscape has one minimizer, so a path that ran to infinity was the step. On a thresholded surface, a path that stops early may be the landscape. It does not mean the lab is the place to demonstrate a nonconvex failure. The lab is a convex bowl on purpose, with the penalty at zero, so that if the path leaves, you cut the step. It does not mean the first block's warning has been canceled. Even when the walk reaches 1 and negative 0.4, that point minimizes the function you differentiated. If that function was an empirical risk rather than the risk, you are back to the hat. Convexity does not delete the hat. A unique minimizer of the training average is still not, by uniqueness, the minimizer of the expectation.

Put the two warnings in one place and stop. The training average is not the risk. The walk on this bowl reaches 1 and negative 0.4 when the step is strictly shorter than one third and the sign is a minus, and it can leave every sublevel set when the step is longer, without the bowl growing a second valley. A threshold, a borrow fee, or a position cap changes the object being minimized. After that change you write the new stationarity condition, or the new constraint, and you say which certificate you still have. If you cannot say it, you are outside the proof, and the honest lecture note is that you are outside the proof.

[[BEAT 0]]

Name the order of the room before you bring a single figure. Protocol, then the correctness test, then the budget, then any timing you actually have. A chart of invented nanoseconds ends the conversation. A failed checksum you found yourself starts a better one. The room is not there to be impressed by a unit. It is there to see whether you can show a handoff, show that you would notice if the handoff were wrong, and show that you know which path the number, if any, belongs to.

Start with the protocol, on one cell, because a defense that opens with a timer has nothing for the timer to be about. Draw the cell. Write the ticket. Write the mask. For a capacity of four, slot zero begins at sequence zero. A producer whose monotonic head is zero may write it, and then publishes one. A consumer whose tail is zero may read when the sequence is tail plus one, and then publishes tail plus capacity, which is four. The next producer publish on that slot, when the head has reached four, is five. Five is not a reset to zero. If you cannot draw that lap, you are not ready for a graph. The mask is the part of the drawing that folds head four back onto slot zero while the monotonic head stays four. Say that the slot wrapped and the counter did not. Then show full and empty at the same index. At the start, head and tail both mask to slot zero, and the consumer's difference is negative, so the ring is empty. After four pushes, head four and tail zero both mask to slot zero, and the producer's difference is negative, so the ring is full. The index did not tell you which. The ticket did. Write release and acquire on the arrows. The producer releases the published ticket after the write. The consumer acquires it before the read. If those words are not on the board, the board is a picture of bytes without an order, and bytes without an order are how a tear sneaks into the next part of the defense.

The correctness test comes second, and it is the checksum, not a feeling that the run looked smooth. The default main pushes order identifiers zero through N minus one. The consumer sums them. The sum is N times the quantity N minus one, divided by two. For N equal to four, the sum is six. For N equal to one hundred thousand, the sum is four billion nine hundred ninety-nine million nine hundred fifty thousand. You compute that by dividing one hundred thousand by two, getting fifty thousand, and multiplying by ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine, which is five billion minus fifty thousand. If the program prints that number, you have a passing equality on that run. If it prints anything else, the equality failed. A failed checksum you found yourself is a better opening than a polished latency chart, because it means you ran the test and you believed it. You say what you expected, what you saw, and what you changed. You do not bury the failure under a later green run and hope nobody asks. The room can tell the difference between a person who understands the sum and a person who has a screenshot of a sum.

The budget comes third, still before any clock. Either the path is parse, decide, and send, with a tiny decide, or you report the ring in isolation. Write that sentence where the room will see it first. If a model sits on the path, the ring's nanoseconds are a rounding error next to the model, and quoting them as the path is how a defense hides the model inside a number. A huge strategy mean means the nanosecond target is about a different path. You say which path you are defending. The teaching numbers in the budget lab are teaching numbers. They are not a measurement you may promote because the figure was convenient.

Only then do you show a timing, and only a timing you actually have. Compiler, flags, CPU, pin. Capacity, event size, cold or warm. A percentile, not only the mean, once you are on metal. The default flags you can name without a machine are optimization level three, the C plus plus twenty standard, and pthread, and the benchmark build adds the define AXIOM WITH BENCHMARK and links the benchmark library. Those flags are not a latency. A browser number is labeled as a browser number, with the two sentences from the measurement hour: the rate is real for the JavaScript loop, and it is irrelevant to the C plus plus target. Nanoseconds come from a counter on the machine, or not at all. A chart of a number you did not measure ends the conversation because the room can no longer tell which of your other sentences were measured. One invented unit contaminates the checksum story, even if the checksum was real. Leave the chart out. The protocol and the sum can carry the defense.

The question to answer is whether a failed checksum you already fixed should stay in the talk, or whether showing it looks like you are unprepared. Keep it, briefly, if you found it yourself and you can say what was wrong. That sequence is evidence that the test can fail and that you know what failure looks like. A defense that only shows the final green run does not teach the room the expected sum, the actual wrong sum, or the edit. You do not need a long apology. You need the arithmetic. Suppose the consumer dropped identifier forty-two out of a short run and the sum was short by forty-two. You say so. Then you say the equality is N times N minus one, over two, and you show the run that meets it. Hiding the forty-two makes the green run look like a ritual. Showing it makes the green run look like a test.

What the claim does not mean is that timing is illegitimate, or that a missing nanosecond is a missing capstone. Timing you actually have belongs in the room, after the protocol, the checksum, and the budget, with the CPU, the flags, and the pin attached. Timing you do not have belongs nowhere. The order is the claim. Reversing it, so that a hypothetical two hundred fifty nanoseconds comes first and the ticket comes last, is how invented numbers end the conversation. The claim also does not mean that a passing checksum on one hundred thousand events is a latency result. The sum can pass on a slow ring. The sum is about identifiers, not about the clock. You may be pleased that it passed. You may not read the pleasure as a nanosecond.

[[BEAT 1]]

The threats are next, and they are not synonyms. A dropped event and a torn event are different. The ring can drop by returning false when full. It should not tear a trivially copyable payload if the release and the acquire are in the right places. A double price torn in half is what the trivial-copy rule is trying to prevent. Integer ticks are still safer.

A drop is a refusal you can name. The producer loads the head, acquires the cell sequence, and finds a negative difference. The push returns false. The monotonic head does not claim. The payload is not written. The event remains in the caller's hands, or it is discarded by a caller who chose not to retry. Either way, the ring does not contain a half-written version of it. On the capacity-four walk, the fifth push is a drop in exactly this sense if the caller gives up. The first four events are intact. If those four carried identifiers zero through three, their sum is six, and a consumer who pops them all will see six. The missing fifth identifier is absent, not corrupted. You can explain the absence by pointing at the false return. A benchmark that spins on that false return is a measurement bug you have already named. A defense that calls the false return a tear is a confusion you should not introduce. False means the event stayed out.

A tear is a value that was never stored as the value you read. The event is trivially copyable, so the intended transfer is a copy of its bytes, not a custom constructor running halfway through the consumer's read. The release and the acquire are what keep that copy on one side of a line. The producer writes the payload, then releases the ticket. The consumer acquires the ticket, then reads the payload. If those orders are present, the consumer does not observe a mixture of an old payload and a new one. If they are absent, the ticket can become visible while the bytes are still in flight, and the consumer can assemble a value that neither the old write nor the new write produced. That assembled value is the tear. It can pass a casual log and fail a checksum, or, worse, it can look locally plausible and still be a price nobody sent.

Take the double seriously, because the lecture names it. A double price torn in half is the picture the trivial-copy rule is trying to prevent. You do not need a mythical bit pattern to see the shape of the harm. A price stored as a double occupies more than one byte. A reader who is not ordered against the writer can observe some bytes from the previous price and some bytes from the next price. The resulting bit pattern is not a rounding of either price. It is a third number, and it can be wildly far from both, because the bytes of a floating-point encoding are not a little-endian counter you may split with a straight face. Suppose the sender wrote a price of one hundred and then a price of one hundred one, and the receiver's torn read is not equal to either. The checksum on order identifiers might still pass if the identifier bytes happened to be intact and only the price was torn, or it might fail if the tear hit the identifier. The lecture's point is not that the sum catches every tear in every field. The point is that a tear is a different disease from a drop, and that a double is a convenient way to suffer it. The trivial-copy rule plus the release and the acquire are the prevention. They are not a suggestion that a double is a good price representation.

Integer ticks are still safer. Say that as an engineering preference you can defend without a new theory. An integer tick is a smaller and duller object. A price of one hundred dollars and two cents, at a one-cent tick, is the integer ten thousand two, if you froze that scale. You can add and compare ticks without wondering whether a torn mantissa produced a quiet NaN or a huge magnitude. The ring should still not tear the integer. Release and acquire are doing that work. The integer is safer because the surrounding code is less likely to treat a torn or a special floating value as a real price that merely looks odd. Safer is not the same word as proven. You still walk the ticket. You still run the sum. If the identifier is the integer you sum, the equality checks the identifier, and a separate check has to carry the price if the price is the thing you are afraid of tearing. Do not describe the identifier sum as a complete inspection of a double you placed beside it.

Put both threats on the same short run so the contrast is numerical. Identifiers zero, one, two, and three are pushed into a capacity of four. The honest sum is six. Case one: the fifth identifier, four, finds a full ring and the caller drops it. The consumer sums six. The drop is visible because four never arrives, and it is visible as an absence. Case two: all four pushes succeed, but a missing release lets the consumer read identifier two's slot while the bytes are half updated, and the consumer adds fifteen instead of two. The sum is zero plus one plus fifteen plus three, which is nineteen, and nineteen is not six. The equality fails. You do not yet know from the number nineteen alone that the cause was a tear rather than a drop of two and an insertion of fifteen. You know the equality failed. You distinguish the threats by the mechanism. A drop has a false return and an untouched cell. A tear has a successful-looking transfer of a value that was not written as a whole. The defense has to be able to say those two sentences. A single word, "corruption," is how they get blurred back together.

The question to answer is whether a drop is simply a tear you decided not to admit. It is not. The ring is allowed to drop by returning false when full. That behavior is part of the protocol you drew on the board. A producer that respects false does not write, so there is nothing to tear. A tear happens when a write and a read overlap badly enough to expose a mixed value, which is what the release and the acquire are placed to forbid, given a trivially copyable payload. You can have a drop with no tear. You can have a tear with no drop, in a ring that never returned false, if the orders are wrong. You can have both, on different events, in a bad run. The checksum fails the equality in the cases where the bytes that arrived are not the identifiers that were sent. It does not label the cause. You label the cause by pointing at the false return or at the missing release. If you cannot point, say that you cannot point. Do not pick the more dramatic word.

What the claim does not mean is that integer ticks make the memory orders optional, or that a trivially copyable double is forbidden. The double is the example of what the rule is trying to prevent when the orders are wrong. With the orders right, the copy is the copy. Integer ticks remain safer as a representation of price in the rest of the system, because the rest of the system also makes mistakes, and a torn or special floating value is a worse neighbor than an integer. The claim also does not mean that every failed checksum is a tear. A drop fails a sum that expected an identifier the ring refused. A bug in the test harness fails sums too. Mechanism first, adjective second. The room is listening for whether you can keep drop and tear apart while you look at one equality.

[[BEAT 2]]

The last idea is a stop. You do not need a matching engine. You need a handoff you can explain on a whiteboard, a test that fails when the handoff is wrong, and a budget that does not hide a model inside a nanosecond. That is the whole practicum. Scope is how it stays finishable. Adding a venue does not make the handoff more serious. It makes the defense about something you did not build.

The non-claims belong on the board next to the claims, because a room will fill any silence with a stronger story than you told. This is not a degree. This is not an exchange. This is not two hundred fifty nanoseconds unless you measured them. Three negatives, each aimed at a different exaggeration. The degree exaggeration treats a practicum defense as a credential you did not earn by giving this talk. The exchange exaggeration treats a single-producer, single-consumer ring as a marketplace. The nanosecond exaggeration treats an ambition, reasonable on pinned metal with a warm cache, as a result. You say the negatives out loud so you do not have to discover them in the questions.

The whiteboard you already started is the first of the three things you do need. One cell, the ticket, the mask. Full and empty at the same index. Release and acquire on the arrows. If a question comes, you walk capacity four again. Push four events, watch the fifth return false, pop one, push one, and read slot zero. The ticket advanced by the capacity. It is five after that extra push, not zero. Then you match a line of the C plus plus to a step you just did. A defense that can do that walk under interruption is a defense of a handoff. A defense that gestures at a pipeline diagram and says the ring is "lock free, so it is fast" has not yet said when the producer is allowed to write.

The test is the second thing, and it has to be able to fail. The equality is the sum of identifiers zero through N minus one, equal to N times N minus one, divided by two. For one hundred thousand events, four billion nine hundred ninety-nine million nine hundred fifty thousand. If you change the listing and drop an identifier, the equality fails. If you tear a payload the sum covers, the equality fails. A test that cannot fail is a slogan. The lab that stores the defense boxes stores them on this machine only. Checking a box is a prompt, not a signature. You check a box only when you can say the sentence out loud. If a box asks for a measurement you do not have, you leave it open and you say you do not have it. An open box you can explain is consistent with the order of the room. A checked box you cannot explain is an invented result with a friendlier shape.

The budget is the third thing, and it is where models try to hide. If the decide is not tiny, you report the ring in isolation, or you report the decide as its own number if you measured it. You do not add an unmeasured model to a hypothesized two hundred fifty nanoseconds and call the sum a trading latency. You do not subtract the model and keep the two hundred fifty unless a counter on a named CPU produced the two hundred fifty. Leave the nanosecond box open until a machine, not this page, has earned it. The page can teach the protocol. It cannot be the counter. A machine earns the box with a binary, flags, a pin, and a checksum that already matched. Until those exist, the open box is the correct state of the defense, and you should be able to say why it is open without apologizing for the protocol. The protocol was never the same object as the clock.

The question that tries to reopen the scope is whether a small matching step would make the story more complete, as long as it stays simple. It would make the story a different story. A match decides which resting interest trades against which incoming interest. The ring does not rest interest. It hands an event from one producer to one consumer, with a fixed capacity, a trivially copyable payload, and no allocation after startup. A simple match is still a match. It has a book, a priority, and a trade, or it is not a match. You do not need it. If you build it, you owe a new test, a new budget, and a new set of non-claims, and you will have spent the defense explaining an exchange you were told not to build. Stop. The handoff is enough when you can draw it, break it, and refuse to hide a model inside it.

What the claim does not mean is that the work is unfinished because the nanosecond box is open, or that an open box is a theatrical gesture. Unfinished would be a ticket you cannot walk, a sum you cannot compute, or a budget sentence you will not choose. An open measurement box is finished honesty about a measurement. The claim also does not mean that two hundred fifty nanoseconds is a foolish ambition. It remains a reasonable ambition on pinned metal with a warm cache, and more than one million events a second remains a different, looser arithmetic target, one thousand nanoseconds per event if a million events share a second evenly. Ambition is not possession. You may want the number. You may not draw it. The conversation ends when the drawing pretends to be the counter, and it continues when you can leave the box open and still teach the cell.

When you give the defense, the order is the same as the hour. The ticket, including a same index that can be full or empty. The sum, including the number four billion nine hundred ninety-nine million nine hundred fifty thousand, or the smaller sum on the run you actually did. The budget sentence, isolation or a tiny decide, chosen on purpose. Then the clock, or the open box. Not a degree, not an exchange, and not a nanosecond you did not measure. If you can say that without a chart you invented, you have defended the engine you built.

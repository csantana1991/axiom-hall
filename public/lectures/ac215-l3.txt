[[BEAT 0]]
The idea in this opening block is that a log is the source of truth or it is not. Kafka-style logs give you a replayable sequence. They do not give you a nanosecond. Use a log for recovery and audit. Use shared memory or a ring for the hot handoff. Pretending one mechanism does both jobs is how budgets die.

Two transports, and only two, are on the slide. A durable log replays and audits, and it is slower. An in-process ring is the hot path, and it is not a system of record. Write the handoff down. Memory is not an audit. The ring can move a market event from a producer to a consumer without becoming the place you go, next week, to prove what was sent. The log can prove what was sent, in the order the log recorded, without being the place you go for the next event on a nanosecond path. Each mechanism has the job the lecture gave it. The failure mode is the sentence that says one of them does both.

A replayable sequence means you can read the events again, in order, and rebuild a state the log is willing to stand behind. That is recovery and audit. It is not a latency. The lecture's limit is blunt: the log does not give you a nanosecond. If your budget for the hot handoff assumes the durable log delivers the next event on that clock, you have assigned the log a job it was not given, and the budget dies in the way the opening sentence describes. Shared memory or a ring is the hot handoff. The capstone ring moves a market event. It is in-process. It is not, by that fact, an audit trail. If the process restarts and the memory is gone, the ring is gone. The log, if you kept one, is what remains. If you kept neither, you do not have a source of truth. You have a machine that traded and then forgot.

The budget arithmetic from the pipeline still applies, and it should be attached to the transport you actually wait on. Suppose the hot handoff is the ring, and the log write is cold, off the path. The hot sum does not include the log. The mean of the hot sum is the sum of the hot stages only. Suppose instead you wait for the log append before you send. Then the log is a stage that blocks, and its mean and its spread enter the sum. You do not get the replay property for free inside the nanosecond claim. You paid a stage for it. The lecture's recommendation is to pay that stage off the hot path: log for recovery and audit, ring for the handoff. The recommendation is not a claim that the log's latency is zero. It is a claim that the log's latency should not be inside the handoff budget, because the log is not the handoff.

Work the five stage names the lab brief asks you to write, the ones the capstone note already listed, and mark which transport each one uses. Gateway read. Ring push. Strategy decision. Ring pop. Gateway write. The ring push and the ring pop are the in-process ring. They are the hot handoff between the read and the decision, and between the decision and the write. They are not a durable log. If you replace the ring push with a Kafka-style append and you wait for it, you have changed the transport and you have changed the budget. The name "push" surviving in a diagram does not keep the latency. The mechanism under the name is the mechanism you have to budget. Write the five names you would actually defend, even if you leave the lab's defaults in the model. The defaults are invented teaching values. The names are the contract. A slider is not a slogan. It is a stage with a spread, or it is a teaching number you must replace before you defend it.

Change one input. Keep the five names and change only the push, from the ring to a durable append you wait on. The sum of the means gains the difference between the append's mean and the ring push's mean. This lecture does not give you those two means, so you do not invent milliseconds and subtract them. You write the sign. Waiting on a durable append, where you previously waited on an in-process ring, adds a stage the lecture classified as slower. The hot budget gets worse by that difference, or you stop waiting and the append is no longer on the hot path. There is no third option in which the append stays on the path and costs the ring's time. That third option is pretending one mechanism does both jobs. The budget dies there. You either pay the slower mean or you take the log off the handoff.

You might ask whether a log can be fast enough, on some hardware, that the distinction collapses. The lecture does not give you a measurement that collapses it, and it states the jobs as different. A fast log is still the recovery and audit mechanism, and the ring is still the hot handoff, unless you have consciously chosen to wait on the log and put that wait in the budget. Speed does not rewrite the source of truth. If the log is fast and you still do not treat it as the record, you have a fast unused record. If you treat it as the record and you do not wait for it, you have a record that can lag the handoff, and the lag is a fact about recovery, not a nanosecond. Say which clock you are on. The log's clock is replay order. The ring's clock is the handoff. They can both be present. They are not the same term in the sum.

What the claim does not mean is that Kafka-style logs are a mistake, or that rings are a system of record if you want them hard enough. The log is the right tool for replay and audit. The ring is the right tool for the hot handoff. It does not mean memory becomes an audit if you pin a thread. Memory is not an audit. It does not mean the five names are a latency measurement. They are the list you must be willing to defend, with spreads you replace over the teaching defaults. It does not mean a gateway write is a log. The gateway write is a stage in the hot list. A durable copy of what you wrote is a different stage, cold unless you waited. It does not mean the nanosecond the log fails to give you is a number you may attach to the ring without saying so. If you claim a nanosecond, the earlier note told you to say it is about the ring alone whenever a heavy stage shares the path. A log you wait on is a heavy stage in exactly that sense. Leave it off the handoff or put it in the sum. Do not leave it off the page and on the path.

[[BEAT 1]]
The idea in this block is that schemas are risk controls. A market event with a double price and an implicit tick size will eventually compare unequal to itself. The capstone struct is explicit. The teaching file still has a double in one field, and the note tells you what to change before production. Add a schema version when the struct changes, or replays will lie.

Read the fields the note names. Timestamp, in nanoseconds. Order id. Price. Quantity. Side. Five fields. That list is the contract the teaching file is willing to write down. Side is a char, in the lecture's description of the explicit struct. Sizes should fit. The layout is fixed, and the padding is written down. The struct is trivially copyable, or the ring's store is a data race. The trivial-copy assertion is the schema check the compiler can do. A compiler check is not a prose comment. It fails the build when the type stops being a plain record you can copy as bytes. That is the risk control you get without a review meeting. You still version the layout when you change it, because a compiler on the new code does not police an old replay.

Price is the field the note singles out. Price is a double in the teaching file so the benchmark matches the earlier listing. A double price, with an implicit tick, will eventually compare unequal to itself. That is the same identity failure the book lecture named for ticks, and here it is a schema failure. If you promote the struct to production, change price to an integer tick and update the checksum test. The note does not give you the new checksum value. You update the test because the bytes changed, not because you remember a magic number. Integer tick means equality is integer equality. Two events at the same tick compare equal on price. The teaching double is allowed only inside the teaching file, for the reason the note gives: the benchmark matches the earlier listing. It is not a permission slip for production.

A schema version is what keeps a replay from lying when those bytes change. Suppose the old layout and the new layout differ by the price representation, double versus integer tick. A replay that reads old bytes with the new struct will interpret the old price bits as an integer, or it will read off the end of a field into the next field. The timestamp, the order id, the quantity, and the side can all shift relative to the truth. The log will happily return those bytes. The lie is in the interpretation. A version on the layout tells the reader which interpretation is legal. Without it, every replay after the change is a candidate lie. The lecture's rule is to add the version when the struct changes. It does not give you a particular version number to worship. It gives you the obligation to change the number, or the tag, when the layout changes, and to refuse to read a mismatch as if it matched.

The ring cares about the trivial copy, and the log cares about the version. Those are the two transports again, with the schema as the shared contract. The ring stores bytes in a slot. If the struct is not trivially copyable, that store is a data race, not a clever serializer. The log stores bytes for later. If the version is missing, the later reader guesses. Padding that is not written down is a guess about where the next field starts. Write the padding down. Fixed layout means the offsets are part of the contract, not an accident of a compiler flag you do not record. When you change a flag and the padding moves, you have changed the layout, and the version rule applies. You do not get to call it the same struct because the field names match.

Change one input: the price representation, from the teaching double to an integer tick, which is the promotion the note describes. The list of fields stays five. The meaning of the price field changes. Equality changes. The checksum test changes. The version changes. What does not change is the set of names: timestamp in nanoseconds, order id, price, quantity, side. A reader who versions only when a name is added will miss this change, and the replay will lie on a same-named field. Version the layout, not the nickname. The nickname "price" survived. The bytes did not. That is the input that matters.

You might ask whether a double is safe if you round it to a tick before you compare. Rounding at the door is a way to build an integer, and the integer is what you should have stored. If the stored form is still a double, a later reader can forget to round, and the unequal-to-itself failure returns. The note does not say "round at compare time" as the production fix. It says change price to an integer tick and update the checksum test. Store the identity you intend to compare. A side stored as a char is the same kind of choice: an explicit small set of values, not an implicit string with invisible variants. Sizes that fit are the same kind of choice. The struct is a risk control because it makes the illegal representations unrepresentable, or at least visible at the boundary where you convert.

What the claim does not mean is that the teaching file is already the production schema. The note distinguishes them. The double is in the teaching file for the benchmark. Production changes it. It does not mean a checksum you do not update will still pass and somehow be right. You update the test because the bytes changed. A passing checksum on the old layout, applied by habit to the new layout, is the replay lie in test form. It does not mean trivial copy is a performance hint. If the struct is not trivially copyable, the ring's store is a data race. That is a correctness condition. It does not mean five fields are enough for every venue. It means these five are the ones the note names. Adding a field changes the layout, so you add a version. It does not mean an implicit tick size can stay implicit once the price is an integer. The integer is the tick count. The tick size is the contract that says what that count means in price space, and it has to be explicit somewhere you version. The lecture's failure mode is an implicit tick next to a double. Moving to an integer without saying what the integer counts is a smaller version of the same hole. Write it down. Version it when it changes.

Work one equality, so the double failure is a comparison and not a slogan. Two teaching events intend the same tick. Their prices are doubles. The lecture says a double price will eventually compare unequal to itself. When that happens, a check that should deduplicate, or a join that should see one price, sees two prices. The quantity and the side can match exactly and the events still fail the price test. After you change price to an integer tick, the same intention becomes two equal integers, and the comparison holds. The timestamp in nanoseconds and the order id are still the other identity fields. They do not repair a price compare that is wrong. A version field, if you added one, matches too, or the reader should have refused the record before the compare. The schema works when unequal things are unequal and equal ticks are equal. The teaching double does not guarantee the second half. The integer tick does, for the tick you defined.

[[BEAT 2]]
The idea in this block is idempotence. A consumer that retries must not double-apply a fill. Sequence numbers are not only a concurrency trick. They are how you know you already saw this event. The ring's ticket and the exchange's sequence are different numbers. Do not overload them.

At-least-once delivery double-applies naive handlers. Read that as arithmetic. A fill arrives. Your handler applies it. The delivery happens again, because at-least-once includes the retry. A naive handler applies it again. If the fill quantity is q, the position moves by q the first time and by q the second time. The position has moved by two q. The true fill was one q. The extra q is the double application. Nothing in the market printed a second fill. The transport printed a second delivery of the same fill. Deduplicate with an id you do not invent twice. The exchange's sequence is that kind of id, if it is stable for the event. The second delivery carries the same sequence as the first. You have seen it. You do not apply it again. The position stays at plus q, not plus two q.

The ring's ticket is a different number. It is how the ring knows about slots and publication in its own protocol. It is not the exchange's sequence. If you use the ticket as the deduplication id, you have overloaded the two numbers. A replay that goes through the ring again can receive a different ticket for the same exchange sequence, or a ticket can mean something that is legal for the ring and meaningless for the fill. The lecture does not ask you to invent that second story in detail. It asks you not to overload them. Deduplicate fills on the exchange sequence, or on an id you assigned once to the fill and stored with it. Do not deduplicate fills on a ring ticket you treat as if it were that id. The ring does not deduplicate. The consumer might have to. Those are different layers. The ring's job is the hot handoff. The consumer's job, under at-least-once delivery from a log or a gateway, is to apply a fill once.

Change one input: the retry count, from one extra delivery to two extra deliveries. The naive handler applies the fill three times in total, the original plus two retries. The position moves by three q. The deduplicating handler applies it once and ignores the two retries, because the sequence matched a sequence already applied. The gap between the handlers is two q on this changed input, and it was one q when there was only one retry. The bug scales with the number of extra deliveries. It does not scale with anything the market did. A quiet market and a single fill are enough to book three q if the transport retries twice and the handler is naive. Idempotence is the property that those retries do not change the position. It is not a property of the ring. The ring does not deduplicate. Putting the retry logic in the ring by overloading the ticket is the mistake the lecture names.

You might ask whether exactly-once delivery removes the need for the check. The lecture does not give you exactly-once as a property of these transports. It gives you at-least-once as the pattern that double-applies naive handlers, and it gives you a consumer that might have to deduplicate. If some other transport promised exactly-once, you would still keep the exchange sequence and the ring ticket distinct, because that distinction is about identity, not about a delivery slogan. A promise you did not receive is not a reason to apply q twice. The handler you can defend treats a repeated sequence as a repeat. The handler you cannot defend treats every arrival as new.

What the claim does not mean is that every duplicate byte is a duplicate fill. Deduplicate with an id. Two different fills can have the same quantity and the same side and still be two fills, if their exchange sequences differ. Collapsing them because q matches is the opposite bug: you drop a real fill. The id you do not invent twice is what separates those cases. It does not mean the ring ticket is useless. It means it is not the exchange sequence. Use it for the ring's own protocol. Do not use it as the fill's identity. It does not mean a consumer must deduplicate if the delivery is not repeated. It means a consumer that retries, or that sits behind at-least-once delivery, must not double-apply. It does not mean sequence numbers are only a concurrency trick. The lecture says they are also how you know you already saw this event. Concurrency can be part of the reason a ring has a ticket. Deduplication of fills is a reason the exchange sequence exists. Different reasons, different numbers.

Put the schema and the idempotence check in one place, because a version mismatch can look like a new event. An old record and a new record might carry the same exchange sequence and different price layouts. If you ignore the version, you may apply a lie, or you may treat the decoded lie as a second fill. The version check comes first. If the version does not match a layout you know, you do not apply the fill and you do not invent a second id. If the version matches and the sequence is one you have applied, you do not apply it again. If the version matches and the sequence is new, you apply q once. Three branches. The naive handler has one branch, apply whatever arrived, and that branch books two q or three q under retries. The lecture's handler has the three. The ring still does not do this for you. The consumer might have to.

Write the five stage names one last time, as the brief asks, with the contract attached rather than a slogan. Gateway read, with a layout you version. Ring push, in-process, not an audit, ticket not overloaded with the exchange sequence. Strategy decision, off the nanosecond claim if it is a net, and not a place that double-applies a fill. Ring pop, same ring, same warning. Gateway write, a stage that blocks only on the work you included, with retries that consult the exchange sequence before they move a position by q again. That list is the one you can defend. The lab defaults behind the sliders remain invented teaching values. Leave them in the model if you must, and do not defend them. Defend the names, the transport under each name, the schema version, and the id that keeps two deliveries of one fill from becoming two q.

One numerical identity check keeps the exchange sequence and the ring ticket from collapsing in a worked retry. Suppose the exchange sequence on the fill is one thousand seven, and the quantity q is five. The first delivery applies five. The position is plus five. A retry arrives with the same exchange sequence, one thousand seven, and the ring, because the event was pushed again, hands the consumer a different ticket, say ticket forty-four the first time and ticket forty-five the second time. Those ticket numbers are a teaching pair, not a trace. They exist so the overload is visible. A handler that deduplicates on the exchange sequence sees one thousand seven twice and applies five once. A handler that deduplicates on the ticket sees forty-four and then forty-five, treats them as two ids, and applies five twice. The position is plus ten. The market printed five. The extra five is the overloaded id, not a second fill. Change one input: let the retry keep the same ticket as well as the same sequence, both one thousand seven and both forty-four. Then even the overloaded handler happens to deduplicate. The bug hides. A single lucky reuse of a ticket is not a proof that the ticket is the exchange sequence. The lecture's rule is the rule that still works when the tickets differ. Do not overload them. The consumer that keys on one thousand seven books five. The consumer that keys on the ticket books ten whenever the ring assigns a fresh ticket to a redelivery. The ring does not deduplicate. The consumer might have to, and the id it uses is the one the exchange, or the fill itself, does not invent twice.

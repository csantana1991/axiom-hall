[[BEAT 0]]
The board in front of you is titled two moments, on purpose. Markowitz keeps the mean and the variance and throws the rest of the distribution away. That is a modeling choice. It is exact for elliptical returns and a rough map otherwise. Fat tails do not invalidate the algebra. They limit what you may say out loud. You may compute a mean, a variance, and a covariance matrix with a clear conscience. You may not say, on the strength of that matrix alone, that you have described the investor, the crash, or the shape of the left tail.

A portfolio on this board is a vector of weights. The portfolio mean, mu sub p, equals w transpose mu. Return is linear in the weights. If you blend two assets, the expected return of the blend is the same blend of the expected returns. Nothing about variance has entered that sentence, and nothing about it is optional. If asset 1 has mean 0.06 and asset 2 has mean 0.12, and you put weight 0.25 on the first, the portfolio mean is 0.25 times 0.06 plus 0.75 times 0.12. That is 0.015 plus 0.090, which is 0.105. Change the weight to 0.90 and the mean becomes 0.90 times 0.06 plus 0.10 times 0.12, which is 0.054 plus 0.012, which is 0.066. The map from weight to mean is a straight line. If your picture in mean-standard-deviation space bends, the bend is coming from risk, not from expected return.

Variance is quadratic. The portfolio variance, sigma sub p squared, equals w transpose capital sigma w. Capital sigma is the covariance matrix. It must be positive semidefinite, or the picture is nonsense. Positive semidefinite means that every weight vector, including the ones with shorts, produces a variance that is at least zero. A variance is a square. If your matrix can hand a negative number to some w, you do not have a risk picture. You have a typing error or an impossible dependence.

Two assets make the quadratic something you can expand by hand, which is the habit the next board will demand. For now, notice the cross term. The diagonal of capital sigma holds the two variances. The off-diagonal holds the covariance, which you can also write as correlation times the product of the two standard deviations. When the correlation is zero, the cross term drops out and the variance of a portfolio is the sum of the weighted variances. It is not the sum of the standard deviations. Take standard deviations 0.10 and 0.30, correlation 0, and an even split, weight one half. Variance equals one half squared times 0.01, plus one half squared times 0.09. That is 0.25 times 0.01 plus 0.25 times 0.09, which is 0.0025 plus 0.0225, which is 0.025. The standard deviation is the square root of 0.025, about 0.158. The average of the two standard deviations would have been 0.20. The portfolio risk is smaller than that average because variance, not volatility, is what adds when correlation is zero, and because the square-root function then pulls the result down. If you had averaged the volatilities and called it diversification, you would have skipped the only algebra this model contains.

Try a matrix that is not positive semidefinite, so the warning is numeric. Suppose someone types a covariance of 0.036 between two assets whose variances are 0.01 and 0.09. The implied correlation would be 0.036 divided by the product of 0.10 and 0.30, which is 0.036 divided by 0.03, which is 1.2. A correlation of 1.2 is not a correlation. The determinant of the matrix is 0.01 times 0.09 minus 0.036 squared, which is 0.0009 minus 0.001296, which is negative 0.000396. A two-by-two symmetric matrix with a negative determinant has eigenvalues of opposite sign, so one of them is negative. Some weight vector has negative "variance." The frontier picture built from that matrix is nonsense in the precise sense the slide uses. You do not interpret the hyperbola. You repair the matrix.

Linearity is worth a third blend, because people remember one arithmetic example and then smuggle a curve into the mean. Keep the means at 0.06 and 0.12. At weight 0 the portfolio mean is 0.12. At weight one half it is 0.09. At weight 1 it is 0.06. The step from 0 to one half drops the mean by 0.03, and the step from one half to 1 drops it by another 0.03. Equal steps in weight, equal steps in mean. If a later plot shows a curve, look at which axis bent. On this pair, with correlation 0, the standard deviation at weight 0 is 0.30, at weight one half is about 0.158, and at weight 1 is 0.10. Those steps are not equal. The drop from 0.30 to 0.158 is about 0.142, and the drop from 0.158 to 0.10 is about 0.058, and you already know the path actually dips to about 0.0949 near weight 0.90 before rising to 0.10. The mean did none of that bending. If your notes ever show a curved mean, you have mixed the axes or you have left the model.

A legal matrix, set next to the illegal one, keeps the semidefinite warning from sounding like a slogan. Variances 0.01 and 0.09 with covariance 0 have determinant 0.01 times 0.09 minus 0, which is 0.0009, positive. Both eigenvalues are the variances themselves, 0.01 and 0.09, both positive. The same variances with covariance 0.03, which is correlation exactly 1, have determinant 0.0009 minus 0.0009, which is 0. The matrix is positive semidefinite and singular. The picture is allowed, and it is degenerate, the line segment you will meet when rho equals 1. The nonsense case was the covariance 0.036, determinant negative 0.000396, which is not semidefinite at all. Three matrices, three futures for the drawing: a real bullet, a collapsed segment, or no picture. You decide which one you have before you minimize anything.

You might ask why a course that knows about fat tails is willing to stop at two moments. Because the choice is the model. For elliptical returns, mean and variance carry the shape the model keeps, and the algebra you are about to do is exact for that shape. For returns that are not elliptical, the same algebra still produces a mean and a variance. What it does not produce is permission to talk as if skew, jumps, and crash probabilities had been priced or hedged. Fat tails do not break matrix multiplication. They break the sentence that says the investor has been fully heard.

Say the same semidefinite test in the language of a portfolio that is supposed to be riskless and is not. If every variance you compute is at least zero, a claim of zero risk is a claim that you found a weight in the null direction, the way rho equal to negative 1 produced a zero at weight 0.75 on the unequal pair. That zero is allowed. A negative number is not a dramatic version of a small risk. It is the matrix failing. When you drag rho in the lab and the bullet reaches a very small standard deviation, you are watching the quadratic form approach the boundary of the semidefinite world, a variance near zero, not cross into a negative variance. The drawing should get thin. It should not flip over.

Here is what the two-moment claim does not mean. It does not mean you believe returns are normal. Elliptical is already a wider phrase than normal, and the notes allow a rough map even when you are outside that family. It does not mean a positive semidefinite matrix is evidence that the world is benign. It is a consistency condition on the quadratic form. The illegal matrix with determinant negative 0.000396 is rejected because it cannot be a covariance, not because the assets were dangerous. It does not mean variance is the only risk you will ever manage. It means this board will not carry any other risk. If you need a third moment, you have left the model, and you should say so rather than secretly reading a crash into a standard deviation. The rest of the hour is the algebra inside the choice, not a defense of the choice against every objection a trading desk can raise.

That is 4 over 9 times 0.04, plus 1 over 9 times 0.0625, plus 4 over 9 times 0.01. Compute 0.16 over 9, plus 0.0625 over 9, plus 0.04 over 9. The numerator is 0.2625 and the denominator is 9, so the variance is 0.029167. The standard deviation is about 0.1708. You paid a little variance, relative to 0.02909, to move the mean from about 0.1018 down to 0.10. The payment is small because the target is close. It is not zero, because the target is not the minimum-variance mean.

At a target of 0.08 the weight is 1, the variance is 0.04, and the standard deviation is 0.20. At a target of 0.11 the weight is one half. The variance is one half squared times 0.04, plus one half squared times 0.0625, plus 2 times one half times one half times 0.01, which is 0.01 plus 0.015625 plus 0.005, which is 0.030625. The standard deviation is 0.175. At a target of 0.14 the weight is 0, the variance is 0.0625, and the standard deviation is 0.25. At a target of 0.17 the weight on the first asset is negative one half and the weight on the second is 1.5. That portfolio is feasible for the unconstrained problem and illegal for a long-only mandate. Its mean is above every long-only mean. The objective can print it. The mandate has to refuse it in a separate sentence.

The one-half in front of the quadratic did not choose any of these weights. The return constraint did, together with the requirement that weights sum to 1. With two assets those two equalities leave a zero-dimensional slice. The weight is pinned. With three assets the same two equalities would leave one free direction, a subspace of portfolios that all hit the target mean and all sum to 1, and only then would the quadratic have something to minimize inside the slice. That freedom is what a long-only book spends on constraints. Each bound you add, a weight at least zero, a weight at most a limit, a sector cap, uses up freedom the two-asset problem never had. Boyd's quadratic program is the sentence you write when the freedom and the inequalities are both present. On two assets with only equalities, the program collapses to the arithmetic you just did, and bringing a solver to it would only hide the pin.

The lab's instruction fits this board. Drag correlation first. The shape of the bullet is the lecture. The marked point is the global minimum-variance mix, shorts allowed. A target-return point is a different mark. It sits on the same curve, higher variance unless you happened to target the minimum-variance mean, and it can leave the long-only arc when the target leaves the range of the two means. If you drag rho toward 1, the interior collapses and the target may become expensive in variance for a boring reason: there is less and less room between the endpoints. If you drag rho toward negative 1, a very small standard deviation becomes available somewhere on the curve. That somewhere is still algebra until the borrow, the basis, and the gap risk are named.

A three-asset sketch shows the free direction without pretending you need a solver for the two-asset pin. Suppose three assets are uncorrelated, with variances 0.01, 0.04, and 0.09, and the only constraint is that the weights sum to 1. With no covariance terms, the global minimum-variance weights are proportional to the reciprocals of the variances. Those reciprocals are 100, 25, and 100 divided by 9. Their sum is 1225 divided by 9. The normalized weights are 36 over 49, 9 over 49, and 4 over 49. About 0.735, 0.184, and 0.082. The quiet asset takes most of the book, and the noisiest asset takes the least, which is the same instinct as the two-asset weight 0.90 on the quieter name, now split across three diagonal entries. The variance of this mix is 9 over 1225, and the standard deviation is 3 over 35, about 0.0857. No target return was imposed. The mean, whatever the three means are, falls out after the weights are chosen.

Add a target return and the slice is no longer a point. You still require the weights to sum to 1, and you now require the weighted mean to equal a number. Two equalities, three weights, one free direction left. Many portfolios hit the same target mean. The quadratic program, or the two-by-two system you get from the derivatives when the matrix is diagonal, picks the one of those portfolios with the smallest variance. A long-only book then spends that leftover freedom on inequalities, a weight at least zero, a weight at most a cap, until the free direction is gone or pinned to a boundary. None of that extra structure appeared in the two-asset target arithmetic, where the weight two thirds was forced before variance was allowed an opinion. The contrast is the point of the slide. Two assets, two equalities, pinned. More assets, a subspace, then constraints.

You might ask whether minimizing variance instead of half variance changes the portfolio you deliver. It does not. The one half cancels the 2 from the derivative, and the constraint set does not mention the factor. The minimizer of variance and the minimizer of half variance, on the same equalities, are the same weight vector. You can check it on the target of 0.10. The weight two thirds was pinned by the means before any objective was evaluated. The objective only scored it, and the score was a variance of about 0.02917 whether or not you halve it afterward. Halving would change the number you hand to a solver as the optimal value. It would not change the holdings.

Here is what the differentiable objective does not mean. It does not mean every portfolio problem in two assets needs an iterative solver. You just pinned five targets with a linear equation and scored them with the quadratic. It does not mean a long-only book with extra constraints remains a two-line solve. Once inequalities bind, more than one weight can sit on a boundary, and the cosmetic one half does not tell you which boundaries. That is a quadratic program. It does not mean a target return is the same problem as global minimum variance. Global minimum variance does not mention mu sub p. The mean falls out after you minimize. In the example it fell out near 0.1018. Targeting 0.14 forced you onto the noisier asset and a variance of 0.0625. Those are different points, and only one of them is the marked minimum the lab is drawing. It does not mean a covariance that fails to be positive semidefinite becomes acceptable because the objective looks smooth. The illegal matrix from the first board, determinant negative 0.000396, can still be written into the formula one half w transpose capital sigma w. The formula will not stop you. The picture is still nonsense, because some direction has negative curvature and the critical point is not a minimum. Differentiability is not a substitute for the semidefinite requirement. You minimize a quadratic that deserves the name variance, subject to the equalities you actually imposed, and you call the result by the name of that problem, not by the name of a neighboring problem you wish you had solved.

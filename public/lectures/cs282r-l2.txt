[[BEAT 0]]

Name the idea. A temporal convolution is a local, shared filter. The same weights slide across time. That is a strong assumption. The pattern you care about has a width, and it means the same thing at ten oh one as at ten oh four. The filter is cheap, and it is biased toward locality. Sometimes that bias is the regularizer you wanted. Sometimes it is a mistake you have dressed up as an architecture.

Take the assumption apart before any arithmetic. Shared weights mean the kernel does not get a new set of coefficients because the clock advanced. If the kernel is three taps long, you own three numbers, not three numbers per time. Those three numbers multiply three neighboring inputs, then you step forward one time and multiply again. Local means the output at a time is a function of a window, not of the whole day, unless you build a wider window by stacking or by dilation. The phrase "beyond what they multiply" matters. The weights do not look at the input and then rewrite themselves. Attention will do a cousin of that rewrite. A convolution does not. The input enters as the thing being multiplied. It does not enter as a second program that chooses the multiplier.

The times in the claim are there so you do not treat the assumption as poetry. Ten oh one and ten oh four are three minutes apart. A pattern that means the same thing at both instants is a pattern whose shape, not whose clock position, carries the meaning. A burst of signed imbalance that lasts a few ticks can be such a pattern. A pattern whose meaning is "this is the open" or "this is the minute before an announcement" is not such a pattern, because the same local shape at those two clocks is not the same event. The convolution will still fire. It has no other way to behave. The bias toward locality is then not a regularizer. It is a refusal to let the clock position change the weights.

Work a receptive field with a width you state as a choice for the calculation. The lab does not hand you this kernel. The slide states the geometry: stacking widens the receptive field linearly in the depth, or exponentially with dilation. Let the kernel width be three, the stride be one, and the dilation of each layer be one. One layer sees three consecutive times. Call the receptive field three. A second layer of the same kind sits on the outputs of the first. Each of those outputs already summarizes three times, and neighboring outputs overlap. The earliest time that can affect the second-layer output is two steps before the earliest time that affects the central first-layer output. Count it as one plus two times the depth. Depth one gives one plus two, which is three. Depth two gives one plus four, which is five. Depth three gives one plus six, which is seven. Depth four gives nine. Depth five gives eleven. Each added layer buys two new times, one on each side, not a new copy of the whole past. The growth is linear in the depth. If you wanted a receptive field of eleven one-second ticks, five layers of width three give it to you, and five layers of width three is not the same object as one layer of width eleven. The single wide layer owns eleven coefficients. The stack owns three coefficients per layer, fifteen coefficients if nothing is tied across layers, and the intermediate maps are forced to be local compositions. That force is the bias.

Check the linear claim against the clock in the opening sentence. From ten oh one to ten oh four is three minutes. If your ticks are one second, three minutes is one hundred eighty ticks. A width-three stack grows the receptive field by two ticks per layer. To reach one hundred eighty one, because the field has the form one plus two times depth, you would need a depth of ninety. Ninety layers to cover three minutes is a particular design, and it may be an absurd one. The arithmetic is not an order to build ninety layers. It is a way to see that "local" does not become "the whole morning" just because you stacked twice. Depth two sees five ticks. At one-second ticks, five seconds is not the distance from ten oh one to ten oh four.

Dilation is the other clause of the slide, and it changes the slope. Keep the kernel width at three, so each layer still owns three taps, but space the taps. The extra reach of a layer is two times its dilation, the two being the kernel width minus one. Start from a receptive field of one, which is a single time before any layer. A dilation of one adds two, and the field is three. A dilation of two adds four, and the field is seven. A dilation of four adds eight, and the field is fifteen. A dilation of eight adds sixteen, and the field is thirty-one. The dilations themselves were one, two, four, and eight, a doubling schedule. The receptive field went three, seven, fifteen, thirty-one, which is one less than two, eight, sixteen, and thirty-two. Each doubling of the dilation roughly doubles the field. That is the exponential clause. Four layers did what a plain stack would need a depth of fifteen to do, because a plain field of thirty-one is one plus two times fifteen. You spent four layers of three taps, twelve coefficients if each layer has its own kernel, and you can see thirty-one ticks. You have not cancelled the shared-weight assumption. You have only changed how fast locality expands. The taps are still the same weights at ten oh one and at ten oh four, only farther apart.

Do one more width so the number three does not look magical. Suppose the kernel width is five, stride one, dilation one. Each layer adds four times of context, two on each side. The receptive field is one plus four times the depth. Depth one sees five. Depth two sees nine. Depth three sees thirteen. Compare that with the width-three stack, which needed depth four to see nine and still saw only seven at depth three. A wider kernel buys field faster and spends more coefficients per layer. Five coefficients instead of three, every layer. The assumption gets stronger in a different direction: the pattern's width is five, and those five weights still mean the same thing wherever the window sits. If the true pattern is two ticks wide, three of the five taps are an invitation to fit noise. If the true pattern is fifty ticks wide and changes shape after lunch, neither the width-three stack nor the width-five stack has abandoned the shared-weight claim. They have only chosen different neighborhoods.

A numerical slide of one shared kernel will keep the geometry from floating above the arithmetic. Again the sequence is a paper example, not the six lab numbers. Let the inputs be one, three, two, three, one, and four. Let the kernel be one, zero, and negative one, applied causally to the window that ends at the current time: the output is the value two steps back, minus the current value. The zero in the middle means the middle tap never contributes, which is a legal kernel, not a hole in the method. At the third position, counting from one, the window is one, three, and two, and the output is one minus two, which is negative one. At the fourth position the window is three, two, and three, and the output is three minus three, which is zero. At the fifth the window is two, three, and one, and the output is two minus one, which is one. At the sixth the window is three, one, and four, and the output is three minus four, which is negative one. The four outputs are negative one, zero, one, and negative one. The weights never changed. Only the window did. That is the shared filter, written as four subtractions.

Replace the kernel with a local average, one third, one third, and one third, on the same causal windows. The third position averages one, three, and two, which is two. The fourth averages three, two, and three, which is eight thirds. The fifth averages two, three, and one, which is two. The sixth averages three, one, and four, which is eight thirds. Same windows as the difference kernel, different weights, different outputs. The average did not become a difference by sliding. Sliding reused the average. If you had allowed a new kernel at the sixth position, say one, zero, and zero, the output there would have been three, the first element of that window, and you would no longer be describing a temporal convolution. You would be describing a list of unrelated filters that happen to be short. The cheapness came from refusing that list.

Put the two clocks on these outputs without pretending the toy tape is three minutes long. The claim is that the same weights mean the same thing at ten oh one and at ten oh four. In the arithmetic you just did, the same weights meant the same linear function at the third position and at the sixth. If those positions were ten oh one and ten oh four, the convolution's promise is that "two steps back, minus now" is still the feature you want at both clocks. If at ten oh four the economically meaningful comparison is against the open rather than against two steps back, the shared kernel will not discover that. It will subtract the current value from three steps earlier in the window and hand you negative one, faithfully, under the wrong assumption.

The question to answer is why you would stack at all, instead of writing one wide kernel and being done. You stack when you want the intermediate computation to be local and shared, and when you want depth, rather than a single inner product, to build the field. You write one wide kernel when you really believe the pattern is a single dot product against a long window and you are willing to estimate every tap separately. Stacking is not free accuracy. A stack of width three, depth five, sees eleven ticks and never represents an arbitrary pattern on those eleven ticks. It represents the patterns that can be built by composing local shared filters. That restriction is sometimes the regularizer you wanted, because an arbitrary eleven-tap kernel has eleven free numbers and will use them. The restriction is a mistake when the event is not local, or when ten oh one and ten oh four do not share a meaning. Cheap is a statement about reused weights. It is not a statement about being right.

What the claim does not mean is that a convolution is a small attention mechanism, or that a transformer is a convolution that grew up. The weights of the convolution do not depend on the input except as the values being multiplied. There is no score, no softmax, and no data-dependent average inside the filter itself. The claim also does not mean that locality is a moral good. A local filter can miss a dependence that runs from the open to the afternoon. Dilation can reach farther, and the exponential growth is real, as fifteen and then thirty-one showed, but the weights remain shared. If that sharing is false at the two clocks named in the idea, a larger field repeats the falsehood over more taps. It does not repair it.

Hold the lab still while you keep this distinction. The six signed imbalance numbers are a sequence in time, and the tool you are about to use on them is not a convolution. Do not describe a sliding kernel you have not fit and call it the lab. The convolution is the assumption you are setting beside attention so you can say, later, what the weighted average refuses to assume.

[[BEAT 1]]

The idea now is that attention is a data-dependent weighted average. For a query q and keys k sub i, the scores are the product q times k sub i, divided by the square root of d, and then you take a softmax. The output is a sum of values weighted by those probabilities. In the lab, d equals one, the value is the number itself, and temperature plays the role of the scale. You can do one row on paper. That is the whole ambition of the calculation. It is not a trained transformer, and you should not talk as if a paper row were a forecast. The forecast question comes after the mask. Here you learn to produce the weights.

Say the three moves in order, because the order is the method. First you score. Second you turn scores into non-negative weights that sum to one, by a softmax. Third you form the weighted sum of the values. If you skip to the weighted sum without the scores, you have a smoother you chose by hand. If you stop at the scores, you have not yet averaged anything. The dependence on the data sits in the score. Change the query, or change a key, and the weights move. A convolution would have kept its weights and only changed the inputs being multiplied. This is the contrast you came in with.

The scale has a job when d is large. A dot product of d independent-ish coordinates grows like the square root of d, and a softmax of large scores saturates. One coordinate wins, the others go to zero, and you stop learning anything useful from the gradient of a nearly hard maximum. Dividing by the square root of d pulls the scores back toward a range where the softmax still has slope. In the lab, d equals one. The square root of one is one. Dividing by one does nothing, which is why the lecture calls it a yawn. Temperature replaces that yawn with a knob you can turn. The weight on index i is proportional to e to the power of q times k sub i, divided by tau, where tau is the temperature. A small tau sharpens. A large tau flattens. The proportionality becomes an equality when you divide by the sum of those exponentials, which is the softmax.

Write the softmax in words, including the stabilization you should actually compute. Let m be the maximum of the scores you are willing to include. Softmax of z at i is e to the power of z sub i minus m, divided by the sum over j of e to the power of z sub j minus m. Subtracting m does not change the softmax. Every term in the numerator and the denominator is multiplied by the same factor, e to the power of negative m, and that factor cancels. What the subtraction changes is the numerics. The largest exponent you actually compute is zero, so you do not overflow. If your paper row disagrees with the lab, the mask or the max-subtraction is the usual culprit. A disagreement that is only "I forgot to subtract the max" should not change the weights once you repair it. A disagreement that is "I included a future index" is not the same bug.

Let the three keys at or before now be one, zero, and negative one. Because d equals one, the score is the ordinary product divided by tau. The scores are one, zero, and negative one. The maximum m is one. Subtract it. The centered scores are zero, negative one, and negative two. The exponentials are e to the zero, which is one; e to the negative one, which is about zero point three six seven nine; and e to the negative two, which is about zero point one three five three. The sum is about one point five zero three two. Divide. The weights are about zero point six six five, zero point two four five, and zero point zero nine zero. They sum to one, up to the rounding. The largest weight sits on the key whose sign matches the query and whose product is the largest. The key of negative one is not erased. It keeps about nine hundredths. Temperature one is not a hard maximum.

The question that belongs to this calculation is why the lab bothers with temperature when the famous formula divides by the square root of d. Because d equals one, and the square root of d is a yawn. The division by the square root of d is the scale that keeps a high-dimensional softmax out of the saturated region. With one-dimensional keys, that scale is one, and you would have no knob. Temperature is the knob. It plays the role of the scale. It does not play the role of a second model. You are not fitting tau on a test set in this lab. You are moving it so you can see sharpening and flattening with your own arithmetic.

What the claim does not mean is that a weighted average of past numbers is a prediction of the next number. You have not looked at a future key yet, and you should not. It also does not mean that the weights are a convolution in disguise. The weights moved when the query changed, and they moved when tau changed, without any edit to a shared kernel. It also does not mean that max-subtraction is a different softmax. The factor cancels. If you subtract the max and your weights change, you have a bug in the division, not a new theory. Finally, equal scores give equal weights. The formula does not contain a quiet preference for the latest tick. If you want that preference, you have to write it down as something the score depends on. Until you do, two matching signs share the row.

The idea that keeps the average honest is the mask. A causal mask sets future scores to negative infinity before the softmax, so a forecast cannot read the ticks it is supposed to predict. The lab's query only attends to indexes at or before now. If you remove that, the pretty fit is leakage. Attend only to j less than or equal to the query index. A bidirectional encoder is not a forecaster. Say which one you trained.

Negative infinity is a calculation, not a mood. Take a score that you set to negative infinity. Subtract the maximum of the scores you are keeping, or, if you are careless, let the negative infinity be the thing you exponentiate directly. e to the power of negative infinity is zero. A zero numerator is a zero weight, as long as the denominator is a sum over the legal indexes and is not zero. The future tick contributes nothing to the weighted sum. The legal ticks are renormalized over themselves. You do not need a second formula. You need to refuse the future index before the exponentials are added.

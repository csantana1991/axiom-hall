[[BEAT 0]]
The idea in this block is that the full gradient is an average, and that a stochastic step is one term of that average, legal as an unbiased estimate only when the index is random. If the loss is a mean over examples, its gradient is a mean of gradients. You may replace the mean by a single term, or by a batch, and the noise you accept is not a decoration. It is the price of a cheaper estimate. The estimate stays unbiased only while every index you might have drawn is doing its share of the average.

Write the identity before any path. The empirical risk is one over n times the sum of per-example losses. Differentiate under the sum. The gradient of the empirical risk is one over n times the sum of the per-example gradients. Nothing stochastic has happened yet. A stochastic step picks an index I, or a handful of indices, and steps using those gradients alone. If I is uniform on the sample, the expected value of the gradient of the loss at I equals the gradient of the empirical risk. That is the sentence on the board. In words, the expectation of the gradient of ell sub I equals the gradient of R hat. Uniform is doing the work. It is the reason the expectation of one term reproduces the average of all the terms.

The lab you already walked does not contain a sample. Its update uses the true gradient of the two-coordinate bowl, the square of x minus 1 plus 3 times the square of y plus 0.4. I am going to keep that gradient as the thing being estimated, and I am going to invent three deviations so the word unbiased has arithmetic. The deviations are not in the lab. The lab remains the noiseless reference. The three deviations, in the x and y coordinates, are negative 3 and negative 1.8, then 0 and 0.6, then 3 and 1.2. Their average is zero in both coordinates. Check the x average: negative 3 plus 0 plus 3 is 0. Check the y average: negative 1.8 plus 0.6 plus 1.2 is 0. Adding a mean-zero deviation to the full gradient does not change the expected step.

At the origin the full gradient is negative 2 and positive 2.4, and the function value is 1.48. Add each deviation. The first stochastic gradient is negative 5 and 0.6. The second is negative 2 and 3.0. The third is 1 and 3.6. Average the x coordinates: negative 5 plus negative 2 plus 1 is negative 6, and negative 6 over 3 is negative 2. Average the y coordinates: 0.6 plus 3.0 plus 3.6 is 7.2, and 7.2 over 3 is 2.4. The average of the three stochastic gradients is the full gradient. That is unbiasedness, on this list, at this point.

Take the step size 0.1 and apply each stochastic gradient once, from the origin, with the minus sign in the update. The first step subtracts 0.1 times negative 5 from x, so x goes to 0.5, and subtracts 0.1 times 0.6 from y, so y goes to negative 0.06. The second step sends x to 0.2 and y to negative 0.3. The third step subtracts 0.1 times 1 from x, so x goes to negative 0.1, and subtracts 0.1 times 3.6 from y, so y goes to negative 0.36. The average of the three landing x coordinates is 0.5 plus 0.2 plus negative 0.1, which is 0.6, divided by 3, which is 0.2. The average of the landing y coordinates is negative 0.06 plus negative 0.3 plus negative 0.36, which is negative 0.72, divided by 3, which is negative 0.24. The full-gradient step with the same step size lands exactly at 0.2 and negative 0.24. The expected stochastic landing point matches the full step. The individual landing points do not. One of them, the third, moved x from 0 to negative 0.1, which is farther from the minimizer at x equals 1, not closer. Unbiasedness is a statement about the average of the steps you might have taken. It is not a statement that the step you did take pointed downhill in every coordinate.

The function values make the same split visible. At 0.5 and negative 0.06 the bowl is the square of negative 0.5, plus 3 times the square of 0.34, which is 0.25 plus 3 times 0.1156, which is 0.25 plus 0.3468, which is 0.5968. At 0.2 and negative 0.3 the bowl is the square of negative 0.8, plus 3 times the square of 0.1, which is 0.64 plus 0.03, which is 0.67. At negative 0.1 and negative 0.36 the bowl is the square of negative 1.1, plus 3 times the square of 0.04, which is 1.21 plus 0.0048, which is 1.2148. All three are below the starting value 1.48, so this particular list happened to descend in the function even when x moved the wrong way. You should not promote that accident into a guarantee. The guarantee you have is about the expected gradient, not about every realized function value on every loss.

Variance is the other half of the estimate, and the lesson's scaling is specific. If the terms are weakly dependent, the variance of a batch average falls like 1 over the batch size. Use the x deviations negative 3, 0, and 3, whose mean is 0. The mean of their squares is 9 plus 0 plus 9, divided by 3, which is 6. One uniform draw of the x deviation has that mean squared size, 6. An average of b independent draws has variance 6 over b. A batch of 1 keeps 6. A batch of 2 cuts it to 3. A batch of 10 cuts it to 0.6. The y deviations negative 1.8, 0.6, and 1.2 have squares 3.24, 0.36, and 1.44, which sum to 5.04, and 5.04 divided by 3 is 1.68. The same scaling says a batch of 10 has y variance 0.168 if the draws are independent. The scaling is not a promise about a batch assembled by some other rule. Weak dependence is a hypothesis you have to own. If the terms move together, the 1 over batch factor is the wrong picture, and a large batch can fail to shrink the noise by anything like that factor.

You might ask whether cycling through the examples in a fixed order is just as good as drawing I uniformly, since each example still appears. It is not the same hypothesis. The expectation in the identity is an average over the random index. If the index is always the first deviation, the stochastic gradient at the origin is always negative 5 and 0.6. Its expectation is negative 5 and 0.6, not negative 2 and 2.4. The step with eta equals 0.1 always lands at 0.5 and negative 0.06. That point is one of the three legal random landings, and it is not the expected landing. A deterministic cycle can be analyzed as its own algorithm. It is not, by itself, the unbiased estimate written here, because the index is not random. Random is a technical term in this lesson. It means the expectation that reproduces the full gradient is the expectation you are actually taking.

What the claim does not mean is easy to overshoot, so say the limits while the numbers are still in the air. It does not mean a stochastic step is a small perturbation of the full step. The three x gradients were negative 5, negative 2, and positive 1, against a full gradient of negative 2. The first is more than twice the full x gradient. Cheaper does not mean gentler. It does not mean the noise is optional flavor you add after the answer is known. If you want the cheaper estimate, the noise is the estimate. It does not mean every batch size is a new theory. The identity for a uniform single index is the basic fact, and the variance remark says the spread of an average of weakly dependent terms scales like 1 over the batch. It does not mean the lab has been stochastic all along. Leave the lab on the full gradient. At the origin that gradient is negative 2 and 2.4, the step of size 0.1 lands on 0.2 and negative 0.24, and the function value there is 0.7168. That landing is the zero-noise reference. You add noise only after the sign on that reference is right. A plus sign plus noise is still the wrong direction, with extra scatter.

Change one input and recompute the unbiasedness check, so the average is not a single stunt. Replace the third deviation's x component by 6, and repair the first deviation's x component from negative 3 to negative 6, leaving the middle x deviation at 0. The new x deviations are negative 6, 0, and 6, which still average to 0. At the origin the new stochastic x gradients are negative 2 plus negative 6, which is negative 8, then negative 2, then negative 2 plus 6, which is 4. The average is negative 8 plus negative 2 plus 4, which is negative 6, divided by 3, which is negative 2. Still unbiased. The mean squared x deviation is 36 plus 0 plus 36, divided by 3, which is 24, four times the old 6, because you doubled the outer deviations and the square produced a factor of 4. A batch of 10 independent draws now has x variance 24 over 10, which is 2.4, where before it had 0.6. Same full gradient, same batch size, larger noise, because the deviations changed. The full step did not move. The cloud around it did. That is the distinction you keep when you read a stochastic path later: location of the expected step, versus spread of the step you will actually see.

[[BEAT 1]]
The idea in this block is that convergence, for a constant step, is convergence to a neighborhood rather than to a point, and that it is slower than the noiseless walk. On a smooth strongly convex function, constant-step stochastic gradient descent does not sit on the minimizer. It jitters in a ball. The radius of that ball shrinks if you decay the step. A proof that shows you a single point, rather than a ball, brought a schedule with it even if the schedule was not the part you circled.

The noiseless walk is the comparison, and you already know its fixed point on this bowl. The minimizer is x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.4, where the gradient is exactly 0 and 0 and the function value is 0. A full-gradient update subtracts eta times the zero vector and stays there. Once the noiseless iteration arrives, it sits. That sitting is what the stochastic update gives up.

Use the same three deviations as in the previous block, because their mean is zero and their size is known. At the minimizer the full gradient vanishes, so the stochastic gradient equals the deviation itself. The three possibilities are negative 3 and negative 1.8, then 0 and 0.6, then 3 and 1.2. None of them is the zero vector. A step cannot stay put unless the vector it subtracts is zero.

Take eta equals 0.1 and start exactly at the minimizer, function value 0. If the draw is the first deviation, the update subtracts 0.1 times negative 3 from x and 0.1 times negative 1.8 from y, which adds 0.3 and adds 0.18. You land at 1.3 and negative 0.22. The function value is the square of 0.3, plus 3 times the square of 0.18. That is 0.09 plus 3 times 0.0324, which is 0.09 plus 0.0972, which is 0.1872. If the draw is the second deviation, x does not move, and y moves by minus 0.1 times 0.6, to negative 0.46. The function value is 3 times the square of negative 0.06, which is 3 times 0.0036, which is 0.0108. If the draw is the third deviation, you subtract 0.3 from x and 0.12 from y, landing at 0.7 and negative 0.52. The function value is the square of negative 0.3, plus 3 times the square of negative 0.12, which is 0.09 plus 3 times 0.0144, which is 0.09 plus 0.0432, which is 0.1332. Three possible function values, 0.1872, 0.0108, and 0.1332, and the smallest of them is already above 0. The iteration was on the minimizer and the constant step kicked it off. That is the whole phenomenon, with no asymptotics required.

The expectation of the landing point is still the minimizer, which is why the phrase "in expectation" has to stay in the sentence. Average the three x landings: 1.3 plus 1 plus 0.7 is 3, divided by 3 is 1. Average the y landings: negative 0.22 plus negative 0.46 plus negative 0.52 is negative 1.2, divided by 3 is negative 0.4. The average position is perfect. You will not see the average position on a single run. You will see one of the three kicks, and then, from wherever you landed, another kick, because a constant step does not get smaller. The cloud is not a temporary entrance cost. It is the steady behavior of a step that keeps multiplying a mean-zero but nonzero gradient noise by the same eta.

Shrink the step and watch the cloud shrink, which is the decay half of the claim. Replace 0.1 by 0.01 and repeat the same three deviations from the minimizer. The first landing is 1.03 and negative 0.382, because you added 0.03 and 0.018. The function value scales by the square of the step ratio. You cut the step by 10, so the squared errors cut by 100, and the old function value 0.1872 becomes 0.001872. The second landing is 1 and negative 0.406, function value 0.000108. The third landing is 0.97 and negative 0.412, function value 0.001332. The kicks are still there. They are a tenth as long, and the function values are a hundredth as large, because the bowl is quadratic. A smaller constant step buys a smaller ball and pays for it with a slower noiseless contraction, the same trade you already saw when a tiny full-gradient step finished after lunch. Decay is how you ask for the ball to keep shrinking rather than to freeze at the radius that belongs to one fixed eta.

A schedule is a list of steps, not a mood. Suppose you take the three deviations in order and you halve the step each time, starting at 0.1, and you start at the minimizer so the only motion is the kick. The first step uses 0.1 on the deviation negative 3 and negative 1.8 and lands at 1.3 and negative 0.22, function value 0.1872, as before. The second step uses 0.05 on the next deviation, which is 0 and 0.6. From 1.3 and negative 0.22 you subtract 0.05 times 0 in x and 0.05 times 0.6 in y, so you land at 1.3 and negative 0.25. You have not repaired the x error of 0.3, because that draw had no x component and the step was spent on y. The third step uses 0.025 on the deviation 3 and 1.2. You subtract 0.075 from x and 0.03 from y, landing at 1.225 and negative 0.28. The kicks are getting shorter. The early kick of size 0.3 in x is still mostly there, because later steps are too small to walk it back in one draw, and this draw only removed 0.075 of it. Decay can recover a point, and it can recover it slowly, and a schedule that falls too fast can freeze you near a place the early noise chose. The lesson's sentence is the slow recovery, not a promise that any decreasing sequence is wise.

That last calculation is also why a constant step and a decaying step answer different questions. With the constant step 0.1, each visit near the minimizer is followed by a kick of the same typical size, so the iteration keeps a radius. With a decaying step, those kicks get smaller, so a point estimate becomes conceivable, and the time you wait for the late, tiny steps to finish the noiseless part of the walk gets longer. Proofs that claim the iterate itself goes to the minimizer have a schedule in them. If the writeup you are reading shows a constant step and a single limiting point, something in the argument is averaging the iterates, or changing the step, or using a hypothesis this lesson did not state. Do not fill that gap with optimism. Name the schedule or name the ball.

You might ask whether the ball is an artifact of starting with large deviations. Change one input, the step, which we already shrunk, or change one deviation and recompute the kick. Replace the first deviation by negative 1 and negative 0.6, and replace the third by 1 and 0.6, leaving the middle at 0 and 0.6. The mean of the x deviations is negative 1 plus 0 plus 1, which is 0. The mean of the y deviations is negative 0.6 plus 0.6 plus 0.6, which is 0.6, not 0. That repair is required before you trust the example. The middle y deviation has to come down. Set the middle y deviation to 0 so the y mean is negative 0.6 plus 0 plus 0.6, which is 0. The new deviations are negative 1 and negative 0.6, then 0 and 0, then 1 and 0.6. They average to zero. At the minimizer, with eta equals 0.1, the three landings are 1.1 and negative 0.34, then 1 and negative 0.4, then 0.9 and negative 0.46. One of the three draws now leaves you exactly on the minimizer, because that deviation was the zero vector. The other two still kick you to function values of the square of 0.1 plus 3 times the square of 0.06, which is 0.01 plus 3 times 0.0036, which is 0.01 plus 0.0108, which is 0.0208. The ball is smaller than the ball from the deviations of size 3, and it is not a single point, because two of the three draws move. Cutting the deviation cut the radius. It did not change the logic of a constant step.

The lab stays full-batch on purpose while you absorb this. Its gradient at a point is the gradient, not a draw. If the path on the lab is running away, the sign or the step is wrong, and noise is not available as an excuse, because you have not added any. Add noise only after that path is heading toward 1 and negative 0.4. The noiseless reference at step size 0.1, after one step from the origin, sits at 0.2 and negative 0.24 with function value 0.7168, and the later noiseless steps keep multiplying the x error by 0.8 and the y error by 0.4. A stochastic path that you cannot compare to that reference is a path you cannot debug. When you later minibatch a real loss, keep this picture as the zero-noise reference: same update direction, no deviation, a point you can sit on once you reach it.

What the claim does not mean needs the same care as the claim. It does not mean constant-step stochastic descent fails to be useful. A small ball around a good parameter can be the object you wanted, especially if the noiseless walk was already an empirical-risk walk rather than a walk on the true risk. It does not mean the radius is the number 0.1872. That was the function value of one draw with one step and one list of deviations. A different step, or the repaired deviations, produced 0.0208. The claim is the existence of a neighborhood that does not collapse while eta stays put, not a universal radius. It does not mean decaying the step removes the need for the unbiased index. A schedule multiplies whatever gradient you hand it. If the index is stuck on one example, you are descending a different function, very precisely, and a tiny late step will sit near that example's minimizer rather than near the minimizer of the average. It does not mean the full-gradient theory's step-size ceiling is automatically the stochastic ceiling. On this bowl the noiseless ceiling was one third, from the curvature 6. Stochastic noise can demand a shorter step than the curvature alone, because the kick is eta times a vector that does not vanish at the minimizer. The lesson does not give you a second formula for that shorter step. It gives you the qualitative split: constant step, ball; decaying step, a point, slowly.

[[BEAT 2]]
The idea in this block is that a minibatch drawn from a financial time series is not automatically a sample from the distribution you will trade, and that the word random does not license a shuffled tape. Shuffling leaks the future into the batch if you are careless in one direction, and it destroys dependence if you are careless in the other. A scrambled path is not a sample from the trading distribution. Walk-forward splits belong to the sequence-model course. In this course the obligation is narrower and stricter: report the split before the loss.

The theory you just used treated the index as uniform on a list of examples that enter symmetrically. That symmetry is a hypothesis. Independent and identically distributed examples satisfy it. A tape of prices in time does not become a list of that kind because you want a stochastic-gradient theorem to apply. If you permute the tape, you may create a list that looks exchangeable inside the optimizer and that could not have been a training set on the day any one of its members was still in the future. The gradient at an early time then contains a later outcome. That is leakage. If instead you refuse to permute and you also pretend the examples are independent, you have the other failure: the dependence that makes the next outcome resemble the recent ones is the dependence your variance formula just dropped. The remark that variance falls like 1 over batch size needed weak dependence. A strongly dependent tape does not owe you that factor.

Make the leak arithmetic with a short tape of outcomes, labeled by time and not offered as a market record. Let the outcomes at times 1 through 5 be 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Suppose the loss is squared error and the parameter is one-dimensional, the same toy as the empirical-risk calculation, used here only as a score you can finish by hand. A walk-forward split that respects time trains on the first three outcomes and scores on the last two. The training mean is 1 plus 2 plus 3, divided by 3, which is 2. The training average squared error at 2 is one third of 1 plus 0 plus 1, which is two thirds. The scoring outcomes are 4 and 5. At the parameter 2 their squared errors are 4 and 9, and the scoring average is 13 over 2, which is 6.5. The number you are allowed to call a score, on this split, is 6.5. The number two thirds is a training average on the past. Reporting two thirds as if it were the score is how a split gets lost.

Now shuffle the same five numbers into one pool and minimize squared error on all of them, which is the operation a careless minibatch regime performs when it draws times freely. The mean of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 is 3. The average squared error at 3 is the average of 4, 1, 0, 1, and 4, which is 10 over 5, which is 2. The fitted parameter moved from 2 to 3 because times 4 and 5 entered the average. The loss you can quote from this pool is 2, which looks better than the walk-forward score 6.5 and better than a fair comparison would allow, because the future helped choose the parameter. A batch that updates the fit using time 5 while the decision time is still time 3 has done the same thing on a smaller scale. The index was random inside the pool. The pool was not a legal past.

Change one input and recompute the split. Move the cut so that training uses the first four outcomes and the score uses only the last. The training mean of 1, 2, 3, and 4 is 2.5. The training average squared error at 2.5 is the average of the squares of negative 1.5, negative 0.5, 0.5, and 1.5. Those squares are 2.25, 0.25, 0.25, and 2.25, summing to 5, and 5 over 4 is 1.25. The score at 2.5 on the outcome 5 is the square of 2.5, which is 6.25. The shuffled five-point fit is still the parameter 3 with in-sample average 2. You now have three different numbers, 1.25, 6.25, and 2, from three different ways of pointing at the same five outcomes. None of them is meaningful until you say which indices were allowed into the sum. That is what it means to report the split before the loss. The loss is an average over a set. The set is the claim.

The other failure is real, and it does not require a villain. Suppose you keep time order for the split, so you do not leak, and you then build minibatches from widely separated times because you want the weak-dependence hypothesis for the variance formula. You may get something closer to that hypothesis, and you may also destroy the dependence your trading distribution actually has. On this tape the adjacent differences are all 1. The consecutive pairs are (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 4), and (4, 5). A scrambled minibatch can pair 1 with 5, difference 4, and pair 2 with 4, difference 2. The scrambled pairs are not draws from the distribution of adjacent moves. If the object you will be scored on is the next step along the tape, a gradient averaged over scrambled pairs is an unbiased estimate of a different average, the average over the scrambled world. Unbiased for the wrong average is a correct theorem pointed at the wrong target. Random did its job inside the pool you handed it. You handed it the wrong pool.

The lab on this board is the escape from both mistakes, and the escape is deliberate. The descent lab uses the true gradient of a two-dimensional function. It is empirical risk only in miniature: there is no sample. You are debugging the optimizer, not a dataset. There is no time index to shuffle and no future to leak, because there is no sequence. The gradient of the square of x minus 1, and of 3 times the square of y plus 0.4, is the update. When that update is stable, you have evidence about the sign and the step. You do not have evidence about a backtest. When you later minibatch a real loss, keep the noiseless path as the reference. If the noisy path and the noiseless path disagree about which way is downhill, fix the noise, the index, or the sign before you touch the tape.

You might ask whether a single random shuffle, frozen before any fitting and never refreshed, is a harmless way to satisfy the word uniform. It is uniform on the shuffled list. It is not a draw from the sequence you will face next, unless that sequence really is exchangeable, which is the hypothesis you were not given. Freeze the shuffle of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 into the order 5, 1, 4, 2, 3, and the sample mean is still 3, because a permutation does not change a sum. The empirical risk at 3 is still 2. What changed is any story in which the position of a number was a time. The first training window in the shuffled order starts at 5, which was the last time. A walk-forward rule applied to the shuffled positions would train on a future outcome and score on a past one. The arithmetic of the mean is innocent. The clock is not. Independent and identically distributed theory does not license that shuffle. It never saw a clock.

What the claim does not mean should keep you from turning a caution into a ban. It does not mean stochastic gradients are unusable on sequences. It means a uniform index over a pool you built is unbiased for the average over that pool, and you owe a sentence about what the pool is. It does not mean the walk-forward score 6.5 is a universal penalty for looking ahead. It is the score on this tape, this loss, and this cut. A different cut produced 6.25. It does not mean dependence always inflates variance by a factor you can quote from this lesson. The lesson says the 1 over batch scaling needs weak dependence. It does not give you the factor for a strongly dependent tape. It does not mean the two-coordinate bowl is a hidden time series. There is no sample in it. If someone shows you a stochastic path on that bowl, the noise was added to a full gradient that you can still write down, and the right first check is whether the expected step matches the full step. It does not mean you should skip the split because this course points sequence models elsewhere. You still report the split before the loss whenever the numbers you are averaging have a time index. The split is which times entered the sum. The loss is the sum. Reversing that order is how a future outcome becomes an ordinary residual and stops looking like a leak.

Hold the three blocks as one discipline. The full gradient is the average of the example gradients, and a single uniform index estimates it without bias and with more variance. A constant step jitters in a ball whose size tracks the step, and a decaying step can shrink that ball slowly. A time-ordered tape is not that index until the pool you draw from is the pool you are willing to name. The lab, until you leave it, is the average itself, with the deviations set to zero, so you can see the bowl before you see the cloud.

[[BEAT 0]]
The board in front of you is titled modulo is a division. A ring buffer maps a monotonic counter onto a slot by taking i mod capacity. The counter i only moves forward. The slot has to live in a fixed array, so something has to fold that growing counter back into the legal indexes. The fold you were taught first is the remainder after division. Integer division is the wrong instruction on a nanosecond path. It is a real division, or the library sequence that implements one, and you do not want that latency in the indexing step if a cheaper instruction computes the same remainder.

Say what the remainder is, before you replace it. i mod m is the unique integer r such that i equals q times m plus r, with r at least 0 and strictly less than m, when i is nonnegative and m is positive. The quotient q is how many whole capacities fit into i. The remainder r is the slot. For a capacity of 8, the counter 0 sits in slot 0, the counter 7 sits in slot 7, and the counter 8 sits in slot 0 again, because 8 equals 1 times 8 plus 0. The counter 19 sits in slot 3, because 19 equals 2 times 8 plus 3. You can check that by division, and you should, because the later identity is only interesting if you still know what the division would have answered.

The cheap replacement exists only for a special capacity. If the capacity m is a power of two, say m equals 2 to the k, the low bits of i are already the remainder. Dividing by 2 to the k shifts the bits of i right by k places. The bits that fall off that shift are exactly the remainder, and there are k of them. You do not have to perform the shift and then look at what fell off. A mask keeps those low bits and clears the rest. The mask is m minus 1, which is a string of ones in the low k bits and zeros above them. Bitwise and with that mask is the remainder. The formula, spoken carefully, is i mod 2 to the k equals i bitwise-and 2 to the k minus 1, for i at least 0. The lab exists so you can move the counter and watch the two sides agree while the capacity stays on a power of two.

Try the mask on the same numbers you already divided, with capacity 8. Then k is 3, because 8 is 2 to the third, and the mask is 7. In bits, 8 is 1 followed by three zeros, and 7 is three ones. The counter 0 and-ed with 7 is 0, and 0 mod 8 is 0. The counter 7 and-ed with 7 is 7, and 7 mod 8 is 7. The counter 8 and-ed with 7 is 0, and 8 mod 8 is 0. The counter 19 in bits is 10011. And with 00111 leaves 00011, which is 3. And 19 mod 8 is 3. The counter 100: 100 divided by 8 is 12 with remainder 4, because 12 times 8 is 96, and 100 minus 96 is 4. In bits, 100 ends with the low three bits 100, which is 4. The mask and the division agree.

Move the same counters to capacity 16, which is also a power of two. The mask is 15, four ones. 19 and-ed with 15 is still 3, and 19 mod 16 is 3, because 16 plus 3 is 19. 100 and-ed with 15 is 4, and 100 mod 16 is 4, because 6 times 16 is 96. Changing the power of two changed the mask from 7 to 15 and changed which bits survive, and both remainders followed. That is the whole trick. The low bits are the remainder precisely because the modulus is a pure shift.

Run the counter from 0 through 16 at capacity 8 and say the slot out loud, because a formula feels finished before your ear trusts the wrap. Mask 7. Slot of 0 is 0. Slot of 1 is 1. Slot of 2 is 2. Slot of 3 is 3. Slot of 4 is 4. Slot of 5 is 5. Slot of 6 is 6. Slot of 7 is 7. Slot of 8 is 0. Slot of 9 is 1. Slot of 10 is 2. Slot of 11 is 3. Slot of 12 is 4. Slot of 13 is 5. Slot of 14 is 6. Slot of 15 is 7. Slot of 16 is 0. Each of those matches the remainder, and the wrap happens exactly when the counter crosses a multiple of 8. The second lap is not a new array. It is the same eight addresses, visited by a counter that did not itself become small.

Change the power and do three checks at capacity 32, so the mask is 31, five ones, and not only the masks 7 and 15. The counter 40 divided by 32 leaves remainder 8, because 32 plus 8 is 40, and 40 bitwise-and 31 is 8. The counter 63 is one less than 64, so 63 mod 32 is 31, and 63 bitwise-and 31 is 31. The counter 64 is exactly two times 32, so the remainder is 0, and 64 bitwise-and 31 is 0. The identity is not getting tired as the power grows. It is the same low-bit story with a longer string of ones.

You might ask why anybody still writes the division if the mask is a single instruction. The division is the definition of the slot for an arbitrary positive capacity. The mask is an implementation of that definition under an extra hypothesis you have to earn. If you write only the mask, a reader cannot see which mathematical fold you meant, and a later edit that changes the capacity can keep the mask and lose the meaning. If you write only the division, the nanosecond path pays for a generality the array does not use. The lecture's resolution is not to pretend the operations are identical for every m. It is to restrict m until they are identical, and to make that restriction something the compiler can see.

Here is what the opening claim does not mean. It does not mean integer division is mathematically suspect. The remainder is well defined, and every example above used it as the standard. It does not mean a power-of-two capacity makes the counter itself wrap. The counter 100 is still 100. Only the slot folded. It does not mean every bitwise and is a modulus. And with an arbitrary mask is just a bit operation. It equals a remainder only for the mask that is one less than a power of two, and only for a nonnegative i, which is the hypothesis we have not yet broken on purpose. The next board breaks it.
[[BEAT 1]]
The board in front of you is titled the identity. For m equal to 2 to the k, and for i at least 0, i mod m equals i bitwise-and m minus 1. The mask m minus 1 is a string of ones in the low bits. You already watched that string work for m equal to 8 and m equal to 16. The identity is false as soon as m is not a power of two. The lab has a switch that breaks it on purpose. This board is that switch, done on paper, so the lab does not surprise you.

The counterexample on the slide is small and final. 19 mod 12 equals 7. 19 bitwise-and 11 equals 3. Same style of instruction, wrong slot. Twelve is not a power of two. Eleven is not a string of ones. In bits, 12 is 1100, so it has more than one bit set. The mask 11 is 1011. The counter 19 is 10011. And with 1011 keeps 00011, which is 3. Division says otherwise. Two twelves are 24, which is already past 19, so the quotient is 1 and the remainder is 19 minus 12, which is 7. Three and 7 are different slots. If a ring of capacity 12 used the mask, the counter 19 would address slot 3 while the mathematical ring addresses slot 7. The notes call that a torn book. The word is not decoration. Two readers of the same counter would disagree about which cell holds the item.

Change the counter and keep the bad capacity, so the failure is not a quirk of the number 19. Take i equal to 30 and m equal to 12. The mask is still 11. 30 in bits is 11110. And with 01011 leaves 01010, which is 10. Now divide. Two times 12 is 24, and 30 minus 24 is 6. So 30 mod 12 equals 6, while 30 bitwise-and 11 equals 10. Again they diverge, and they diverge to a different pair than 7 and 3. The bug is not "always off by four." There is no stable offset you could subtract to repair the mask. The low bits are simply not the remainder when the modulus has more than one bit set.

Put the same counter 30 back on a power of two so you can see the identity recover without changing the instruction, only the modulus. Capacity 16, mask 15, and 15 is 01111, a string of ones. 30 and-ed with 15 is 01110, which is 14. And 30 mod 16 equals 14, because 16 plus 14 is 30. One input changed, the capacity, from 12 to 16. The bitwise and went from a wrong slot to the right slot. That is the lab switch, read in reverse. Flip the capacity off the power of two and they diverge. Flip it back onto a power of two and they match.

The slide limits the identity, and you should say the limits before you get clever. It holds for positive i, and the zero counter is the harmless boundary you already checked, since 0 mod m is 0 and 0 and-ed with any mask is 0. Negative numbers are out of scope. Signed overflow is out of scope. If a counter is a signed integer and it passes its maximum, you are no longer in the arithmetic this identity describes. The remainder of a negative integer is a language-lawyer question this lecture refuses. The fix is not a more elaborate mask. The fix is to stay in the nonnegative range the statement names, or to use an unsigned counter whose wrap, if it ever happens, is a different protocol problem. Do not extend the formula with a story about two's complement that the notes did not claim.

A lucky hit is the trap in the bad modulus, so do not accept a single agreeing pair as a proof. Stay with capacity 12 and the mask 11, and test every remainder you can form with a counter that is still smaller than 12. For those counters, the true remainder is the counter itself. The mask agrees for 0, 1, 2, and 3, because those numbers only use bits the mask keeps. It agrees again for 8, 9, 10, and 11. It fails in the middle. 4 bitwise-and 11 is 0, not 4. 5 bitwise-and 11 is 1, not 5. 6 bitwise-and 11 is 2, not 6. 7 bitwise-and 11 is 3, not 7. If your test suite only published the counter 8, or only the counter 3, the mask would look correct on a capacity that is not a power of two. The identity claims every nonnegative i. Four quiet successes do not survive the counter 4, and they do not survive the slide's counter 19.

One more counter past a multiple, so the failure is not only the numbers below 12. Take i equal to 25 and m equal to 12. 25 bitwise-and 11: 25 is 11001 in bits, 11 is 01011, and the and is 01001, which is 9. Division: two times 12 is 24, so 25 mod 12 is 1. Nine and 1 are different slots again. You now have disagreements at 4, 5, 6, 7, 19, 25, and 30, and accidental agreements at several other counters. Agreement is not the pattern. Agreement is an accident of which bits happened to be zero.

You might ask whether a capacity like 12 could be rescued by a different mask, maybe something other than m minus 1. A different mask is still a bitwise and with a constant. Bitwise and with a constant keeps some bits and clears others. It cannot implement remainder by 12, because remainder by 12 is not a function of a fixed set of bits. You just saw 19 and 30 produce mask results 3 and 10 against true remainders 7 and 6. Those are not the low bits of anything that equals the quotient-remainder split for modulus 12. There are other cheap ways to compute a remainder by a constant, and this lecture does not develop them. The claim on the board is narrower. The identity you are allowed to use is the mask m minus 1, and only when m is a power of two.

Here is what the identity does not mean. It does not mean bitwise and is an approximate modulus that is close enough for a buffer. Off by one slot is a wrong cell, not a rounding error. Three versus 7 is a torn book, not a tolerance. It does not mean every power of two in the program is a capacity. The identity is about the modulus you divide by, which is the length of the ring. A power-of-two timestamp, or a power-of-two batch size, does not by itself license a mask on an unrelated index. It does not mean you should test the identity in production traffic. The lab flips the capacity so you can see the divergence in a controlled counter. A live ring that indexes the wrong slot has already mixed two generations of data. And it does not mean the failure is rare for non-powers of two. It failed for 19 and it failed for 30. The match for powers of two, on 0, 7, 8, 19, 30, and 100, is the special case, not the other way around.
[[BEAT 2]]
The board in front of you is titled why the capstone cares. The SPSC ring publishes a sequence number per slot and indexes with head bitwise-and capacity minus 1. That is the identity from the previous board, written as an address calculation. The slot formula in the notes is slot equals head bitwise-and capacity minus 1. The static assert in the C++ file is this lecture, enforced by the compiler. A clever runtime check is already too late. If the capacity is wrong, you want the build to fail while the mask is still a lie you have not shipped, not a branch you discover after a bad index has been computed on the hot path.

Read the assert as a placement rule, not as a slogan. The slide says to write the assert next to the array, not in a comment. A comment does not constrain the type. An assert that the compiler evaluates, next to the array whose length is the modulus, ties the mask to the object it indexes. If someone later changes the capacity to 12, the identity becomes the torn book you already computed, 19 masked to 3 against a remainder of 7. A comment would survive that edit and become false quietly. A static assert fails the build. That is the sense in which the compiler is enforcing the lecture. A runtime check on the publishing path would be checking a fact that was supposed to be true before the process started, and it would be checking it on the nanosecond path the mask was introduced to protect. Too late means both too late to be safe and too late to be cheap.

The mask is only an address calculation. It is not the fullness test. That separation is the part students collapse, and the notes are blunt about it. Students sometimes store head modulo capacity. Then a full ring and an empty ring look identical. The Vyukov protocol keeps head and tail monotonic and stores the ticket on the cell. The counter only increases. It does not wrap in the protocol. The slot index wraps. The sequence number does not. Full and empty can share a slot index. The sequence tells them apart.

Do that with capacity 8, so the mask is 7 and the arithmetic stays in the table you already trust. Suppose head and tail are both 0. Both masked slots are 0 and-ed with 7, which is 0. The difference head minus tail is 0. The ring is empty. The cell at slot 0 may carry an old ticket or no live ticket, and the protocol's sequence says this lap has not published a new item there. Now advance the producer until head equals 8 and tail is still 0. The masked slot of head is 8 and-ed with 7, which is 0. The masked slot of tail is also 0. The slot indexes look like the empty picture. The difference head minus tail is 8, and 8 equals the capacity, so the ring is full. Every cell holds a published item the consumer has not taken. If you had stored only the wrapped head and the wrapped tail, both situations would show the pair 0, 0. You would not know whether to push or to refuse the push. The monotonic counters still say 8 and 0. Their difference is the occupancy. The mask was never asked.

Move the same ring forward one more step in your head, after the consumer has caught up. Head is 8 and tail is 8. Difference 0, empty again. Both masked slots are still 0. The slot index did not record that a full lap happened. The sequence number on the cell is what distinguishes the publication that used counter 8 from the earlier publication that used counter 0, if a ticket was stored when the producer wrote. The slot was 0 both times. The ticket was not. That is the sentence on the slide: full and empty can share a slot index, and the sequence tells them apart. Sharing a slot index is normal. Sharing a pair of wrapped counters, with no monotonic difference and no ticket, is the bug.

Change the capacity to 16 and watch the address move while the fullness test stays a subtraction. Mask 15. Head equal to 19 maps to slot 19 and-ed with 15, which is 3, and 19 mod 16 is also 3. Tail equal to 19 maps to the same slot, and the difference is 0, so this state is empty even though the slot number is 3 rather than 0. Head equal to 19 and tail equal to 3 gives difference 16, which equals the capacity, so the ring is full, and the masked slots are 3 and 3. Again the slot pair collides at full and at empty if you only look at slots. The difference of the monotonic counters does not collide: 0 versus 16. Nothing about the mask computed that difference. If you replace the difference with a comparison of masked slots, you throw away the only number that knew the occupancy.

Walk a few ordinary occupancies at capacity 8 before you jump to the full ring, so subtraction and masking stay distinct in a quiet state. Head 3 and tail 1. The difference is 2, so two slots are occupied, which is neither empty nor full. The masked head is 3 and-ed with 7, which is 3. The masked tail is 1. Nothing collides, and you might fool yourself into thinking the slot pair was the occupancy. It was not. The occupancy was the difference. Head 11 and tail 3. The difference is 8, which equals the capacity, so the ring is full. The masked head is 11 and-ed with 7. Eleven is 8 plus 3, so the mask returns 3. The masked tail is 3. The slot pair is 3 and 3, the same shape as a later empty state in which both counters equal 3, or both equal 11. Fullness is the 8 in the difference. The repeated 3 is only the address of one cell, shared by a head and a tail that are a full capacity apart.

The producer can keep going. Head 20 and tail 12 at this same capacity. Difference 8, still full. Masked head is 20 and-ed with 7. Twenty is 16 plus 4, so the low three bits are 4. Masked tail is 12 and-ed with 7. Twelve is 8 plus 4, so the slot is also 4. Again one slot number, occupancy equal to the capacity, and the monotonic counters 20 and 12 are the only place the number 8 is still written down. If a later edit stores head modulo 8 back into the counter, 20 becomes 4 and 12 becomes 4, and the full ring prints as empty. That is the bug the notes describe, obtained by changing one habit, not by changing the mask.

You might ask whether the static assert makes the protocol correct by itself. It does not. The assert, as this lecture describes it, guards the hypothesis of the mask identity. It demands a power-of-two capacity so that slot equals head bitwise-and capacity minus 1 really is head mod capacity. A ring can satisfy that assert and still be wrong if the producer stores the wrapped slot back into the counter, or if the fullness test compares slots instead of monotonic head and tail, or if the cell does not carry the ticket the consumer expects. The assert does not check those. The Vyukov protocol is a separate set of commitments: monotonic counters, a ticket on the cell, and the mask used only to form the address. Passing the assert means the address step matches the remainder. It does not mean you have implemented the rest of the protocol.

Here is what the capstone claim does not mean. It does not mean the mask is a fullness test in disguise. Capacity 8, counters 0 and 0, versus counters 8 and 0, produce the same slot and opposite occupancy. It does not mean a runtime branch that checks the capacity on every publish is a safer version of the same idea. The lecture calls that check already too late. The place for the fact is beside the array, enforced when the program is built. It does not mean a non-power-of-two capacity is morally forbidden in every buffer you will ever write. It means this indexing trick is false there, as 19 mod 12 versus 19 bitwise-and 11 already showed, 7 against 3. If you need a capacity of 12, you do not get to keep this mask. You pay for a real remainder, or you round the capacity up to a power of two and you understand that the extra slots are part of the address arithmetic. It does not mean the sequence number wraps just because the slot does. The protocol on this board keeps the sequence monotonic. The slot is the only quantity that folds, and it folds only because the mask implements a remainder you were willing to prove.

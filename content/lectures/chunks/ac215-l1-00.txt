The idea in this opening block is to name the stages before you name the tools. The stages in this lecture are ingest, feature, model, risk check, and gateway. A tool is an implementation of a stage. Kubernetes does not belong in the budget until a stage's distribution is real.

Say what a budget is asking for, before any percentile. A latency budget is an account of time, and time here is random. Each stage has a duration. The duration has a mean, if you are willing to defend a mean, and it has a spread, if you are willing to defend a spread. The thing you eventually compare to a limit is a statement about the sum of those durations. You cannot write the sum until you have named the summands. Ingest is one summand. The feature computation is another. The model is another. The risk check is another. The gateway is another. Five names, five random variables, and only then a conversation about what implements them. A diagram of a cluster is not a sixth stage. It is a way you might run one of the five, or several of them, and it enters the budget only by changing the distribution of a stage you already named.

A point estimate with no spread is a wish. If you write down a single number of time units for the feature stage and you will not defend a deviation around it, you have not given the budget a random variable. You have given it a hope. The later arithmetic needs a mean and a standard deviation for each stage you include. Refusing to state the standard deviation does not make the stage deterministic. It makes the budget incomplete. Queues belong in the stage that blocks. If the model waits on a queue, the queue's waiting time is part of the model's duration, or it is its own stage with its own mean and its own spread. It is not a footnote under a picture of a server. A queue you forgot to name is a random variable you forgot to add, and the sum is then a sum of the wrong list.

The lab will assume a shape for those variables, and this block's job is to stop you from assuming the shape before the names exist. Independence and a Gaussian tail come next. They are assumptions about the five durations, or about however many of the five you are actually defending. They are not assumptions about a product. If you cannot say which stage a millisecond belongs to, you cannot say whether two milliseconds are independent, and you cannot say whether a tail is a property of the sum or a property of one slow name. Naming is the whole method of this opening. The arithmetic is what you do after the names are stable.

Work a count, not a latency, because this block does not give you the lab's default times. There are five stage names. A budget that includes all five has five means to add and five variances to add, once independence is on the table. A budget that drops the model has four. The difference is not a small bookkeeping preference. Dropping a name sets that name's contribution to zero, which is a claim that the stage is absent, not a claim that it is fast. If the model is absent, say absent. If it is present and you hope it is fast, it is still present, and it still needs a mean and a spread. Count the names you would actually defend. The lab brief on the later contract lecture will ask you for five stage names again. The list in this opening is the list you are accountable to unless you replace it on purpose: ingest, feature, model, risk check, gateway.

Change one input in that list. Remove the risk check, one name, and leave the other four. You now have four means, not five, and you have made a real architectural claim: nothing in the path checks risk, or the check lives inside a stage you still named and you have failed to say which. Those are different claims. The first shortens the sum. The second does not. If the risk check was hiding inside the gateway and you only deleted the label, the gateway's mean and spread are still supposed to contain it, and your four-name budget is a relabeling, not a saving. The discipline is dull and it is the lecture. Write the stage down in the stage that blocks. Do not save a line by forgetting the name.

You might ask whether a tool can be faster than the stage it implements, in a way that makes the stage list the wrong object. The tool can change the distribution. It cannot remove the work from the sum if the work still happens. A faster gateway changes the gateway's mean and the gateway's spread. It does not delete the gateway line. Kubernetes, named in the opening, is a tool you might reach for to run a stage. It does not belong in the budget until the stage's distribution is real, meaning until you can say what that choice did to a mean and a spread you will defend. A picture of the tool with no distribution is not a latency. It is a drawing. Drawings do not add.

What the claim does not mean is that these five names are a law for every system you will build. They are the names this lecture gave you so the later sums have terms. A path with no model does not need a model line, and you should delete it rather than carry a zero you do not believe. A path with two gateways has two gateway lines, or one line whose distribution already adds them, and you must say which accounting you chose. It does not mean naming a stage makes its distribution Gaussian or independent. Those are the next block's assumptions, and they can fail after the names are right. It does not mean a wish becomes a budget if you attach a unit to it. A single number, even in milliseconds, is still a point estimate with no spread. It does not mean queues are free if they are short on a quiet afternoon. A queue belongs in the stage that blocks, including on the afternoon when it is short, because the short wait is a draw from a distribution you still have to own.

Here is a second list, so the five names do not fossilize. Suppose the work you actually do is a gateway read, a decision, and a gateway write, and the decision includes the feature and the model and the risk check because you refused to separate them. You may budget three stages. You must then put a mean and a spread on the decision that are wide enough to contain everything you buried inside it. You do not get to bury the model inside the decision and also describe the decision as a cheap branch. The opening rule is unchanged. Name the stages before the tools, and put each wait in the stage that blocks. Three honest names beat five names that hide a queue in a caption. Five honest names beat a single number called "the pipeline." The count is not the virtue. The match between the names and the waits is the virtue.

Take the five names and assign the queue to a stage out loud, because "the stage that blocks" is a rule you can violate by a pronoun. Suppose the feature computation waits on a store, and the wait is the long part. The queue belongs in the feature stage. It does not belong in ingest, unless ingest is the stage that actually blocks, and it does not belong in a caption under the model. If you later measure a feature mean, that mean includes the wait. If you measure a feature mean on a warm cache and put the cold wait nowhere, you have two distributions and you are budgeting the flattering one. The lecture does not give you the milliseconds of that wait. It gives you the slot the wait must occupy. A budget review that cannot point to the slot is not yet a review of a sum. It is a review of a drawing.

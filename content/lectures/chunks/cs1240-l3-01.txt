Put the same counter 30 back on a power of two so you can see the identity recover without changing the instruction, only the modulus. Capacity 16, mask 15, and 15 is 01111, a string of ones. 30 and-ed with 15 is 01110, which is 14. And 30 mod 16 equals 14, because 16 plus 14 is 30. One input changed, the capacity, from 12 to 16. The bitwise and went from a wrong slot to the right slot. That is the lab switch, read in reverse. Flip the capacity off the power of two and they diverge. Flip it back onto a power of two and they match.

The slide limits the identity, and you should say the limits before you get clever. It holds for positive i, and the zero counter is the harmless boundary you already checked, since 0 mod m is 0 and 0 and-ed with any mask is 0. Negative numbers are out of scope. Signed overflow is out of scope. If a counter is a signed integer and it passes its maximum, you are no longer in the arithmetic this identity describes. The remainder of a negative integer is a language-lawyer question this lecture refuses. The fix is not a more elaborate mask. The fix is to stay in the nonnegative range the statement names, or to use an unsigned counter whose wrap, if it ever happens, is a different protocol problem. Do not extend the formula with a story about two's complement that the notes did not claim.

A lucky hit is the trap in the bad modulus, so do not accept a single agreeing pair as a proof. Stay with capacity 12 and the mask 11, and test every remainder you can form with a counter that is still smaller than 12. For those counters, the true remainder is the counter itself. The mask agrees for 0, 1, 2, and 3, because those numbers only use bits the mask keeps. It agrees again for 8, 9, 10, and 11. It fails in the middle. 4 bitwise-and 11 is 0, not 4. 5 bitwise-and 11 is 1, not 5. 6 bitwise-and 11 is 2, not 6. 7 bitwise-and 11 is 3, not 7. If your test suite only published the counter 8, or only the counter 3, the mask would look correct on a capacity that is not a power of two. The identity claims every nonnegative i. Four quiet successes do not survive the counter 4, and they do not survive the slide's counter 19.

One more counter past a multiple, so the failure is not only the numbers below 12. Take i equal to 25 and m equal to 12. 25 bitwise-and 11: 25 is 11001 in bits, 11 is 01011, and the and is 01001, which is 9. Division: two times 12 is 24, so 25 mod 12 is 1. Nine and 1 are different slots again. You now have disagreements at 4, 5, 6, 7, 19, 25, and 30, and accidental agreements at several other counters. Agreement is not the pattern. Agreement is an accident of which bits happened to be zero.

You might ask whether a capacity like 12 could be rescued by a different mask, maybe something other than m minus 1. A different mask is still a bitwise and with a constant. Bitwise and with a constant keeps some bits and clears others. It cannot implement remainder by 12, because remainder by 12 is not a function of a fixed set of bits. You just saw 19 and 30 produce mask results 3 and 10 against true remainders 7 and 6. Those are not the low bits of anything that equals the quotient-remainder split for modulus 12. There are other cheap ways to compute a remainder by a constant, and this lecture does not develop them. The claim on the board is narrower. The identity you are allowed to use is the mask m minus 1, and only when m is a power of two.

Here is what the identity does not mean. It does not mean bitwise and is an approximate modulus that is close enough for a buffer. Off by one slot is a wrong cell, not a rounding error. Three versus 7 is a torn book, not a tolerance. It does not mean every power of two in the program is a capacity. The identity is about the modulus you divide by, which is the length of the ring. A power-of-two timestamp, or a power-of-two batch size, does not by itself license a mask on an unrelated index. It does not mean you should test the identity in production traffic. The lab flips the capacity so you can see the divergence in a controlled counter. A live ring that indexes the wrong slot has already mixed two generations of data. And it does not mean the failure is rare for non-powers of two. It failed for 19 and it failed for 30. The match for powers of two, on 0, 7, 8, 19, 30, and 100, is the special case, not the other way around.

The board in front of you is titled why the capstone cares. The SPSC ring publishes a sequence number per slot and indexes with head bitwise-and capacity minus 1. That is the identity from the previous board, written as an address calculation. The slot formula in the notes is slot equals head bitwise-and capacity minus 1. The static assert in the C++ file is this lecture, enforced by the compiler. A clever runtime check is already too late. If the capacity is wrong, you want the build to fail while the mask is still a lie you have not shipped, not a branch you discover after a bad index has been computed on the hot path.

Read the assert as a placement rule, not as a slogan. The slide says to write the assert next to the array, not in a comment. A comment does not constrain the type. An assert that the compiler evaluates, next to the array whose length is the modulus, ties the mask to the object it indexes. If someone later changes the capacity to 12, the identity becomes the torn book you already computed, 19 masked to 3 against a remainder of 7. A comment would survive that edit and become false quietly. A static assert fails the build. That is the sense in which the compiler is enforcing the lecture. A runtime check on the publishing path would be checking a fact that was supposed to be true before the process started, and it would be checking it on the nanosecond path the mask was introduced to protect. Too late means both too late to be safe and too late to be cheap.

The mask is only an address calculation. It is not the fullness test. That separation is the part students collapse, and the notes are blunt about it. Students sometimes store head modulo capacity. Then a full ring and an empty ring look identical. The Vyukov protocol keeps head and tail monotonic and stores the ticket on the cell. The counter only increases. It does not wrap in the protocol. The slot index wraps. The sequence number does not. Full and empty can share a slot index. The sequence tells them apart.

Do that with capacity 8, so the mask is 7 and the arithmetic stays in the table you already trust. Suppose head and tail are both 0. Both masked slots are 0 and-ed with 7, which is 0. The difference head minus tail is 0. The ring is empty. The cell at slot 0 may carry an old ticket or no live ticket, and the protocol's sequence says this lap has not published a new item there. Now advance the producer until head equals 8 and tail is still 0. The masked slot of head is 8 and-ed with 7, which is 0. The masked slot of tail is also 0. The slot indexes look like the empty picture. The difference head minus tail is 8, and 8 equals the capacity, so the ring is full. Every cell holds a published item the consumer has not taken. If you had stored only the wrapped head and the wrapped tail, both situations would show the pair 0, 0. You would not know whether to push or to refuse the push. The monotonic counters still say 8 and 0. Their difference is the occupancy. The mask was never asked.

Check the telescope on this prefix of eight. Actual costs are 1, 2, 3, 1, 5, 1, 1, and 1. They sum to 15. Amortized costs are 2 for the special row and then 3 seven times, which is 2 plus 21, which is 23. Starting potential 0, ending potential 8. Actual sum should equal amortized sum plus 0 minus 8, which is 23 minus 8, which is 15. It checks. The running average of actual cost is 15 divided by 8, which is 1.875, under the constant 3, with slack equal to the ending potential divided by the number of inserts.

Continue through the next doubling so the second growth is not a rumor. Insert 9 copies 8 and writes 1, actual cost 9. New capacity 16, new population 9, phi equal to 18 minus 16, which is 2. The potential falls from 8 to 2, a change of negative 6. Amortized cost is 9 minus 6, which is 3. Cumulative actual cost is 15 plus 9, which is 24. Cumulative amortized cost is 23 plus 3, which is 26. Ending potential 2. And 26 minus 2 equals 24. The slack has collapsed to 2 because you just spent the account. The running average of actual cost is 24 divided by 9, which is 2.666..., closer to 3 than the full-table average was.

The notes tell you to change the growth factor to 3 and expect a new constant, with the method left intact. Do that as a fresh ledger, not as a guess. Capacity becomes three times the old capacity when you grow. The potential that resets is no longer two n minus m. Use phi equal to three n minus m, all over 2. A quiet insert raises n by 1 and changes this phi by three halves. Actual cost 1, so the amortized cost is 1 plus three halves, which is five halves, or 2.5. A growth from a full table copies n and writes 1. Before the step, n equals m, so phi equals two n over 2, which is n. After the step the capacity is three n, using the old n, and the population is old n plus 1. Phi equals three times that population minus three n, all over 2, which is three halves. The change is three halves minus n. Amortized cost equals n plus 1 plus three halves minus n, which is five halves again. Same method, new constant.

Spot-check the tripling ledger on the rows that move. The empty first insert still costs 1 and ends at n equal to 1, m equal to 1, phi equal to three minus 1, over 2, which is 1. Amortized cost 1 plus 1, which is 2, special again. Insert 2 triples from capacity 1 to capacity 3, actual cost 2, and ends at n equal to 2, phi equal to 6 minus 3, over 2, which is 1.5. Amortized cost is 2 plus 0.5, which is 2.5. Insert 3 is quiet, from n equal to 2 to n equal to 3 inside capacity 3, and is billed 2.5. Insert 4 grows from 3 to 9, actual cost 4, and lands on phi equal to 1.5, billed 2.5. The constant really did change, from 3 to 2.5, and every ordinary row still matches every other ordinary row. If someone tells you the factor 2 in the original phi is sacred, this is the counterexample. It was sacred only for doubling.

You might ask why the quiet steps are asked to pay 3 rather than 2, since the write is the only work a quiet step performs. The extra 2 is the deposit, not a claim that the quiet step touched three cells. Over a long doubling run the deposits are what make the copy's n cancel. If each quiet step deposited only 1, the account just before growth would hold about n over 2 rather than n, the copy of n would not be covered, and the amortized cost of growth would still contain a term that grows with n. You would have a ledger, and you would not have a constant. The factor is chosen so the quiet steps pay exactly enough.

Here is what the constant 3 does not mean. It does not mean the actual cost is 3. You watched actual costs of 1, of 5, and of 9 on rows whose amortized cost was 3. It does not mean a different potential is illegal. Any potential that is on the order of n just before growth and on the order of 1 just after it can push the spike off the amortized column. Two n minus m is the choice that makes the quiet deposit and the growth withdrawal land on the same 3. It does not mean the first row is a counterexample to the proof. The empty allocation is outside the full-table arithmetic, its amortized cost came out to 2, and the inequality still held because we started from zero and stayed nonnegative. It does not mean tripling is free of spikes. Its ordinary amortized cost is 2.5, and its growth row still copies every live element. The constant changed. The spike did not become a quiet write.

The board in front of you is titled what the bound does not say. The bound says that if the potential starts at zero and stays nonnegative, the sum of the actual costs is at most the sum of the amortized costs. After the special first insert, each amortized cost on the doubling table is 3, so a run of n inserts is billed at most something like 3 n once you grant the first row its 2. That is a statement about a sum. It is not a statement about the next call, and the notes are explicit. Amortized order 1 does not mean every call is order 1.

Put the spike back on the table at a size where nobody can shrug. Suppose the doubling table is full with n equal to 1024 and m equal to 1024. Phi equals 1024. The next insert copies 1024 elements and writes one. Actual cost 1025. After the step the capacity is 2048 and the population is 1025. Phi equals two times 1025 minus 2048, which is 2050 minus 2048, which is 2. The change in potential is 2 minus 1024, which is negative 1022. Amortized cost is 1025 minus 1022, which is 3. The algebra is the same cancellation you already trust. The caller does not get to call the cost 3. The caller copies 1024 cells on this call. The 1022 of savings are a proof artifact that reconciles the sum. They do not shrink the copy.

The notes give the operational version of that gap. A trading gateway that copies a megabyte on the hot path once a second is still late. Amortized reasoning can truthfully say the average is fine and still leave you late once a second. If your listener cares about the late call, you use the potential to prove the average, and then you look at the tail separately. The tail here is not a mysterious distribution. It is the growth row, and you know exactly when it happens. It happens when n equals m.

Look at how the running average moves, because the lab draws it, and a careless reading of the drawing will confuse you. The brass line is the constant 3. The cream line is the average actual cost so far. They are on the same board because the potential was chosen so the amortized column is that constant. They do not sit on top of each other at a small n. After eight inserts the cream line reads 1.875. After nine, just after the growth to capacity 16, it reads 24 divided by 9, or 8 thirds, about 2.667. Inside the next epoch the quiet inserts pull the average down. After sixteen, with the table full again, the total actual cost is 31 and the average is 1.9375. The following insert, the growth to capacity 32, costs 17. Cumulative actual cost 48 over 17 inserts, which is about 2.824. The next full table, thirty-two inserts, has total cost 63 and average 1.96875. The insert after that costs 33, and the average becomes 96 divided by 33, which is about 2.909.

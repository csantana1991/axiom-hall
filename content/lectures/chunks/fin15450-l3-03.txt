Raising the financing rate raised the call. That is not a universal proverb about all options. It is this arithmetic. A put can move the other way. You do not have the put from the lab. If you add a put later and both the call and the put rise when r rises, parity is the test you failed.

Shorten the maturity as another single edit. S and K equal 100, r equal to 0.05, sigma equal to 0.2, T equal to 0.25. Half variance times T is 0.02 times 0.25, which is 0.005. r T is 0.0125. If you scale option prices by T rather than by the square root of T, you are using the wrong clock. The formula’s denominator and the gap d1 minus d2 both use the square root.

A very small volatility checks the deep intrinsic limit from the other side. Keep S and K at 100, r at 0.05, T at 1, and set sigma to 0.01. One half sigma squared is 0.00005, negligible next to r in a first look, but keep it. The numerator is about 0.05005. The denominator is 0.01. d1 is about 5.005. d2 is about 4.995. Both are many standard deviations above zero, so both Phis are 1 for any desk-calculator approximation you will actually use. The call collapses to S minus K e to the minus r T, which is about 4.877. The formula and the forward intrinsic value agree. There is almost no optionality left when the total volatility is 0.01 and the forward is not near a knife edge in a way that 0.01 cannot resolve. At our inputs the forward moneyness is not zero. Log of the forward over the strike is r T, which is 0.05, many times 0.01, so the call is effectively the forward contract. Compare that 4.877 with the twenty-percent call of about 10.451. The extra about 5.57 points was volatility, sitting in Phi of d1 and Phi of d2 being far from a pure step. Turning sigma back up is how you buy that difference back.

You might ask whether Phi is a special function you are supposed to derive in this lecture. The lecture calls it the standard normal cumulative and sends you to an approximation, a desk calculator. You are responsible for d1 and d2, for which input enters which slot, and for the arithmetic that turns Phi values into a price. You are not responsible for a series expansion that this lesson does not write down. If two calculators differ in the fifth digit of Phi of 0.35, they do not differ in the lesson. If one calculator changes the price when mu changes, it is not an approximation of this formula at all.

The column they might be reading is the penalized objective, and you should be able to separate it out loud. At lambda equals 10 the penalty term is 10 over 2 times the sum of the square of one sixth and the square of 0.15. The square of one sixth is one over 36, about 0.02778. The square of 0.15 is 0.0225. The sum is about 0.05028. Times 5 is about 0.2514. The penalized value is the bowl about 0.8819 plus about 0.2514, about 1.133. At the old minimizer the penalized value would be lambda over 2 times 1.16, which at lambda equals 10 is 5 times 1.16, which is 5.8, plus a bowl of 0, so 5.8. The new point wins on the penalized objective, 1.133 against 5.8, and loses on the original bowl, 0.882 against 0. Report both, or the win hides the loss. Risk is neither of those two numbers unless your scoring distribution happens to make it so, and nothing in the lab estimates that expectation. The sentence "that can lower risk" is permission to go compute a risk, not a receipt that you already lowered it.

You might ask whether a coordinate can hit exactly zero if the step is coarse, even though the fixed point does not. A finite number of gradient steps can land near zero, and a sloppy printout can round a small coordinate to zero. Neither event is the fixed point. Take lambda equals 2 and the exact point 0.5 and negative 0.3. A step that has not converged might pass through y equals negative 0.299 or y equals negative 0.301. Those are not zeros. Take a much larger lambda, 100, where y is about negative 0.0226. Printing one digit after the decimal would show 0.0 and would be a display choice, not a stationarity result. The equation y times the quantity 6 plus lambda equals negative 2.4 has the solution negative 2.4 over 106, and 106 times that solution is negative 2.4, not zero. If your logs show exact zeros in both coordinates for a moderate lambda, you are not looking at this penalty. You may be looking at a thresholding map, or at a bug, or at a format string. Those are different maps, and the fix is different.

Change one input in the closed form and recompute, so the two denominators stay distinct in your hands. Leave the x formula alone and imagine the y target inside the original bowl changed only in the constant the notes already isolate, the constant 2.4, which is 6 times 0.4. Suppose the constant in that stationarity equation were 1.2 instead of 2.4, which is the edit "the y center sat at negative 0.2 rather than negative 0.4," because 6 times 0.2 is 1.2. Then y equals negative 1.2 divided by the quantity 6 plus lambda, and x is still 2 divided by the quantity 2 plus lambda, because you did not touch the x bowl. At lambda equals 2, x is still 0.5, and y is negative 1.2 over 8, which is negative 0.15. The old unpenalized y for that edited bowl would have been negative 1.2 over 6, which is negative 0.2. The penalty moved y from negative 0.2 to negative 0.15. The original, unedited lesson has negative 0.4 moving to negative 0.3 at the same lambda. Same penalty, different unpenalized center, different landing. You may not copy the landing from one bowl onto another. The derivation is short enough that copying is unnecessary. Set the gradient to zero again.

What the claim does not mean, for the whole lesson, is the last distinction to keep. It does not mean the quadratic penalty and an L1 penalty are two implementations of one shrink. One of them leaves a smooth fixed point at 2 over the quantity 2 plus lambda and negative 2.4 over the quantity 6 plus lambda. The other can place a coordinate at exactly zero and is not the update this lab runs. It does not mean bias is a defect you should always drive back to zero by taking lambda to 0. Lambda equals 0 is available, and it returns the unpenalized minimizer, and the lesson's point is that any other lambda is a chosen target, not a numerical convenience wrapped around the same target. It does not mean the step-size story from the unpenalized bowl transfers without editing. The stiff curvature becomes 6 plus lambda, and the safe step drops from one third at lambda equals 0 to one quarter at lambda equals 2, and to 2 over 16, which is one eighth, at lambda equals 10. A step you liked before the penalty can be an unstable step after it. It does not mean a path that fails to match the fixed point has a unique cause. If the late iterates are still moving, you may simply not have arrived. If they have settled somewhere else, the penalty may be missing from the gradient, or the step may be past the new ceiling and only looks settled because you stopped looking. Those are different bugs. Match the last iterate to the fixed point before you leave the board. If it matches, you minimized the objective you wrote down. You did not, by that match, minimize the original bowl, and you did not, by that match, produce an exact zero.

Here is what the ray does not mean. It does not mean every point on it is available in the market you trade. Borrowing at r sub f is an assumption you have to say out loud, and if the rate is a fiction the points beyond tangency are a fiction. It does not mean a high slope is a statement about drawdowns, gaps, or a client who cannot survive a path. The denominator is standard deviation in the two-moment model, nothing else. It does not mean the risky mix changes when you add cash. Half cash and half tangency kept the weights 27 over 79 and 52 over 79 inside the risky half. Cash sat beside them. It does not mean the even split's Sharpe ratio of about 0.457 was a failure. It was a lower slope than about 0.470. On a flat board those can look close. The geometry still picks the steeper line, and the next board shows where that line touches.

The board in front of you is titled one fund, if you believe the inputs. Every mean-variance investor who agrees on mu, capital sigma, and r sub f holds the same tangency portfolio and differs only in leverage. That is the two-fund separation theorem. One fund is the tangency portfolio of risky assets. The other fund is the risk-free asset. Disagreement about mu is the whole industry. If your mean vector is not mine, separation across the two of us is already gone, even when we share a covariance matrix and a risk-free rate.

The closed form is the proportionality you should derive once and then check. The risky weights are proportional to capital sigma inverse times the quantity mu minus r sub f times the vector of ones. You then normalize so the risky weights sum to 1. For two assets the lab can also evaluate the Sharpe ratio on a fine grid of w, including modest shorts. For two assets the grid is the method, not an approximation you need to apologize for. The closed form is the check against that grid when capital sigma is well conditioned.

Do the inverse in the worked pair, with correlation 0.20, so the matrix is comfortable. Capital sigma is the matrix with 0.04 and 0.01 on the first row and 0.01 and 0.0625 on the second. The determinant is 0.04 times 0.0625 minus 0.01 squared, which is 0.0025 minus 0.0001, which is 0.0024. The inverse has diagonal entries 0.0625 divided by 0.0024 and 0.04 divided by 0.0024, which are about 26.0417 and 16.6667, and off-diagonal entries negative 0.01 divided by 0.0024, about negative 4.1667. With r sub f equal to 0.03 the excess means are 0.05 and 0.11. Multiply the inverse by that excess vector. The first raw weight is about 26.0417 times 0.05 plus negative 4.1667 times 0.11, which is 1.30208 minus 0.45833, which is 0.84375. The second raw weight is about negative 4.1667 times 0.05 plus 16.6667 times 0.11, which is negative 0.20833 plus 1.83333, which is 1.625. The raw weights sum to 2.46875. Divide. The normalized weight on the first asset is 0.84375 divided by 2.46875, which is 27 over 79, about 0.3418. The weight on the second is 1.625 divided by 2.46875, which is 52 over 79, about 0.6582. Those are the holdings inside the risky fund. They are not yet a statement about how much of your wealth sits in the fund.

Score it. The mean is 27 over 79 times 0.08 plus 52 over 79 times 0.14, which to four decimals is 0.1195. The variance is about 0.03625, and the standard deviation is about 0.1904. Excess over 0.03 is about 0.0895. Sharpe ratio about 0.4700. Set that next to the three candidates from the previous board. Asset 1 scored 0.25. Asset 2 scored 0.44. The even split scored about 0.457. The tangency fund scores about 0.470. A grid with steps of one hundredth in the weight, including a modest range of shorts, finds its best slope at a weight near 0.34, with a Sharpe ratio that matches 0.470 to the digits you can hear. The grid and the inverse are the same answer on a well conditioned two-asset matrix. You do not apologize for the grid. You use it to see the closed form land.

Separation is the scale, and risk aversion chooses the scale, including a short of the fund if someone wants the ray on the other side of cash. Take the tangency excess of about 0.0895 and the tangency variance of about 0.03625. An investor who maximizes mean minus a times one half variance, with all risk coming from a scale y on this single fund, chooses y equal to the excess divided by a times the variance. At a equal to 4, y is about 0.0895 divided by 0.145, about 0.617. That investor holds about 62 percent in the tangency fund and the rest in cash. The risky weights inside the fund stay 27 over 79 and 52 over 79. At a equal to 2, y is about 1.234. That investor borrows. About 123 percent of capital goes into the same fund, and the extra is financed at 0.03, if that rate is real. At a equal to 8, y is about 0.309. More cash, same fund. Three aversions, one risky recipe. That is the theorem, and the hypothesis is sitting in the first sentence: they agree on mu, on capital sigma, and on r sub f.

Change mu and the fund moves. Keep everything else, and lower the second mean from 0.14 to 0.10. The excess vector becomes 0.05 and 0.07. The same inverse produces raw weights about 1.010 and 0.958, which normalize to about 0.513 and 0.487. The tangency fund is now nearly an even split, and its Sharpe ratio falls to about 0.343. Raise the second mean instead, from 0.14 to 0.20. The normalized weights become about 0.184 and 0.816, and the Sharpe ratio rises to about 0.690. One input, the second mean, moved the risky mix from about 66 percent in asset 2, to about 49 percent, to about 82 percent. Investors who disagree about that mean do not hold one fund. They hold three different funds in this calculation. Disagreement about mu is not a footnote to separation. It is the reason two desks do not share the recipe. The theorem never promised they would, once the means differ.

Change the risk-free rate instead of the mean, so you can see a second input with a cleaner story. At r sub f equal to 0.05, with the original means 0.08 and 0.14, the excesses are 0.03 and 0.09. Raw weights come out 0.40625 and 1.375, which normalize to 13 over 57 and 44 over 57, about 0.228 and 0.772. The fund tilts harder toward the higher-mean asset because the hurdle rose and the first asset's excess shrank more, in relative terms, against that hurdle. The Sharpe ratio, now measured over 0.05, is about 0.369. Same covariance, different cash rate, different tangency. Separation still holds inside a room that agrees on the new rate. It does not hold between a room using 0.03 and a room using 0.05.

Constraints break separation. A long-only rule, a leverage cap, a ban on borrowing at r sub f, or a ban on shorting the fund, cuts the ray. Two investors with different risk aversion can then be pressed against different constraints, and the risky mix itself can change. You are back to a quadratic program. The closed form proportionality assumed the only restriction on risky weights was that they sum to 1 after scaling, with cash or borrowing absorbing the rest. Take the a equal to 2 investor who wanted y about 1.23. If borrowing is forbidden, that investor cannot sit at 1.23. The optimum under the cap is not "the same fund, smaller scale" in every constrained problem. Sometimes the boundary wants a different blend of the two risky assets than the unconstrained tangency. Do not quote separation in a mandate that has already broken it.

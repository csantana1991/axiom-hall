You might ask which policy you should ship. The lecture's answer is narrower than a shipping decision. Read the toy objective. If the parameter is a tiny kappa, the sinh schedule is almost linear, and linear's advantage is the temporary term. If the parameter is a large kappa, the sinh schedule sells earlier, and it is buying down the variance proxy at a temporary cost that approaches the dump's cost only in the limit. Dump-now is still available as a policy on the board, and it can win the toy when lambda is large relative to eta, as the two-step threshold showed. Shipping any of the three as a market schedule would require a calibrated eta and a variance you are willing to defend, and the notes tell you not to fit eta to a fantasy and quote J as dollars. Inside the lab, the honest sentence is: the sinh curve is the compromise the parameter asked for, and the winner of the toy flips with that parameter.

What the claim does not mean is that the flip is a market fact. It is a comparison of three numbers computed from one toy. It does not mean the closed form is being re-solved by an optimizer in the lab. The lab uses the closed form, not a numerical optimal control. It does not mean permanent impact is hiding inside eta. Eta is the temporary-impact coefficient in the square root and in the temporary score. Permanent impact changes the cash account and is outside this score. It does not mean a doubled sigma and a doubled square root of lambda are interchangeable in every formula just because both can double kappa. Doubling sigma quadruples sigma squared. To double kappa by touching only lambda, you multiply lambda by four, because lambda sits to the first power inside the square root. The two edits match on kappa and do not match on the separate sigma squared that still multiplies the variance score. If you edit sigma, you bend the path and you rescale the variance term. If you edit lambda, you bend the path through kappa and you rescale the weight in front of the variance term. Write down which input you changed before you say the totals moved for the same reason.

Work that sigma edit on the two-step grid as its own case, so the warning has arithmetic. Start from the totals already written. Multiply sigma by two. Sigma squared multiplies by four. Kappa doubles, same as when lambda was multiplied by four. On the toy, the variance score has sigma squared in it, so both variance scores multiply by four, and lambda is unchanged. Dump's total becomes eta X squared plus lambda times four sigma squared delta t X squared. Linear's total becomes eta X squared over two plus lambda times four sigma squared delta t times five fourths X squared. Compare that with the earlier edit that multiplied lambda by four and left sigma alone. The variance pieces of J land in the same place, because four times lambda times sigma squared equals lambda times four sigma squared. On this particular toy, and on this grid, the two edits happen to scale the same term. The path formula is where they would come apart if permanent impact or another sigma, not inside this J, were on the page. This J only sees sigma through sigma squared in the variance score, and kappa sees lambda sigma squared over eta. Inside the toy as written, quadrupling lambda and quadrupling sigma squared are the same scale on J and the same scale on kappa. The lecture still tells you to name the input. The algebra then tells you, on this score, that those two particular edits coincide. That coincidence is not a reason to stop naming them. It is a reason to check.

One further check, on the sum of children, so a front-loaded path cannot claim a free temporary score. Any schedule that starts at X and ends at zero has child orders that add up to X. The sum of the children is fixed. The sum of the squares is not. For a fixed sum, the sum of squares is smallest when the mass is spread out, and largest when the mass is concentrated in one child. Dump puts the whole sum in one square and pays eta X squared. An equal split across n children pays eta times n times the square of X over n, which is eta X squared over n, and that falls as n grows. The sinh schedule, at small kappa, is near the equal split and pays a temporary score near that lower number. At large kappa it concentrates early and the sum of squares rises. There is no schedule, on this objective, that cuts the variance proxy all the way to the dump's variance proxy while keeping the equal-split temporary score. The square is what makes the combination costly. J is the sum of a term that hates concentration and a term that hates leftover inventory. The sinh trajectory is the closed-form compromise between those two hatreds, for the kappa the square root defined. The lab asks you to move kappa and watch which hatred the toy is charging more for. That is the exercise. A single winner, detached from kappa, is not in the notes.

Recompute the square root on a one-step change that is not a pure doubling, so the arithmetic is not only powers of four. Suppose lambda, sigma, and eta are such that the fraction lambda sigma squared over eta equals one. Then kappa equals the square root of one, which is one. This is a normalization, not a measured risk aversion. It makes the increments easy. Now multiply lambda by nine, one changed input. The fraction becomes nine. Kappa becomes three. The path front-loads because three is larger than one, and the midpoint inventory is X divided by two times the hyperbolic cosine of three T over two, which is smaller than X divided by two times the hyperbolic cosine of T over two. You still do not evaluate the cosine. You know the direction. On the toy, multiplying lambda by nine multiplies every variance contribution to J by nine and leaves every temporary contribution alone. Dump's relative appeal increases by that factor of nine on the variance gap. Linear's temporary advantage is unchanged in eta units and is nine times less important relative to the variance gap. A flip that did not occur at kappa one can occur at kappa three. Again the winner is a statement about J after the edit, not a statement about the hyperbolic sine being three.

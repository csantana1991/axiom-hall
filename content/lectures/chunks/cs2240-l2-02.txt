What the bound does not mean is that every query on the five-by-twenty-eight grid is inside the slack. The probability is at least one minus delta, here ninety-nine hundredths. There is a budgeted chance, one hundredth, that a query is worse than truth plus epsilon times the length. A single bad query is not a refutation. A systematic miss, on a grid that is not the grid the formula sized, is not a refutation either. It is a different object. The two-by-four lab, eight counters, was never five by twenty-eight. Pointing at a collision in the lab and announcing that epsilon has failed is a category error. The lab did not claim epsilon. The notes say the grid is too small to cash the bound, and the slides say the lab grid is a picture, not a calibrated pair. Quote the overestimate as one-sided. Do not quote it as one tenth.

The approximation written with a tilde, width about e over epsilon and depth about the natural log of one over delta, is the same sizing with the ceilings left implicit. You use the ceilings when you allocate, because you cannot allocate a fraction of a column. Twenty-seven point one eight columns is not a width. Twenty-eight is. Four point six rows is not a depth. Five is. The tilde form is how you remember which parameter serves which Greek letter. The ceiling form is how you build the array. Forgetting the ceiling and allocating twenty-seven because you truncated will not satisfy the hypothesis the bound used. The theorem’s width is a ceiling, not a floor.

Walk a short tape through the same parameters so the additive term is not always one hundred. Epsilon one tenth, delta one hundredth, width twenty-eight, depth five. Let the tape be the lab’s opening, the same symbol five times, and nothing else. The length is five. Epsilon times the length is one half. The bound says the estimate is at most the truth plus one half, with probability at least ninety-nine hundredths. The truth is five. Truth plus one half is five and a half. Counts here are integers, and the sketch’s cells are integers, so an integer estimate at most five and a half is an estimate of at most five. Combined with the one-sided floor, the estimate is exactly five on a good draw. That is a strong conclusion, and it depends on the tape being length five, not on the lab’s tiny width. A five-by-twenty-eight sketch can cash that statement. A two-by-four picture cannot, even if on this particular five it happens to print five. Happening to print five is the audit. The half-unit slack is the bound. Do not transfer the bound onto the picture because both returned five.

If the five arrivals are followed by a mix and the length becomes, say, five hundred, epsilon one tenth times five hundred is fifty. A symbol whose truth is five is then bounded by fifty-five on a good draw, not by five and a half. The same sketch, the same epsilon, a longer tape, a larger additive term. People hear “epsilon equals one tenth” and think ten percent of the point frequency. That is not what was stated. The slack is epsilon times the ell-one norm of the whole stream, not epsilon times the truth of the symbol. Five plus one tenth of five would be five and a half even on the long tape. The theorem does not say that. It says five plus one tenth of the length. On a length of five hundred, that is fifty, and the cap is fifty-five. Relative to a rare symbol, the additive guarantee can be many times the truth. That is why heavy hitters, symbols that are a real fraction of the length, are the queries where an additive epsilon times the length looks like a relative error you might tolerate. A rare symbol can be drowned by a legal error term. The estimate remains at least the truth. It may sit far above it and still satisfy the bound. If your product needs rare-symbol precision, this sizing is the wrong instrument, or this epsilon is too coarse for the length you have.

You might also ask whether the natural logarithm can be swapped for a logarithm in another base if you adjust the constant. The formula as the lecture writes it uses the natural logarithm, paired with e in the width. e and the natural log are a matched pair in the analysis. Replacing the natural log with a base-ten log, and leaving the depth as the ceiling of that smaller number, shrinks the depth and drops the hypothesis. Log base ten of one hundred is two, and the ceiling of two is two, against the five you got from the natural log. Two rows are not the depth this delta asked for under the stated formula. If a derivation elsewhere uses a different base, it carries a different constant in front. You do not mix the e from the width with an unmatched log in the depth. The lecture’s pair is width ceiling of e over epsilon, depth ceiling of the natural log of one over delta. Speak that pair, or speak the tilde form, and do not improvise a third.

A negative update still destroys the floor, and the sized bound does not resurrect it. The probability statement is an upper bound, truth plus the slack, and it sits on top of an estimator that is supposed to be at least the truth. If a decrement drives a cell below the truth, you can violate the floor on every draw, which is a failure mode outside the one-hundredth you budgeted. Budgeted failure is an unlucky hash. An unbudgeted failure is an update the proof excluded. The notes are direct: the moment you allow decrements, a cell can fall below the truth and the argument is gone. Width twenty-eight and depth five do not grant an exception.

Say the sized example once, cleanly, as the thing you would write next to a real allocation. Epsilon one tenth, delta one hundredth, width twenty-eight, depth five, one hundred forty counters. On a stream of one thousand unit increments, the estimate is at most the true point frequency plus one hundred, with probability at least ninety-nine hundredths, and at least the true frequency on every draw if you never decremented. Change epsilon to one twentieth and the width becomes fifty-five, the additive term on that stream becomes fifty, and the depth stays five. Change delta to one twentieth instead and the depth becomes three while the width stays twenty-eight. The two-by-four board with a truth of five and a collided cell of eight shows the minimum and the floor. It does not show one tenth, and you do not say that it does.

Hashes are part of the algorithm. What this lab actually hashes with is a tiny linear map, not a family built for cryptography. Collisions are the point. A hash that never collides on eight keys is a lookup table in a costume. You should be able to run one of those tiny maps by hand, see which keys share a bucket, and predict the minimum before you look at the board. If you cannot, the grid is a picture of boxes and the hash is a rumor.

There is a direct link to the microprice, and it falls out of the gap you already derived. The gap between microprice and mid equals the spread times the bid size minus the ask size, divided by twice the sum of the sizes. The imbalance is that same difference of sizes, divided by the sum, without the two. So the gap equals the spread times the imbalance, divided by two. In words, the microprice equals the mid plus half the spread times the imbalance. Check it on the numbers you have. Imbalance one third, half of that is one sixth, times the spread is the spread over six, which matched the microprice when the sizes were two and one. Imbalance one half, half of that is one quarter, times the spread is the spread over four, which matched the sizes three and one. Imbalance zero gives microprice equal to the mid. Imbalance negative one third puts the microprice a sixth of the spread below the mid. You do not need a new derivation for each book. You need the mid, the spread, and this ratio. That also tells you the limit. As imbalance approaches one, the microprice approaches the mid plus half the spread, which is the ask. As imbalance approaches negative one, the microprice approaches the bid. The thin side pulls, and a missing side would pull all the way to the price that disappeared, which is another reason not to evaluate the model on a one-sided touch.

You might ask whether you can add the size at the next price into the ratio and still call it top-of-book imbalance. You cannot. The moment the next price's size enters the numerator or the denominator, you are computing a different feature. The lecture calls depth a different feature. Top-of-book imbalance ignores everything behind the touch. If the deep book is large on the ask and the touch is large on the bid, the touch ratio is positive and the depth may say something else. Using the touch ratio as if it were the whole book is how the deep book makes a fool of the touch. You may build a depth feature. You may not rename it to this one. Say which one you used.

What the claim does not mean is a set of trading conclusions the ratio does not support. A positive imbalance does not mean the price will rise. The lecture never said that. It said positive means more size on the bid, at the touch, now. It does not mean the bid is informed. Nothing in the ratio is a label for information. It does not mean the book is busy or quiet. The common-scale check showed a busier touch with the same ratio. It does not mean the microprice has become a forecast you should size. The link between them is algebraic. Half the spread times the imbalance shifts the mid. That shift is still the one-line model, with the same prohibition. It does not mean a value near one is a stronger signal in the sense of a tested prediction. It means one side of the touch is almost gone. It does not mean two books with the same imbalance have the same sizes. They have the same ratio. The sizes themselves can differ by any common scale, and the spread can differ too. The ratio throws that scale away on purpose.

Work a join as a size change that is not a common scale, so the feature moves for a reason you chose. Start from bid size two and ask size one, imbalance one third. Join the bid with two more units. Join adds size at the current best, so the bid size becomes four and the ask size stays one. You just computed that ratio: three fifths. The spread did not change, because join does not move the price. The mid did not change. The imbalance did, from one third to three fifths. The microprice, by the link, moves from a sixth of the spread above the mid to three tenths of the spread above the mid, because half of three fifths is three tenths. On a one-tick book that is a move from about seventeen hundredths of a cent above the mid to three tenths of a cent above the mid. No take occurred. No news was required. You added resting size on the bid, and both features moved. If a log of the mid is your only log, this join is invisible. The lecture's state is not the mid alone.

Change the join to the other side, one changed input. From the same start, sizes two and one, join the ask with two units instead. The ask size becomes three. The bid size stays two. Imbalance is two minus three, over two plus three, which is negative one fifth. The sign flipped. You began with more size on the bid and, by adding two to the ask, finished with more size on the ask. The microprice crosses from above the mid to below it: half of negative one fifth is negative one tenth, so the microprice sits a tenth of the spread below the mid. On the one-tick book that is a tenth of a cent below the mid. The same verb, join, on the other side, moved the ratio from positive one third to negative one fifth. That is a large move relative to the interval from negative one to one, and it came from posting two units, not from a trade. Posting is part of the state. A feature that claims to describe the touch has to notice it.

The idea in this block is that the lab stores cents as integers. Floating point is a bad representation of a tick. If your production book uses doubles for price identity, you will eventually fail to match an order that is equal. Display is not identity. Integer cents are the identity in this lab. Dollars are how those integers are shown.

Separate the two representations before you do arithmetic. The identity of a price is the integer count of cents. Two prices are the same price when those integers are equal, and not otherwise. The display takes that integer and divides by one hundred, and shows a dollar amount. One hundred cents display as one dollar. One cent displays as one hundredth of a dollar. Two cents display as two hundredths of a dollar. The display can look like a decimal. The thing you compare, cancel, and hash is the integer. A match on the display string is a match only insofar as the string is a faithful picture of the integer. The lab's choice is to keep the integer as the thing itself.

Work the conversions that the later shortfall will assume you can do without a calculator, using the one-tick and two-tick books. A spread of one cent is an integer, and it displays as zero point zero one dollars. A spread of two cents displays as zero point zero two dollars. The mid of the two-tick book was an integer cent, B plus one, and it displays by dividing that integer by one hundred. The mid of the one-tick book was B plus one half cents, which is not an integer. You can still display it, as the dollar picture of B cents plus half a cent. You should not store that half cent back into the book as a price identity. The book does not have a tick there. The summary is allowed to be a half. The order is not. If you round the mid to a cent and then use the rounded cent as a price key, you have created an identity the average did not have. On the one-tick book, rounding half a cent is exactly the choice between the bid and the ask, and those are different orders.

The midpoint is the cleanest place to see front-loading, and it uses a double-angle identity you can say in words. The hyperbolic sine of two u equals two times the hyperbolic sine of u times the hyperbolic cosine of u. Set u equal to kappa times T over two, so two u is kappa T. Then the hyperbolic sine of kappa T equals two times the hyperbolic sine of kappa T over two, times the hyperbolic cosine of kappa T over two. Remaining shares at the midpoint equal X times the hyperbolic sine of kappa T over two, divided by that product. One hyperbolic sine cancels, and you are left with X divided by two times the hyperbolic cosine of kappa T over two. The hyperbolic cosine of zero is one, and for any positive argument the hyperbolic cosine is strictly greater than one. So, whenever kappa is positive, remaining shares at the midpoint are strictly less than X over two. Halfway through the clock you have already sold more than half the block. That is front-loading. It is not a picture you have to take on faith. It is the identity.

The linear schedule is the limit, not a plug-in of kappa equals zero. If you set kappa to zero inside the ratio, you divide by the hyperbolic sine of zero, which is zero. The expression as written does not accept that plug-in. What the lecture asserts is the limit as kappa approaches zero. For small positive u, the hyperbolic sine of u is near u itself. Apply that to the numerator and the denominator. The ratio of hyperbolic sines approaches open time divided by T. Remaining shares approach X times open time over T, which is a straight line from X down to zero. At the midpoint that line holds X over two. The same midpoint formula agrees, because the hyperbolic cosine of a tiny number approaches one, and X divided by two times one is X over two. Small kappa is almost that line. The lab's language is "almost linear," and the limit is why. You never divide by zero to get there.

Large kappa does the opposite, and the midpoint formula is enough to see it. As kappa grows, kappa T over two grows, and the hyperbolic cosine of that argument grows without a finite ceiling. X divided by a larger and larger number goes to zero. Remaining shares at the midpoint become a small fraction of the block. Most of the shares are already sold in the first half of the horizon. You sell earlier. You do not, at any finite kappa, sell the entire block in the first instant. The trajectory is still a smooth curve from X down to zero. Dump-now is the extreme policy that puts the whole child at the first step. The sinh curve approaches that impatience only as kappa grows. At a moderate kappa it is strictly between the straight line and the dump. That betweenness is the compromise the next block will score.

Change one input: increase kappa, and hold X and T fixed. In the midpoint formula the only place kappa appears is inside the hyperbolic cosine in the denominator. A larger kappa makes that cosine larger and makes remaining shares at the midpoint smaller. You can say the direction without a table of hyperbolic cosines. You cannot say a percentage sold unless you pick a number for kappa T, and this lesson does not give you one. Do not invent a kappa, run a calculator, and then call the percentage a property of the lab. The lab's slider moves kappa from near zero to large, and the remaining-inventory curve goes from a straight line to a front-loaded drop. Your hand check is the midpoint identity, plus the two limits. If a curve on the screen is above X over two at the midpoint, it is not this formula at a positive kappa. If it is exactly the straight line, you are looking at the small-kappa limit, not at a large kappa.

You might ask whether the linear schedule is the sinh schedule with kappa equal to zero. Not as a substitution. The denominator would vanish. Linear is the limit of the sinh schedule as kappa approaches zero, and a small positive kappa is the lab's "almost linear" case. The distinction matters the moment you code the closed form. A direct evaluation at zero will fail or return a not-a-number. A stable evaluation uses the limit, or it uses a series for the ratio of hyperbolic sines when kappa T is tiny. This lecture does not give you that series beyond the first-order fact that hyperbolic sine of u is near u. You have that fact, and it is enough to see the straight line. It is not enough to claim a second-order error term you have not derived.

What the claim does not mean is that every liquidation you will ever run has this shape. The shape is the mean-variance trajectory when permanent impact and temporary impact are linear, which is the setting of the block. A different impact function produces a different trajectory, and this lab does not solve that control problem numerically. It uses this closed form. Front-loaded does not mean dump-now. You still hold X divided by two times a hyperbolic cosine at the midpoint, and that quantity is positive at every finite kappa. It does not mean the hyperbolic sine is a mystical preference for early selling. It is the solution of the tradeoff, and the midpoint identity is the part you can see without the calculus of variations. It does not mean kappa is a free aesthetic slider disconnected from the objective. The next block ties kappa to lambda, sigma, and eta. Moving it moves the compromise the parameter asked for. It does not mean time is continuous in the lab's score. The score sums over children and over inventory marks. The closed form gives you the continuous trajectory those sums are taken from. If your grid is coarse, the children are coarse, and the toy J is a coarse reading of a smooth curve.

Work the first half against the second half with no derivative, so the selling speeds are totals you can compare. From time zero to the midpoint you sell X minus the midpoint inventory. That quantity is X minus X over two times the hyperbolic cosine of kappa T over two. Because the cosine is greater than one, this is more than X minus X over two, which is more than half the block. From the midpoint to the horizon you sell whatever remains, and that remainder is less than half the block. The two halves of the clock do not sell the same number of shares. The first half sells more. The linear limit sells exactly half in each half of the clock. The sign of the departure is the sign of the cosine minus one, which is positive whenever kappa is positive. If you remember nothing else from the identity, remember that inequality. Halfway through a positive-kappa schedule, less than half the shares remain.

The idea in this opening block is the exchange between trading fast and trading slow. Trading fast costs spread and impact. Trading slow costs variance. You must sell a block before a horizon. Temporary impact punishes large child orders. Price variance punishes inventory you still hold. A risk aversion, call it lambda, sets the exchange rate between dollars of impact and variance of the cash account.

Name the state before the objective. Let x of t be the shares remaining at time t. You start with a block, call it X, so x at the start is X. You choose the decrements. A child order is the drop in remaining shares from one grid time to the next. In words, the child order at a step is the remaining shares at that step minus the remaining shares at the next step. The horizon T is finite. When the horizon arrives, a liquidation schedule is supposed to have nothing left. This is not a stationary market-making problem. You are not posting a spread forever. You are selling a block by a deadline. A strategy that still holds a large piece at the deadline has not finished the job the problem stated.

The lab scores three policies on a toy objective. The temporary score equals eta times the sum of squared child orders. The variance score equals sigma squared, times the step delta t, times the sum of squared remaining inventory. The total equals the temporary score plus lambda times the variance score. In one line, J equals eta times the sum of squared child orders, plus lambda times sigma squared times delta t times the sum of squared remaining shares. Read the squares. A child order twice as large costs four times as much temporary score, not twice as much. Inventory twice as large costs four times as much in the variance proxy. Lambda is the knob that says how many units of variance proxy you will trade for one unit of temporary score. Permanent impact is not inside this J. The notes say permanent impact changes the cash account, and that the lab's score is the temporary-plus-variance toy. If you quote J as dollars of implementation shortfall, you have left the toy and you have not been given a calibration.

Put the toy on a two-step grid so the squares have an integer count. This grid is a reading of the formula, chosen so you can add the terms by hand. It is not a claim that the lab's slider uses two steps, and it is not a calibrated market. Let the horizon be two steps of width delta t. Mark remaining inventory at the start of each step. The linear policy sells at a constant pace. Remaining shares: X, then X over two, then zero. The two child orders are each X over two. Square them: each square is X squared over four. The sum of squares is X squared over two. The temporary score is eta times X squared over two. The variance proxy sums the squared inventory you still hold at the starts of the steps, X squared plus X squared over four, which is five fourths X squared. The variance score is sigma squared times delta t times five fourths X squared. The total is eta X squared over two, plus lambda sigma squared delta t times five fourths X squared.

Dump-now on the same grid sells the whole block in the first child and nothing in the second. Child orders: X, and zero. The sum of squares is X squared. The temporary score is eta X squared. That is twice the linear temporary score, because eta X squared divided by eta X squared over two equals two. Inventory at the step starts: X, then zero. The sum of squares is X squared, which is smaller than five fourths X squared. The variance score is sigma squared times delta t times X squared. Compare the two variance scores. Linear minus dump equals sigma squared delta t times one fourth X squared. Dump wins the variance term. Linear wins the temporary term. That is the sentence on the slide, checked on a two-step reading: dump-now wins variance and loses impact. Linear does the opposite.

The totals can be subtracted. Dump's total minus linear's total equals eta X squared minus eta X squared over two, plus lambda sigma squared delta t times X squared, minus lambda sigma squared delta t times five fourths X squared. The temporary piece of that difference is eta X squared over two. The variance piece is negative one fourth lambda sigma squared delta t X squared. Factor X squared over four. The difference equals X squared over four, times the quantity two eta minus lambda sigma squared delta t. When two eta is larger than lambda sigma squared delta t, dump has the higher total, and the toy prefers linear. When lambda is large enough that lambda sigma squared delta t exceeds two eta, the difference flips, and the toy prefers dump. Lambda is the exchange rate in that inequality. Nothing here required a numerical value for eta, sigma, or lambda. The comparison is a threshold, not a universal winner.

Change one input. Multiply eta by four and leave lambda, sigma, and delta t alone. Every temporary score scales by four, so the temporary gap, which was eta X squared over two, becomes four eta times X squared over two, which is two eta X squared. The variance scores do not move, because eta is not in them. The difference of totals, dump minus linear, becomes X squared over four times the quantity eight eta minus lambda sigma squared delta t. The threshold moved. You now need a larger lambda, four times larger, before dump's variance advantage pays for the temporary gap, because the temporary gap itself grew by four. That matches the later formula for kappa, which shrinks when eta grows. A more expensive temporary impact pushes the compromise toward the slower schedule. You have not yet used kappa. You have only seen the toy do the same qualitative thing on two policies.

You might ask why you do not simply dump whenever you hate variance. Because dump wins only the variance term. It loses the temporary term, and the temporary term is eta times a square, which is harsh on a single child of size X. Hating variance means a large lambda, and a large lambda can push the inequality across the threshold so that dump's total is smaller. It does not erase eta. If eta is large, the threshold is far away and the same hatred of variance is not enough. The question "which policy wins" is incomplete until lambda, eta, sigma, and the step are in the same inequality. On this grid the inequality was two eta against lambda sigma squared delta t. A different grid would produce a different threshold. The shape of the answer would not change. Dump spends impact to buy a smaller variance proxy. Linear does the reverse. Lambda prices the purchase.

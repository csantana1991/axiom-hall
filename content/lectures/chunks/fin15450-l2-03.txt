The other pieces of the drift are short. The time partial is 0. Mu S times the first partial is mu S times 1 over S, which is mu. The S cancels there too. So the full drift coefficient of the log is mu plus the correction negative one half sigma squared. The diffusion coefficient is sigma S times the first partial 1 over S, which is sigma. Therefore d log S equals the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times dt, plus sigma dW. At mu equal to 0.1 and sigma equal to 0.2, the drift of the log is 0.1 minus 0.02, which is 0.08. Ordinary calculus would have said the drift of the log is mu, which is 0.1, because it would divide dS by S and forget the curvature. The difference is 0.02, and 0.02 is one half sigma squared. Over a year that is a shift of 0.02 in the log, which is not a cosmetic change in the exponent.

Integrate the differential. The right-hand side has constant coefficients if mu and sigma are constant. From time 0 to time t, the log of S at t minus the log of S at 0 equals the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, plus sigma times W at t, using W at 0 equal to 0. Exponentiate. S at t equals S at 0 times the exponential of the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, plus sigma times W at t. That is the exact solution. The lab’s exponential step on a grid is this formula on each step, with t replaced by the step width and W at t replaced by the Wiener increment over the step. It is not an Euler step on the level of S, and it is not a guess added to make a plot look random. If your code updates S by multiplying by 1 plus mu dt plus sigma times the square root of dt times Z, you have discretized the level. If your code multiplies S by the exponential of the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times dt, plus sigma times the square root of dt times Z, you have used the integral. Those are different updates. On a fine grid they are close. On a coarse grid they are not, and the exponential one matches the distribution of geometric Brownian motion at the grid times.

Put numbers on both updates for a coarse step, so the gap is not theoretical. Let S equal 100, mu equal 0.1, sigma equal 0.2, and let the step be a whole year, dt equal to 1, so the square root of dt is 1 and dW equals Z. The exact multiplier is the exponential of 0.08 plus 0.2 Z. The Euler multiplier is 1 plus 0.1 plus 0.2 Z, which is 1.1 plus 0.2 Z. If Z equals 0, the exact new level is 100 times the exponential of 0.08. The exponential of 0.08 is about 1.0833, so the level is about 108.33. The Euler level is 100 times 1.1, which is 110. If Z equals 1, the exact exponent is 0.08 plus 0.2, which is 0.28, and the exponential of 0.28 is about 1.3231, so the level is about 132.31. The Euler level is 100 times 1.3, which is 130. If Z equals negative 1, the exact exponent is 0.08 minus 0.2, which is negative 0.12, and the exponential of negative 0.12 is about 0.8869, so the level is about 88.69. The Euler level is 100 times 0.9, which is 90. The gaps are about 1.67, about 2.31, and about 1.31 on these three draws. A one-year Euler step is a blunt instrument. The lab is not required to be blunt, because the log integral is available in closed form. Use it.

The mean is the fact that makes the minus one half sigma squared feel paradoxical, so derive it instead of memorizing the paradox. S at t is S at 0 times the exponential of a normal random variable. The normal is the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, plus sigma times W at t. Its mean is the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, because W at t has mean 0. Its variance is sigma squared times t, because W at t has variance t. The exponential of a normal is lognormal, and the mean of the exponential of a normal with mean m and variance v is e to the m plus one half v. Here m is the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, and v is sigma squared t. So m plus one half v equals mu t minus one half sigma squared t plus one half sigma squared t, which is mu t. The one half sigma squared terms cancel. Therefore the expected value of S at t is S at 0 times e to the mu t. The correction that lowered the drift of the log is exactly undone, in the mean of the level, by the variance of the log. If you had forgotten the correction in the exponent and also forgotten to restore it through the lognormal mean, you might land on the right mean by a double error. The disciplined route is to keep the correction in the path and to watch it cancel in the expectation.

Jensen’s inequality is the sentence that sits between the two expressions. The exponential is a convex function. The expectation of the exponential is at least the exponential of the expectation. The expectation of the log of S at t is the log of S at 0 plus the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t. Exponentiate that, and you get S at 0 times e to the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t. That number is the exponential of the expected log. It is not the expected level. The expected level is larger, unless sigma is 0, in which case the two expressions agree because there is no variance and Jensen’s gap closes. Convexity of the exponential pushes the mean of S above the exponential of the mean of the log. The gap is not a simulation bias. It is the inequality, and for this lognormal law you even know both sides in closed form.

Compute both sides on the running example. S at 0 is 100, mu is 0.1, sigma is 0.2, t is 1. The expected level is 100 times e to the 0.1. The exponential of 0.1 is about 1.10517, so the mean is about 110.517. The exponential of the expected log is 100 times e to the 0.08, about 100 times 1.08329, about 108.329. The gap is about 2.188. The path formula spends most of its time, in the median sense associated with W at t equal to 0, near 108.329, while the mean is pulled up by the right tail to 110.517. Change only sigma, to 0.4. One half sigma squared is one half of 0.16, which is 0.08. The log drift is 0.1 minus 0.08, which is 0.02. The exponential of the expected log is 100 times e to the 0.02, about 102.020. The expected level is still 100 times e to the 0.1, about 110.517, because the mean does not depend on sigma. The gap widens from about 2.188 to about 8.497. More diffusion, same mean of S, lower median-type center, fatter right tail doing the lifting. If your simulated average level moves when you change sigma and you claim to be estimating the mean, you are seeing Monte Carlo noise or a bug, not a sigma dependence in the formula. The formula’s mean does not contain sigma.

The ring's ticket is a different number. It is how the ring knows about slots and publication in its own protocol. It is not the exchange's sequence. If you use the ticket as the deduplication id, you have overloaded the two numbers. A replay that goes through the ring again can receive a different ticket for the same exchange sequence, or a ticket can mean something that is legal for the ring and meaningless for the fill. The lecture does not ask you to invent that second story in detail. It asks you not to overload them. Deduplicate fills on the exchange sequence, or on an id you assigned once to the fill and stored with it. Do not deduplicate fills on a ring ticket you treat as if it were that id. The ring does not deduplicate. The consumer might have to. Those are different layers. The ring's job is the hot handoff. The consumer's job, under at-least-once delivery from a log or a gateway, is to apply a fill once.

Change one input: the retry count, from one extra delivery to two extra deliveries. The naive handler applies the fill three times in total, the original plus two retries. The position moves by three q. The deduplicating handler applies it once and ignores the two retries, because the sequence matched a sequence already applied. The gap between the handlers is two q on this changed input, and it was one q when there was only one retry. The bug scales with the number of extra deliveries. It does not scale with anything the market did. A quiet market and a single fill are enough to book three q if the transport retries twice and the handler is naive. Idempotence is the property that those retries do not change the position. It is not a property of the ring. The ring does not deduplicate. Putting the retry logic in the ring by overloading the ticket is the mistake the lecture names.

You might ask whether exactly-once delivery removes the need for the check. The lecture does not give you exactly-once as a property of these transports. It gives you at-least-once as the pattern that double-applies naive handlers, and it gives you a consumer that might have to deduplicate. If some other transport promised exactly-once, you would still keep the exchange sequence and the ring ticket distinct, because that distinction is about identity, not about a delivery slogan. A promise you did not receive is not a reason to apply q twice. The handler you can defend treats a repeated sequence as a repeat. The handler you cannot defend treats every arrival as new.

What the claim does not mean is that every duplicate byte is a duplicate fill. Deduplicate with an id. Two different fills can have the same quantity and the same side and still be two fills, if their exchange sequences differ. Collapsing them because q matches is the opposite bug: you drop a real fill. The id you do not invent twice is what separates those cases. It does not mean the ring ticket is useless. It means it is not the exchange sequence. Use it for the ring's own protocol. Do not use it as the fill's identity. It does not mean a consumer must deduplicate if the delivery is not repeated. It means a consumer that retries, or that sits behind at-least-once delivery, must not double-apply. It does not mean sequence numbers are only a concurrency trick. The lecture says they are also how you know you already saw this event. Concurrency can be part of the reason a ring has a ticket. Deduplication of fills is a reason the exchange sequence exists. Different reasons, different numbers.

Put the schema and the idempotence check in one place, because a version mismatch can look like a new event. An old record and a new record might carry the same exchange sequence and different price layouts. If you ignore the version, you may apply a lie, or you may treat the decoded lie as a second fill. The version check comes first. If the version does not match a layout you know, you do not apply the fill and you do not invent a second id. If the version matches and the sequence is one you have applied, you do not apply it again. If the version matches and the sequence is new, you apply q once. Three branches. The naive handler has one branch, apply whatever arrived, and that branch books two q or three q under retries. The lecture's handler has the three. The ring still does not do this for you. The consumer might have to.

Write the five stage names one last time, as the brief asks, with the contract attached rather than a slogan. Gateway read, with a layout you version. Ring push, in-process, not an audit, ticket not overloaded with the exchange sequence. Strategy decision, off the nanosecond claim if it is a net, and not a place that double-applies a fill. Ring pop, same ring, same warning. Gateway write, a stage that blocks only on the work you included, with retries that consult the exchange sequence before they move a position by q again. That list is the one you can defend. The lab defaults behind the sliders remain invented teaching values. Leave them in the model if you must, and do not defend them. Defend the names, the transport under each name, the schema version, and the id that keeps two deliveries of one fill from becoming two q.

One numerical identity check keeps the exchange sequence and the ring ticket from collapsing in a worked retry. Suppose the exchange sequence on the fill is one thousand seven, and the quantity q is five. The first delivery applies five. The position is plus five. A retry arrives with the same exchange sequence, one thousand seven, and the ring, because the event was pushed again, hands the consumer a different ticket, say ticket forty-four the first time and ticket forty-five the second time. Those ticket numbers are a teaching pair, not a trace. They exist so the overload is visible. A handler that deduplicates on the exchange sequence sees one thousand seven twice and applies five once. A handler that deduplicates on the ticket sees forty-four and then forty-five, treats them as two ids, and applies five twice. The position is plus ten. The market printed five. The extra five is the overloaded id, not a second fill. Change one input: let the retry keep the same ticket as well as the same sequence, both one thousand seven and both forty-four. Then even the overloaded handler happens to deduplicate. The bug hides. A single lucky reuse of a ticket is not a proof that the ticket is the exchange sequence. The lecture's rule is the rule that still works when the tickets differ. Do not overload them. The consumer that keys on one thousand seven books five. The consumer that keys on the ticket books ten whenever the ring assigns a fresh ticket to a redelivery. The ring does not deduplicate. The consumer might have to, and the id it uses is the one the exchange, or the fill itself, does not invent twice.

What the claim does not mean is as important as the table, because the table looks like algebra and people treat it like algebra that applies to every product in sight. It does not mean W is differentiable and dW over dt is an ordinary random function you may cancel. The previous lecture’s difference quotients blew up as the mesh shrank. This table is a substitute for that derivative, not a computation of it. It does not mean every square of every process equals its variance. The replacement of the square of dS by sigma squared S squared dt used the diffusion coefficient of this S, and it used the table on dW. A process with a different diffusion coefficient keeps a different quadratic term. If the diffusion coefficient were 0, the square of dS would be killed entirely and ordinary calculus would return. The second-order term survives because there is a diffusion, not because Taylor series always keep second derivatives in time. It does not mean the finite-step square 4.41 was illegal. It was the square of a real increment on a real grid. The formula replaces it by 4 when you pass to the limiting integral. Inside a coarse simulation you still have 4.41 if you square the realized increment. Do not “correct” a simulated increment by editing its square down to the limit expression. The limit expression is for the calculus, not a post-processing step on a single draw.

Change one input, the normal draw, and recompute the dropped piece so the drift’s absence from the quadratic term is checked rather than trusted. Keep S equal to 100, mu equal 0.1, sigma equal 0.2, and dt equal 0.01, and set Z equal to negative 0.5. Then dW equals negative 0.05. The drift piece is still 0.1. The diffusion piece is 0.2 times 100 times negative 0.05, which is negative 1. So dS equals negative 0.9. The raw square is 0.81. The rule's replacement is still sigma squared S squared dt, which is 0.04 times 10,000 times 0.01, which is 4, and it does not depend on Z. The raw square 0.81 is not close to 4. That is a useful shock. On a single step with a small Z, the realized square can be far from sigma squared S squared dt, because sigma squared S squared dt is the quadratic variation contribution, an order-dt object that a single draw estimates very noisily. The cross and drift pieces are 0.1 squared plus 2 times 0.1 times negative 1, which is 0.01 minus 0.2, which is negative 0.19, and  negative 1 squared is 1, and 1 plus negative 0.19 is 0.81. The table still tells you to replace the square by 4 inside the limiting calculus, not because 0.81 is near 4, but because sums of such squares concentrate, the way sums of squared Wiener increments concentrate on the clock. One step is allowed to look rude. The rule is about the accumulated integral. Hold that distinction when the formula in the next block writes one half sigma squared S squared times the second derivative as if it were a sure dt term. It is the sure term in the limit, not a claim about your last increment.

The idea in this block is the formula you will actually use. For geometric Brownian motion, the differential of f is the time partial, plus mu S times the S partial, plus one half sigma squared S squared times the second S partial, all of that times dt, plus sigma S times the S partial times dW. In words, df equals the quantity f sub t, plus mu S f sub S, plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS, times dt, plus sigma S f sub S dW. The drift of f is not the time partial plus mu S times the S partial. The second-derivative correction is the content of the course. If you remember a shorter drift, you have remembered ordinary calculus, and ordinary calculus is the wrong tool on this path.

Assemble it from the previous block rather than memorizing the parentheses first. The first-order contribution of dS is the S partial times dS, and dS is mu S dt plus sigma S dW, so this contribution splits into mu S times the S partial, times dt, and sigma S times the S partial, times dW. The time partial contributes its own dt. The second-order contribution that survived is one half the second S partial times sigma squared S squared dt. Add the dt coefficients. They are the time partial, the mu S times the first S partial, and the one half sigma squared S squared times the second S partial. Add the dW coefficient. It is sigma S times the first S partial. There is no second-derivative piece in front of dW. The second derivative joined the drift. That placement is the entire novelty.

The general diffusion statement is the same pattern with names for the coefficients. If dS equals a dt plus b dW, then df picks up one half b squared times the second S partial, times dt, on top of the first-order pieces f sub t dt plus f sub S times dS. For geometric Brownian motion, a is mu S and b is sigma S, so one half b squared is one half sigma squared S squared, and you recover the formula above. When you change models later, you change a and b and you keep the pattern. You do not re-derive the multiplication table unless the noise itself has changed. This course’s noise is a Wiener process, and the table from the previous block stays.

Read the drift of f as a function you could code, so the correction cannot hide inside notation. The drift coefficient is f sub t plus mu S f sub S plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS. The diffusion coefficient of f is sigma S f sub S. Both coefficients can depend on time and on S, through f and through the coefficients of S. Nothing in the formula requires f to be the identity function. If f is the identity, f of S equals S, then the time partial is 0, the first S partial is 1, and the second S partial is 0. The drift coefficient collapses to mu S, and the diffusion coefficient collapses to sigma S. You get dS back. That check is cheap and you should do it every time you write a general formula, because a general formula that cannot reproduce the process you started from has a partial wrong.

Do a second check on a function whose second derivative is not zero, but whose answer you can see without stochastic calculus, so the correction is forced to earn its place. Let f equal S squared. Then the time partial is 0, the first S partial is 2 S, and the second S partial is 2. The drift coefficient is mu S times 2 S, plus one half sigma squared S squared times 2. That is 2 mu S squared, plus sigma squared S squared. The diffusion coefficient is sigma S times 2 S, which is 2 sigma S squared. So the formula says the differential of S squared equals S squared times the quantity 2 mu plus sigma squared, times dt, plus 2 sigma S squared dW. Notice the sigma squared inside the drift. Ordinary calculus, which keeps only mu S times 2 S, would have reported 2 mu S squared and would have missed sigma squared S squared. The miss is not a change of notation. It is a different drift. At S equal to 100 and sigma equal to 0.2, the missed term sigma squared S squared is 0.04 times 10,000, which is 400. The kept ordinary term 2 mu S squared, at mu equal to 0.1, is 0.2 times 10,000, which is 2,000. The correction 400 is not a rounding error next to 2,000. It is a fifth of the ordinary piece, at these parameters, and it is present for every hour the process runs, in the drift coefficient, not only on volatile days. Days do not enter. The dt coefficient is a rate.

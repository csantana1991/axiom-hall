The idea in this opening block is a process with four properties, and then a stop. A standard Wiener process starts at 0. It has independent increments. It has Gaussian increments whose variance equals the length of the interval. It has continuous paths. Those four sentences are the definition you are allowed to use. Continuity is not differentiability. The path is too rough for ordinary calculus. Adding folklore on top of the four properties is how a later calculation picks up a derivative that is not there.

Start at time 0, with the value 0. That is the first property, and it is an equality, not a distribution. W at 0 is 0. There is no coin flip at the origin of the clock. If a picture starts somewhere else, it is not a standard Wiener process, though it may be a Wiener process plus a constant, which is a different object and should be named as one.

The second and third properties travel together. Take two times s and t with t greater than s. The increment W at t minus W at s is independent of the past up to time s, and it is normal with mean 0 and variance t minus s. The length of the interval is the variance, not the standard deviation. That slip is the one that ruins a simulation quietly. On an interval of length 1, the variance is 1 and the standard deviation is 1. On an interval of length 0.25, the variance is 0.25 and the standard deviation is 0.5. On an interval of length 4, the variance is 4 and the standard deviation is 2. The standard deviation scales with the square root of time. The variance scales with time. When you say "the increment is about the size of the interval," you have said the wrong scaling. The typical size is the square root of the interval.

Put the Gaussian law on a concrete interval. From time 0.25 to time 1, the length is 0.75, so the increment is normal with mean 0 and variance 0.75. The standard deviation is the square root of 0.75, about 0.866. An increment of 0.3 on that interval is a modest draw. An increment of 3 is several standard deviations out, not a draw you should treat as typical when you sketch. From time 2 to time 5 the length is 3, so the variance of the increment is 3 and the standard deviation is the square root of 3, about 1.732. Independence says that this increment does not remember the value of W at time 2, except that it is added to it. The past can set the level. It does not set the distribution of the change.

The variance of the level itself is the increment law with s equal to 0. Because W at 0 is 0, W at t is an increment over an interval of length t, so W at t is normal with mean 0 and variance t. At time 1, W is a standard normal. At time 0.25, W is normal with variance 0.25, standard deviation 0.5. At time 4, W is normal with variance 4, standard deviation 2. The distribution is spreading like the square root of the clock. A path that has the same visual width at time 4 as it had at time 1 is not a sketch of this process.

The covariance follows from the increment law, and you should derive it once so the formula min of s and t is not a charm. Assume s is less than or equal to t. Write W at t as W at s plus the increment from s to t. The increment is independent of the past, hence uncorrelated with W at s, and it has mean 0. The covariance of W at s and W at t is the covariance of W at s with itself, plus a zero, which is the variance of W at s, which is s. If instead t is the smaller time, the same argument gives t. So the covariance of W at s and W at t is the minimum of s and t. Check the arithmetic on numbers. Covariance of W at 0.25 and W at 1 is 0.25. Covariance of W at 2 and W at 5 is 2. Covariance of W at 0.5 with itself is 0.5, which matches the variance. The correlation is not the covariance. Correlation divides by the two standard deviations. At times 0.25 and 1, the standard deviations are 0.5 and 1, so the correlation is 0.25 divided by 0.5, which is 0.5. At times 2 and 5 the standard deviations are the square root of 2 and the square root of 5, about 1.414 and 2.236, and the covariance is 2, so the correlation is 2 divided by about 3.162, about 0.632. The covariance formula is the one on the board. The correlation is a rescaling you can compute when you need it, and it is not a substitute for the minimum.

Independence of increments has a second numerical face. The covariance between the increment from s to t and the level W at s should be zero when s is the earlier time. That covariance is the covariance of W at t and W at s, minus the variance of W at s. With s equal to 0.25 and t equal to 1, that is 0.25 minus 0.25, which is 0. With s equal to 2 and t equal to 5, that is 2 minus 2, which is 0. The increment really does not correlate with where you were. If a simulation produces increments that systematically continue the previous step, the independence property is the one that failed.

The fourth property is continuous paths. A continuous path can be drawn without lifting the pen, in the sense that it does not jump. It does not follow that the pen has a slope. Differentiability would ask for a slope. This process does not have one, as a pathwise fact you should treat as part of the roughness, not as a rare accident on a few unlucky draws. Ordinary calculus is the calculus of functions that are smooth enough for a first-order Taylor expansion to be the whole story. The Wiener path is continuous and still too rough for that story. The next block says what the roughness is, in the only units that survive: squared increments. Until then, the prohibition is enough. Do not differentiate W with respect to t and then feed that derivative to an ordinary chain rule.

You might ask whether the four properties overdetermine the process, or whether continuity is already implied by the Gaussian increments. Keep the list as the lesson states it. The increment law describes the process at a finite set of times. Continuity is a statement about the whole path, including times you did not list. A finite collection of normal random variables with the right covariances matches the process at those times and says nothing, by itself, about whether the path jumps between them. The continuous version is the process in the definition. When you plot, you will be tempted to connect the dots with straight segments. That temptation belongs to the last block. For now, notice that a finite list of increment laws plus a refusal to jump is the whole object. You do not need a velocity to finish the definition. The definition stopped.

A step past the new ceiling is the other bug, and it should look familiar with new numbers. Lambda equals 2, eta equals 0.3, and 0.3 is above 0.25. Start at the origin. The first step still goes to 0.6 and negative 0.72, because the first gradient does not yet contain the penalty. The original bowl there is 0.4672, but the penalized value is the bowl plus 1 times 0.36 plus about 0.518, which is 0.36 plus 0.5184, which is 0.8784, plus the bowl 0.4672, which is 1.3456. Already the penalized value only barely fell, from 1.48 to 1.3456. The next gradient is not the unpenalized gradient. The y coordinate then swings positive, to about 0.288, and the penalized value rises above 2. Later swings throw y past negative 1 and then past positive 1, and the penalized value keeps growing. The same step size that converged on the unpenalized bowl, where the ceiling was one third, diverges once lambda equals 2 has lowered the ceiling to one quarter. If the path explodes after you raise lambda, the step is a suspect before the formula is.

You might ask whether y and x slide at the same rate, since both are being pulled to the origin. They do not. The formula is the reason. The x coordinate is 2 over the quantity 2 plus lambda, so half the distance from 1 to the new x is closed when 2 over the quantity 2 plus lambda equals one half, which means 2 plus lambda equals 4, so lambda equals 2. You already computed that point: x equals 0.5, halfway from 1 to 0 in the sense of the coordinate itself. The y coordinate at lambda equals 2 is negative 0.3, and the old y was negative 0.4, so you have only moved from negative 0.4 to negative 0.3, a quarter of the way from the old value toward zero, not halfway. y gets halfway to zero when negative 2.4 over the quantity 6 plus lambda equals negative 0.2, so 2.4 over the quantity 6 plus lambda equals 0.2, so 6 plus lambda equals 2.4 over 0.2, which is 12, so lambda equals 6. At that same lambda, x is already 0.25, which is three quarters of the way from 1 to 0. The y curvature of the original bowl was three times the x curvature. The stiffer direction resists the penalty longer. Bias is not a uniform shrink of the vector. It is the solution of two different linear equations.

What the claim does not mean, on this slide, is a list you should be able to say back. It does not mean the new point is the old point rescaled by a constant you can pull out of both coordinates. At lambda equals 2 the x coordinate went from 1 to 0.5, a factor of one half, and the y coordinate went from negative 0.4 to negative 0.3, a factor of three quarters. Two factors, one lambda. It does not mean you have lowered the original loss. You raised it, from 0 to 0.28 at lambda equals 2, and from 0 to about 0.521 at lambda equals 4. It does not mean a late iterate that is still creeping is a wrong formula. Compare it to 2 over the quantity 2 plus lambda and to negative 2.4 over the quantity 6 plus lambda when it has settled, not on the first step, which matches the old walk. It does not mean the origin is the answer at lambda equals 100. The answer there is about 0.0196 and negative 0.0226. Close is not the same sentence as equal, and the formula will give you the difference for any finite lambda you are willing to plug in.

The idea in this block is that an L1 penalty is a different geometry, and that the slide you just computed is not a soft shrink under another name. An L1 penalty has a subgradient, a kink at zero, and coordinates that can become exactly zero. This lab does not show L1. Weight decay, in the sense of the quadratic penalty on this board, and a map that snaps a coordinate to exactly zero, are different maps. If you describe one with the vocabulary of the other, you will report a zero that the formula cannot produce, or a smooth slide that a kink does not make.

Stay with the formula you can solve, and read what it refuses to do. For any finite lambda, x equals 2 divided by the quantity 2 plus lambda, which is positive, and y equals negative 2.4 divided by the quantity 6 plus lambda, which is negative. Neither coordinate is zero. Raise lambda from 18 to 30. At lambda equals 30, x equals 2 over 32, which is 0.0625, and y equals negative 2.4 over 36, which is negative 0.0667. Both coordinates are small. Neither is zero. The original bowl at that point is the square of 0.0625 minus 1, plus 3 times the square of negative 0.0667 plus 0.4. The x error is negative 0.9375, and its square is about 0.8789. The y error is about 0.3333, and its square is about 0.1111, times 3 is about 0.3333. The bowl is about 1.212. You have nearly returned to the origin's bowl value of 1.48, and you still have not set a coordinate to zero. A quadratic penalty spends its effort on shrinking. Exact zeros are a different purchase, and this objective does not sell them.

The kink is the geometric reason, said without pretending the lab ran an L1 solver. A squared penalty is smooth at the origin. Its derivative contribution is lambda times the coordinate, which is zero when the coordinate is zero, so the origin does not exert a pull unless you are away from it, and the pull then grows with distance. A penalty built from absolute values has a corner at zero. The subgradient at that corner is a set of slopes rather than a single slope, and zero can be a stationary coordinate when the unpenalized partial at zero sits inside that set. That is how a coordinate becomes exactly zero and stays there. This lesson names that fact so you do not confuse it with the slide. It does not give you the lab path, the threshold formula, or a numeric L1 solution to memorize. If you need a number, the number you are allowed to compute is the ridge fixed point. If you need an exact zero, you are in a different objective, and you should say so before you quote this board.

Bias, named, is the comparison between the two fixed points, not a comment on the printer. The unpenalized minimizer is 1 and negative 0.4. The penalized minimizer is 2 over the quantity 2 plus lambda, and negative 2.4 over the quantity 6 plus lambda. The difference is the bias relative to the unpenalized minimizer. At lambda equals 2 the difference in x is 1 minus 0.5, which is 0.5, and the difference in y is negative 0.4 minus negative 0.3, which is negative 0.1. At lambda equals 10 the difference in x is 1 minus one sixth, which is five sixths, and the difference in y is negative 0.4 minus negative 0.15, which is negative 0.25. The bias grows as lambda grows. That can lower risk. It does not lower training loss. You already measured the training loss. At lambda equals 2 the original bowl rose from 0 to 0.28. At lambda equals 4 it rose to about 0.521. At lambda equals 10 it is the square of one sixth minus 1, plus 3 times the square of negative 0.15 plus 0.4. The x piece is the square of negative five sixths, which is 25 over 36, about 0.6944. The y error is 0.25, its square is 0.0625, times 3 is 0.1875. The bowl is about 0.8819. Training loss went 0, then 0.28, then 0.521, then 0.882 as lambda went 0, then 2, then 4, then 10. The direction is up. Anyone who tells you the penalty improved the training loss is reading a different column.

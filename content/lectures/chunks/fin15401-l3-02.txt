Put the two beta-1 assets on the security market line by their betas and then again by their standard deviations, so the axes cannot be confused in memory. Both have beta 1, so both sit at horizontal position 1 and vertical position 0.08 when the risk-free rate is 0.02 and the market excess is 0.06. One of them has standard deviation 0.20. The other has standard deviation 0.30 because of idiosyncratic variance 0.05. On a standard-deviation chart those are two different x-coordinates, 0.20 and 0.30, with the same expected return 0.08, a flat pair, not a line with slope 0.06. Add the beta one half asset, expected return 0.05, standard deviation about 0.245. In beta space it is halfway from 0 to 1 and its expected return is halfway from 0.02 to 0.08, because halfway in beta is halfway in the priced mean. In standard-deviation space it sits at about 0.245, between 0.20 and 0.30, with a lower mean than both. Halfway in risk measured by sigma is not where it lives, and its mean is not halfway between 0.08 and 0.08. The security market line is the beta chart. If your drawing slopes against sigma, you have changed the theorem.

One more substitution, beta equal to 0, keeps the intercept honest. Covariance 0, market variance 0.04, beta 0. Expected return equals 0.02 plus 0 times 0.06, which is 0.02. Lambda times the covariance is 1.5 times 0, which is 0. There is no excess. If someone adds a "diversification bonus" on top of 0.02 because the asset is uncorrelated, they have left the pricing statement. Uncorrelated, in this model, means unpriced, and unpriced means the risk-free rate, not the risk-free rate plus a compliment. The idiosyncratic variance of that asset can be large. It still does not enter the expected return, and that conclusion still dies if the holder cannot diversify. The formula does not contain a slot for their inability. You have to say the inability in a separate sentence and stop using the line.

Here is what the pricing statement does not mean. It does not mean the market portfolio is efficient. It means if it is, then expected excess returns are linear in beta. CAPM holds when the market portfolio is the tangency portfolio. If your market proxy is not that portfolio, the equality is not a claim about the proxy. The slide names the empirical warning in one line: Roll's critique is that you test a proxy, not the market. Keep that in a different sentence from the algebra. The algebra did not look at a proxy. It looked at M, defined to be the tangency portfolio. It does not mean idiosyncratic risk is irrelevant to an investor who cannot diversify. The sentence that leaves idiosyncratic variance unpriced dies exactly there. An undiversified holder of the noisy beta-1 asset, standard deviation 0.30, does not experience the same risk as the holder of the clean beta-1 asset, standard deviation 0.20, even though the line quotes both at expected return 0.08. The line is a statement about priced expected return in the model where the idiosyncratic piece can be diversified away. It is not a statement that the two holders sleep the same.

The board in front of you is titled what you may not conclude. A low-beta stock is not automatically a buy. CAPM restricts means. It does not rank purchases. Mispricing, if you want that word, is a residual against a model you are willing to defend. Alpha from an in-sample regression is a fitting error until it survives a clock it did not see. The lab does not estimate a beta from prices. It only shows the mean-variance object the model assumes is the market. Do not fit a stock in the lab and call it a CAPM test.

Start with a low beta that the line has priced exactly, so the temptation has nothing to grab. Beta one half, risk-free rate 0.02, market excess 0.06. The line says the expected return is 0.05. Suppose the mean you computed in the same sample is also 0.05. The residual is zero. There is no alpha. A person who calls this a buy because the beta is low is not pointing at a cheap residual. The residual is gone. What they are pointing at is a lower mean, 0.05 against the market's 0.08, which is the model being consistent. Low beta paid less, in expectation, because the slope says it should. Treating that lower mean as a discount sticker reverses the equality. The equality already assigned the mean. It did not leave a bargain underneath.

Now take a residual that is not zero, and refuse to promote it. The beta 1.2 asset is priced at 0.092 on the first line. Suppose the sample mean on the same window is 0.11. The gap is 0.11 minus 0.092, which is 0.018. That 0.018 is the in-sample alpha against this line. It is the fitting error of a comparison that used the covariance, the market variance, the risk-free rate, the market mean, and the asset mean from one clock. It has not survived a clock it did not see. You may write the number down. You may ask whether the model is the one you are willing to defend, because if you will not defend the if, the residual is not mispricing. It is the distance to a line you do not believe. If you will defend the line, the residual is still a same-sample gap until a later window, one that did not choose these inputs, still shows it. The board does not give you that later window. It tells you not to pretend the current gap is already the test.

A second residual, built from the smaller case, keeps the moral from depending on 0.018. Beta 0.80, risk-free rate 0.01, market excess 0.06, priced mean 0.058. Suppose the sample mean is 0.07. The gap is 0.012. Suppose instead the sample mean is 0.04. The gap is negative 0.018. The positive gap and the negative gap are the same kind of object. One of them flatters a purchase and the other flatters a sale, and neither has left the sample that produced the beta. Changing the risk-free rate to 0.02 moved the priced mean from 0.058 to 0.060 and would move both gaps by 0.002. A residual that changes when you retouch an input you already had is not a property of the asset. It is a property of the line you drew.

The market weights identity puts a lid on what an in-sample alpha can mean even before a new clock arrives. Betas weighted by market weights equal 1, so the market's own alpha against itself is zero. In the two-asset picture, weights 0.40 and 0.60, betas 1.5 and two thirds, the priced means are 0.11 and 0.06 if the risk-free rate is 0.02 and the market excess is 0.06. The market mean is 0.40 times 0.11 plus 0.60 times 0.06, which is 0.044 plus 0.036, which is 0.080, exactly the market mean you started with. If your sample means differ from 0.11 and 0.06, the value-weighted gap is a gap against a market mean that those same sample means also define, once the market is the weighted sum. You can move alpha from one asset to the other. You cannot give every asset a positive in-sample alpha against the portfolio that is built from them and then act surprised when the weights refuse to add up. That is part of why a proxy test is not the theorem. If the portfolio in the regression is not the market, Roll's critique is the right sentence: you tested the proxy. If the portfolio is exactly the weighted bundle of the assets you are scoring, a cloud of positive alphas is an arithmetic mistake, not a basket of bargains.

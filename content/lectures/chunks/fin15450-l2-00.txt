The idea in this block is a Taylor expansion in which you keep the term ordinary calculus drops. For a function f of time and of a price S, the second derivative in S, multiplied by the square of dS, does not vanish when S has a diffusion. If dS equals mu S dt plus sigma S dW, then the square of dS equals sigma squared S squared dt, once you use the rule that the square of dW equals dt and you drop what is of higher order. You substitute that square back into the expansion and you collect terms in dt and in dW. The multiplication table is the whole license for the dropping. It is a calculation rule for integrals, not a claim that a derivative of W exists.

Write the table before you use it, in words, because the notation is easy to misuse. The product dt times dt is 0. The product dt times dW is 0. The product dW times dW is dt. Nothing in that table says W has a time derivative. The previous lecture forbade that derivative, and this table does not sneak it back in. The table tells you which products of increments survive when the mesh goes to zero inside an integral, given that the sum of squared Wiener increments tends to the length of the interval. You use the table inside a second-order expansion, and then you drop what the table kills. If a term contains dt times dW, it goes. If a term contains dt squared, it goes. If a term contains dW squared, it becomes a dt, and it stays.

Start from the ordinary second-order expansion so the new piece has a place to sit. For a smooth function of time and S, the change in f is the time partial times dt, plus the S partial times dS, plus one half the second S partial times the square of dS, plus higher-order remainders. I am suppressing the mixed partial and the pure second time partial in that sentence because the table will kill them, and I want their death to be a calculation rather than a shrug. The mixed partial produces a product dt times dS. The pure second time partial produces dt squared. Expand dS itself as mu S dt plus sigma S dW. Then dt times dS contains dt squared and dt times dW. Both of those are 0 under the table. So the mixed partial does not survive. The second time partial does not survive. What can survive, besides the first-order pieces, is one half the second S partial times the square of dS.

Now square dS with the table in your hand. dS equals mu S dt plus sigma S dW. The square expands into three pieces: the square of mu S dt, the cross term 2 mu S sigma S dt dW, and the square of sigma S dW. The first piece has dt squared in it and dies. The cross term has dt times dW in it and dies. The last piece is sigma squared S squared times dW squared, and dW squared becomes dt. So the square of dS equals sigma squared S squared dt, in the sense of this calculation rule. The drift mu entered the square and then left, because every place it entered was a higher-order product. The diffusion coefficient is the one that sets the quadratic term. For geometric Brownian motion the diffusion coefficient b, in the form dS equals a dt plus b dW, is sigma S, and b squared is sigma squared S squared. The same sentence in the general diffusion is that the square of dS equals b squared dt. Geometric Brownian motion is the case a equals mu S and b equals sigma S.

A numerical step shows what was dropped, so the phrase higher order is a size and not a slogan. Let S equal 100, mu equal 0.1, sigma equal 0.2, and dt equal 0.01. Let Z equal 1, so dW equals the square root of 0.01 times 1, which is 0.1. The drift piece mu S dt is 0.1 times 100 times 0.01, which is 0.1. The diffusion piece sigma S dW is 0.2 times 100 times 0.1, which is 2. So dS equals 2.1, and the raw square of dS is 4.41. The rule says the square of dS should be replaced by sigma squared S squared dt, which is 0.04 times 10,000 times 0.01, which is 4. The difference between 4.41 and 4 is 0.41, and that difference is what the table tells you to drop. Where did 0.41 come from? The drift piece 0.1 squared is 0.01. The cross term 2 times 0.1 times 2 is 0.4. Together they are 0.41. They are real on this finite step. They are the terms the limit will kill as dt shrinks, relative to the dt that you keep. The piece you keep, 4, is sigma squared S squared times dt, and it is the same order as dt. The piece you drop is smaller order in the limit, even though on a width of 0.01 the number 0.41 is not tiny next to 4. A width of 0.01 is a coarse illustration, not the limit. The rule is the limit’s rule. You should see the dropped piece once, so you know what you are discarding, and then you should discard it when you invoke the formula.

Shrink the same step and watch the dropped fraction fall. Change only dt, from 0.01 to 0.0001, and keep Z equal to 1. Then dW equals 0.01. The drift piece is 0.1 times 100 times 0.0001, which is 0.001. The diffusion piece is 0.2 times 100 times 0.01, which is 0.2. So dS equals 0.201, and the raw square is 0.040401. The rule’s replacement is sigma squared S squared dt, which is 0.04 times 10,000 times 0.0001, which is 0.04. The absolute gap is 0.000401. Relative to the kept term 0.04, the gap is about one percent, whereas on the width 0.01 the gap 0.41 relative to 4 was about ten percent. Refining the step shrinks the relative size of what the table drops. That is the sense in which the replacement becomes accurate. It is not accurate because 4.41 and 4 were close on the coarse step. They were only roughly close. It is accurate in the limit the quadratic variation lecture already prepared.

Put the kept square back into the second-order piece. One half times the second S partial, times the square of dS, becomes one half times the second S partial, times sigma squared S squared, times dt. That term is a dt term. It joins the other dt terms. It is not a curiosity off to the side of the drift. It is part of the drift of f. If your expansion stops at the first derivative, you have omitted a dt term of exactly the order you intended to keep. The omission is not a small numerical leak on a fine grid. It is a wrong coefficient in the limit.

You might ask whether the table could have kept dt times dW, since a sum of those products might conceivably accumulate. The rule used here sets that product to 0, and the justification is the same mesh limit that sets dt squared to 0 and dW squared to dt, not a separate aesthetic. On the numerical step, dt times dW is 0.01 times 0.1, which is 0.001, while dW squared is 0.01. The product dt dW is an order smaller than the dW squared term you are promoting into a dt. On the finer step, dt times dW is 0.0001 times 0.01, which is 10 to the minus 6, while dW squared is 0.0001. Again the cross time-noise product is negligible next to the square you keep. If a derivation you are reading retains dt times dW as a dt term, it is using a different multiplication rule from the one in this lesson. This lesson’s table is dt times dt equals 0, dt times dW equals 0, and dW times dW equals dt.

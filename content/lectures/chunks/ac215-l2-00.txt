The idea in this opening block is that serving is a stage with a tail. A model server has a queue, a batching window, and a device transfer. Those are random variables. Put them in the budget. A picture of Triton or Kubernetes is not a latency.

Name the three pieces before you add them. The queue is the wait until the server takes the request. It is a random variable, and it belongs in the stage that blocks, which is the serving stage if that is where the request waits. The batching window is the time the server holds inputs so it can run them together. Throughput loves batches. Latency hates them. The window is a stage, or it is part of the serving stage, and either way it gets a mean and a tail. Timeouts are part of the mean. A timeout you forgot is a mass of long draws you deleted from the average, which makes the mean look kinder than the policy you actually run. The device transfer is the move onto the hardware that runs the model, and the move back. It is not instant because the picture drew a box next to a chip. It is a duration with a distribution.

A picture of Triton is a picture of a server. A picture of Kubernetes is a picture of a way to run processes. Neither picture is a number of time units. You may use either tool. You may not enter its name on the budget line where a mean should be. The budget line wants the queue, the window, and the transfer, each with a spread you will defend, or one serving line whose distribution already contains all three and says so. If you write "Triton" in the cell, you have named a tool. The opening rule of the budget lecture still applies. Tools implement stages. Stages have distributions. The distribution is the latency.

The lab's instruction for this block is an order of magnitude, not a catalog of defaults. Set the model stage an order of magnitude above the gateway. See it dominate. Then set it near zero and read what the handoff budget becomes. An order of magnitude, in the ordinary arithmetic this sentence needs, means a factor of ten. Let the gateway mean be g. The model mean is ten g. The sum of those two means is eleven g. The model's share of that pair is ten elevenths. Ten divided by eleven is about zero point nine zero nine. About ninety-one percent of the pair's mean is the model. The gateway is the other one eleventh, about nine percent. Dominance here is not a metaphor. It is ten against one, inside a sum of eleven. Any percentile you build from these means will sit above a number that is already mostly the model, because the mean of the sum is already mostly the model. Spreads can emphasize the point, and they are not required to see it.

The notes say the lab's default numbers are invented teaching values. Replace them before you defend. Ten g and g are not those defaults. They are the order-of-magnitude instruction translated into a factor you can divide. If the lab's slider is sitting on invented defaults, you still do this ratio after you set the model to ten times the gateway, whatever the gateway's current teaching value is. You do not publish the teaching value. You publish the structure: the model line dominates the pair by ten to one in the mean.

Change one input. Set the model to ten times the gateway, then cut that factor from ten to five, a single change in the multiple. The model mean is five g. The pair sums to six g. The model's share is five sixths, about zero point eight three three. It still dominates, and it dominates by less. Eighty-three percent is not ninety-one percent. The lecture's phrase was an order of magnitude, which is the factor ten, not the factor five. The factor five is the changed input that shows dominance is a ratio you compute, not a word you earn by having a model at all. A model whose mean equals the gateway's mean is a share of one half. That is not dominance. The brief asked you to set the stage an order of magnitude above, so that the dominance would be obvious in the sum. If your slider is at a factor of two, you have not yet done the exercise, and you should not describe the picture as the model eating the budget. Compute the share.

You might ask whether the tail can dominate even when the mean does not. Yes, in principle, and the budget lecture already gave you the mechanism. A stage with a modest mean and a huge standard deviation can own the ninety-ninth percentile of an independent Gaussian sum, because variances add and the square root of a large variance is still large. This block's brief does not ask you to build that case first. It asks you to put the model an order of magnitude above the gateway in the level of the stage, and to watch the mean get eaten. The tail is still part of serving. Queue, window, and transfer each have a tail. If you only inflate the model mean and leave its spread at zero, you have modeled a slow constant, not a server. A real serving stage gets both the factor-of-ten lesson and a spread you will defend. The picture of the server supplies neither.

What the claim does not mean is that every model must be ten times slower than every gateway, as a fact about systems. The factor ten is the lab's instruction for seeing dominance, a teaching edit. It does not mean Triton or Kubernetes is slow. They are names of tools, and a name is not a latency. It does not mean batching is forbidden. Throughput loves batches. The window is allowed. It is not allowed to hide outside the budget. It does not mean a timeout is a failure you exclude from the mean. Timeouts are part of the mean. Dropping them is how a reported mean stops describing the policy. It does not mean the other stages vanished. Ingest, feature, and risk check, if they are on the path, still add their means. The pair of model and gateway was the comparison the brief named. A full sum is larger than eleven g by every other positive mean. The model's share of the full sum is then less than ten elevenths, and it remains the largest single term if every other stage is on the scale of the gateway rather than on the scale of the model. Say that condition. Do not hide it inside the word dominate.

Work the near-zero edit the brief asks for, on the same pair, so the handoff has a before and after. Model mean ten g, gateway mean g, pair mean eleven g. Set the model near zero. Take the teaching extreme the sentence permits and set it to zero, which is nearer than any positive leftover you have not been given. The pair mean falls from eleven g to g. Everything that remains, in this pair, is the gateway. That is the handoff budget for the pair: one gateway mean, and whatever spread the gateway still has. If serving was the only large term, removing it does not leave a mysterious residual ten g. It leaves the stages you did not remove. Read them. Write them down. The architecture you meant is whichever of those two budgets you are willing to defend: the one where the model is on the path and owns about ten elevenths of the pair, or the one where the model is off the path and the pair collapses to the gateway. The slider is how you see the difference. The sentence you write in the design note is the difference.

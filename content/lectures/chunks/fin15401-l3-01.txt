Here is what the definition does not mean. It does not mean the asset ought to have beta 1.2, or that 1.2 is aggressive in any moral sense. It means this covariance over this variance equals 1.2. It does not mean the number is stable because the formula is simple. Move the covariance from 0.048 to 0.044 and the slope is 1.1. It does not mean a weighted average of betas equals 1 for an arbitrary set of weights. The weights have to be the market weights, the ones inside the market return you covaried against. A portfolio that uses different weights has a beta equal to its own weighted average, and that average equals 1 only if the portfolio is the market, or if you chose betas that happen to average to 1. The identity is about that construction. It is not a compliment you pay every book.

The board in front of you is titled the pricing statement. If the market portfolio is mean-variance efficient, expected excess returns are linear in beta. In words, the expected return of asset i equals r sub f plus beta sub i times the quantity expected market return minus r sub f. The theorem is an if. The tests argue about the if. You can know the line cold and still not know whether the market portfolio you have in mind is the tangency portfolio. Those are different sentences. The notes tell you to keep the algebra and the empirics apart, and this is the place they come apart.

The line is the security market line. It plots expected return against beta, not against standard deviation. Build it on the same market you already used. Let the risk-free rate be 0.02 and the expected market return be 0.08, so the market excess is 0.06. Beta 0, which is a projection with no covariance to the market, has expected return 0.02. You earn the risk-free rate and nothing for a beta that is not there. Beta one half has expected return 0.02 plus one half times 0.06, which is 0.05. Beta two thirds, the second asset in the market-weight identity, has expected return 0.02 plus two thirds times 0.06, which is 0.06. Beta 1 is the market itself, expected return 0.08. Beta 1.2 has expected return 0.02 plus 1.2 times 0.06, which is 0.02 plus 0.072, which is 0.092. Beta 1.5 has expected return 0.02 plus 0.09, which is 0.11. The points lie on a straight line in the beta direction. They do not lie on a straight line in the standard-deviation direction, and the idiosyncratic piece is why.

Idiosyncratic variance is not priced if it can be diversified. That sentence dies if investors cannot diversify. Take two assets that both have beta 1. The first has no idiosyncratic variance. Its total variance is beta squared times market variance, which is 0.04, so its standard deviation is 0.20, and the line says its expected return is 0.08. The second has idiosyncratic variance 0.05. Its total variance is 0.04 plus 0.05, which is 0.09, so its standard deviation is 0.30, and the line still says 0.08. Same expected return, different total risk. If you plot expected return against standard deviation, these two points share a height and not a horizontal position. They do not draw the security market line. A third asset with beta one half and idiosyncratic variance 0.05 has total variance 0.25 times 0.04 plus 0.05, which is 0.06, standard deviation about 0.245, and expected return 0.05. In the standard-deviation plot it sits left of the noisy beta-1 asset and below it. In the beta plot it sits at one half, on the line, and the noisy beta-1 asset sits at 1, on the same line. The horizontal axis is the whole difference between the two drawings.

The route from tangency to this line is a first-order condition, and the notes ask you to walk it. Take the tangency portfolio as the market M. The first-order condition says expected excess returns are proportional to covariance with M. There is a single constant of proportionality, call it lambda, that does not depend on the asset. Apply the condition to the market itself. The market's excess expected return equals lambda times the market variance. So lambda equals the market excess divided by the market variance. In the numbers, lambda equals 0.06 divided by 0.04, which is 1.5. For the asset with covariance 0.048, the excess expected return is 1.5 times 0.048, which is 0.072. Add the risk-free rate and the expected return is 0.092, which is the same point you got from beta. Divide the proportionality by the market variance instead of multiplying lambda by the covariance, and the covariance over the variance appears by itself. That ratio is beta. Excess expected return equals beta times the market excess. Nothing in that paragraph estimated a regression from a tape of prices. It rewrote the tangency condition in the units of beta. The frontier geometry and this pricing corollary are the same if, said in two vocabularies. Do not fit a stock in the frontier lab and call the fit a test of the corollary.

Change one input, the risk-free rate, and watch the line tilt without any new covariance. Keep the expected market return at 0.08 and move r sub f from 0.02 to 0.03. The market excess falls from 0.06 to 0.05. Lambda falls from 1.5 to 0.05 divided by 0.04, which is 1.25. The asset with beta 1.2 now has expected return 0.03 plus 1.2 times 0.05, which is 0.03 plus 0.06, which is 0.09. It was 0.092. The covariance did not change, and the priced mean did, because the hurdle and the market excess changed. Beta is not a complete description of the expected return. It is the coefficient on an excess you still have to supply. A conversation that quotes 1.2 and does not quote r sub f or the market excess has not stated the pricing sentence.

A second, smaller case keeps the algebra from being a single memorized 1.2. Let the market variance be 0.0225, so the market standard deviation is 0.15. Let the covariance be 0.018. Beta equals 0.018 divided by 0.0225, which is 0.80. Let the risk-free rate be 0.01 and the expected market return be 0.07, so the excess is 0.06. The expected asset return is 0.01 plus 0.80 times 0.06, which is 0.058. Lambda here is 0.06 divided by 0.0225, which is about 2.667, and lambda times the covariance is about 2.667 times 0.018, which is 0.048. Add 0.01 and you get 0.058 again. Move only the risk-free rate to 0.02 and keep the expected market return at 0.07. The excess is now 0.05. The expected asset return is 0.02 plus 0.80 times 0.05, which is 0.060. Same beta, different line, different priced mean. If your notes treat beta as if it were already an expected return, this is the counterexample.

You might ask where a separate forecast of the asset's mean goes. Under the if, it does not go anywhere. The expected return is pinned by the risk-free rate, the market excess, and beta. A forecast that disagrees with 0.092, in the first case, is a disagreement with the if, or a disagreement with the inputs you used to compute beta. It is not an extra term you add because the forecast feels informed. You may reject the if. The tests argue about that. You may not keep the if and also keep an unrelated mean. The sentence is an equality, not a suggestion.

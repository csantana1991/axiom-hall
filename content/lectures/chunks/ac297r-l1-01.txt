What the claim does not mean is that the event is forbidden to look like a market event. An order identifier, a side, a price in integer ticks, a size: those can live in a trivially copyable struct, and the checksum can still add the identifiers. The marketplace prohibition is about behavior, not about vocabulary. The ring does not match, does not advertise, and does not credit an account. It copies bytes from one thread to another under a protocol, with no lock and with no allocation after startup. The claim also does not mean that one producer and one consumer is a temporary simplification you may violate with a comment. The listing is specialized to one writer by convention. A second producer is a different protocol. You will see that again when the compare-exchange is on the table. Here, the point of saying it early is that the scope is a contract. One producer, one consumer, fixed capacity, trivially copyable event, no allocation after startup. If a sentence in your defense requires a second writer, the sentence is about a system you did not build.

The second idea is that the targets are hypotheses. More than one million events a second, and a handoff under two hundred fifty nanoseconds, are reasonable ambitions on pinned metal with a warm cache. They are not properties of this browser. The lab measures the protocol in JavaScript and says so. The C plus plus file is what you time. Browser timings are JavaScript timings. Nanoseconds come from a counter on the machine, or not at all. When you do have a machine number, you state the CPU, the compiler flags, and the core pin. If you do not have them, you do not have the number.

Turn the ambitions into arithmetic so they cannot hide inside a prefix. One second contains one billion nanoseconds. If you hand off one million events in that second, and you pretend the handoffs share the second evenly, each handoff occupies one thousand nanoseconds. One thousand is four times two hundred fifty. So a handoff of two hundred fifty nanoseconds is a stricter pace than one million events a second, if the handoff were the only work in the second. At two hundred fifty nanoseconds each, one billion divided by two hundred fifty is four million events in a second. The two ambitions are not the same sentence. "More than one million events a second" allows a thousand nanoseconds per event. "Under two hundred fifty nanoseconds" asks for something at least four times tighter, and it asks for it as a handoff latency rather than as a throughput slogan. Both are described as reasonable ambitions on pinned metal with a warm cache. Neither is a measurement sitting in the repository waiting for you to copy it onto a slide.

Apply the same arithmetic to the checksum's one hundred thousand events, still as arithmetic and not as a timing you ran. At one thousand nanoseconds each, one hundred thousand events occupy one hundred thousand times one thousand nanoseconds, which is one hundred million nanoseconds, which is one tenth of a second. At two hundred fifty nanoseconds each, they occupy one quarter of that, twenty-five million nanoseconds, which is twenty-five milliseconds. Those products are what the ambitions would imply if the ring were already at the target and nothing else were on the path. They are not what the JavaScript lab measured, and they are not what you may write under a chart unless a counter on a stated CPU produced them. The usefulness of the product is comparative. A tenth of a second and twenty-five milliseconds are long enough to notice and short enough to drown inside a slow strategy, which is the next idea. They are not a result.

The lab is a different instrument, and the lecture wants you to say the difference in the same breath as any number you show. The lab runs the protocol in JavaScript. A rate it prints is a rate of that JavaScript loop. It can teach you that a full ring drops, and it can teach you the order of the tickets. It cannot teach you that the handoff was two hundred fifty nanoseconds, because it is not the C plus plus binary on pinned metal, and it is not a warm cache on a core you named. If you show the browser number, you label it as a browser number. Nanoseconds come from a counter on the machine, or not at all. "Or not at all" is the option you will actually use until you have the counter. Leaving the number off the slide is a completed part of the assignment, not a gap you paper over with the ambition.

When you do time the C plus plus file, the report has three companions or it does not have a latency. The CPU, because the ambition was about pinned metal and a warm cache, and a different chip is a different hypothesis. The compiler flags, because optimization level three and the C plus plus twenty standard and pthread are part of the default build, and a debug build is a different program. The core pin, because a thread that wanders is not the pinned case the ambition named. If any of the three is missing, you can still say you ran the checksum. You cannot say you tested the hypothesis. The benchmark binary, when you link it, adds the define and the benchmark library. It does not add a CPU. You still write the CPU down. And if the checksum mismatches, you do not get to skip ahead to that binary and hope the timer averages the bug into something smooth.

Put the two ambitions next to a capacity so throughput and latency stop sharing a sentence. Suppose the capacity is sixteen, which is the ring you will step in the protocol hour, and suppose you could fill it at the loose ambition of one thousand nanoseconds per event. Sixteen events would take sixteen thousand nanoseconds, which is sixteen microseconds, if every push were a successful handoff at that pace and nothing stalled. At two hundred fifty nanoseconds, the same sixteen successful handoffs would take four thousand nanoseconds, four microseconds. A full ring of sixteen is a small object. The ambition is not "sixteen is fast." The ambition is a sustained pace after the consumer has been keeping up for millions of events, on metal, with a warm cache, pinned. Sixteen times two hundred fifty is only the arithmetic of one fill. One million divided by sixteen is sixty-two thousand five hundred fills. At four microseconds a fill, sixty-two thousand five hundred fills take sixty-two thousand five hundred times four microseconds. Sixty-two thousand five hundred times four is two hundred fifty thousand microseconds, which is a quarter of a second. That quarter-second is what one million events would occupy at a steady two hundred fifty nanoseconds, and it matches the earlier result that two hundred fifty nanoseconds is four million events per second, because a quarter of four million is one million. The arithmetic is consistent. It is still not a measurement. It tells you that a sixteen-slot ring is not "too small to be a million events." The consumer has to recycle each slot tens of thousands of times. The ticket publish of tail plus capacity is what makes that recycling legal. A ring that works once, for sixteen events, and then sticks, does not have a throughput hypothesis at all.

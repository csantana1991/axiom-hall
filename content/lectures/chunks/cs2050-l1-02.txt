Change one input. Keep the twelve-byte record and the sixty-four-byte line, and change the record count from eight to four. Four times twelve is forty-eight. Forty-eight is less than sixty-four, and sixty-four minus forty-eight is sixteen, so four grouped records fit on a single line with sixteen bytes spare. A loop that touches every field of those four records moves one line, and the line is mostly payload. A loop that wanted only one field still moves that same line under the grouped layout, because the field does not have its own run of addresses. The trip got smaller because the working set got smaller, not because the layout learned which field you meant. If you then switch those four records to a struct of arrays, the useful field occupies its own contiguous run. The lesson does not give you a separate width for that one field, so you do not invent a byte count for the run. You do say what the layout changed: successive useful values became successive addresses, which is the reason a tight loop often wants the separated form.

You might ask whether every array should be rewritten as a struct of arrays before you measure. No. The layout you write first is allowed to be the grouped one, and for a market event consumed as a whole record it can stay that way. Rewriting first, and measuring never, inverts the instruction. The instruction is to name the inner loop, see whether it streams one field or a whole record, and measure that loop rather than the picture of x, y, z against x, x, x. A picture can tell you which layout is a candidate. It cannot tell you the time.

What the preference for struct of arrays does not mean is that the separated layout is faster on every loop, or that a grouped market event is a mistake. It means the separated layout puts the next useful field at the next address when you stream one field. If your loop touches the whole record, the grouped layout already does that for the record as a unit. What it also does not mean is that a drawing of the two layouts is a profile. Padding you cannot see in a profiler is still padding, and a layout you have not timed is still a hypothesis. The slide’s advice is to measure the loop. Treat a confident sentence about speed, spoken from the picture alone, as a sentence you have not earned.

Work the spill again with the record count chosen so the line boundary falls in a place you can point at. Five twelve-byte records fill sixty bytes. A sixth begins at offset sixty and needs twelve bytes, but only four bytes remain in the line, so the sixth record crosses. An array of structs does not magically align your records to lines. It only groups fields. If a record crosses a line, a touch of that record can move two lines even though you named one object. That crossing is ordinary address arithmetic, not a failure of the grouped idea. You notice it by computing sizes and offsets, and you confirm it by reading the addresses, the same way you refuse to assume a width you have not checked.

Consider a loop that streams one field across a long run, and keep the claim qualitative where the lesson gives you no per-field width. In the separated layout the next value of that field is adjacent. Hardware that moves a line then brings several upcoming values of the same field, which is what you wanted. In the grouped layout the same line brings a mixture, which is what you wanted only when the mixture is the record. The phrase to keep is the one from the notes of the lecture: the right layout is the one the inner loop streams. “Often wants” is doing real work in the sentence about struct of arrays. Often is not always. A market event used as a whole record is the lecture’s own example of a grouped layout that can stay.

A second numeric change, distinct from cutting eight records down to four. Keep eight records and change the record size from twelve bytes to sixteen, the same one-input change you used when you were only counting passengers. Eight times sixteen is one hundred twenty-eight. One hundred twenty-eight divided by sixty-four is two, exactly. The grouped array fills two lines with no remainder. Five would not be the packing count anymore, because sixteen does not go into sixty-four five times. Four times sixteen is sixty-four, so each line holds four records, and two lines hold eight. If the loop reads every field, both lines are intended traffic. If the loop reads one field only, both lines are still the traffic of the grouped layout, and the separated layout would have given that field a private run whose length you would then measure instead of guessing. Same eight records, different record size, different bytes, same decision rule.

You might also ask whether a struct of arrays removes false sharing by itself. It does not. False sharing is two cores writing the same line. Separating fields into different arrays can place two hot counters far apart, or it can place them adjacent at the starts of two arrays that the linker lays next to each other. The layout changes addresses. It does not pronounce a verdict until you know whether those addresses share a line. That verdict is the next beat’s subject, under alignment, and it is why a picture of x, x, x is not yet a promise about cores.

Hold the lab’s model beside the layout choice without pretending the model knows which layout you picked. The model charges one or eight according to whether two writers share a line. A grouped layout makes sharing more likely when two cores write different fields of nearby records, because those fields are interleaved inside the same lines. A separated layout makes sharing less likely when the two hot fields live in runs that fall on different lines, and it does nothing for you when the runs still share. The charge is still a teaching penalty, not a chip. You use it to see the mechanism. You use a harness when you need a time.

What you should not conclude is that the twelve-byte market event must be split because ninety-six bytes became two lines. Two lines for eight whole records is a small working set, and if you consume the record whole, those two lines are the stream you asked for. The lecture allows that event to stay grouped. Split it when the inner loop streams one field and a measurement says the mixture hurts. Leave it when the loop wants the record. The algebra of ninety-six and sixty-four tells you how many lines the grouped walk touches. It does not tell you to rewrite the type tonight.

Say the decision in one breath you could give a classmate. You write an array of structs first. You look at the inner loop. If it touches the whole record, you may keep the grouped form, and a market event is the example already on the slide. If it streams one field, a struct of arrays puts the next useful value at the next address, which is why a tight loop often wants it. Time that loop before you trust the layout. You do not argue from the picture. And you do not treat either layout as a cure for two cores writing one line until you have looked at the addresses.

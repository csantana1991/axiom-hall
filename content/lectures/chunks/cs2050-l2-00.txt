Two counters on one line are enough to make a fast algorithm look slow. In the book you are building, the producer writes head and the consumer writes tail. Those are different variables, written by different sides, and if the atomics that hold them share a cache line, each side invalidates the other on every update. The algorithm can be lock-free and still slow, because the coherence protocol is the lock you claimed not to take.

Name the actors before you name the penalty. One side publishes new work by moving head. The other side retires work by moving tail. Nothing in that division requires the two addresses to be neighbors. They are neighbors only if you laid them out that way. A line is commonly sixty-four bytes. Two counters that sit a few bytes apart fall inside one such line. Every store to head then threatens the consumer’s copy of that line, and every store to tail threatens the producer’s copy. The logical protocol may be wait-free of locks. The line is still a single piece of coherence state.

Use the lab’s model so the invalidation has a number you are allowed to quote. The cache lab is not a processor. It charges one when two writers sit on different lines, and eight when they share one line. The eight is a teaching penalty so the toggle does something visible. Head and tail are the two counters the brief tells you to keep in mind. When they share, you quote eight. When padding puts them on different lines, you quote one. Eight divided by one is eight, which matches the brief’s sentence that a shared line costs eight times two private lines. You say “model” in the same breath as eight. You do not publish eight as a cycle count.

Run the model on a concrete number of updates. Suppose the producer and the consumer each complete one thousand updates, and you score the pair the way the lab scores the pair: one charge when the counters sit apart, eight when they share. The apart case totals one thousand model units. The shared case totals eight thousand. The extra seven thousand is not extra messages in your protocol. It is the teaching penalty for co-location. Change one input, the update count, from one thousand to two hundred fifty. The apart case costs two hundred fifty. The shared case costs two thousand. The ratio is still eight. Change the penalty instead, from eight down to two, and leave the updates at one thousand. The shared case costs two thousand, the apart case costs one thousand, and the gap shrinks to one thousand. The layout problem does not shrink with it. Only the classroom constant moved.

You might ask whether lock-free means the updates no longer interfere. It does not. Lock-free, in the sense this lecture uses, means someone is always making progress if the operating system lets a thread run. It does not mean uncontended. It does not mean wait-free. A push can fail and retry. Two threads can be lock-free and still bounce a line on every store, because the coherence protocol does not consult your progress theorem before it invalidates. The theorem is about forward progress in the algorithm. The bounce is about who owns the line. You can have the first and still pay the second.

What the slowdown claim does not mean is that every pair of atomics must be padded, or that the number eight is what your chip will report. The eight exists so the toggle is obvious. On metal, the related experiment is two threads incrementing adjacent counters, with and without padding, under a proper harness. The course numbered 6.172 assigns versions of that experiment. Your capstone still has to be timed on the machine you claim. What the claim also does not mean is that head and tail are the same variable. They are different writes. The failure is that different writes landed on one line. Distinct names in the source did not create distinct lines.

Look at the fix the capstone actually writes, so the counters are not an abstract pair. The cell is aligned to sixty-four. head and tail are each aligned to sixty-four. That is the false-sharing fix for the counters. Alignment to sixty-four makes the address a multiple of sixty-four, and for an aligned object the language makes sizeof a multiple of the alignment, so the next object can start on a fresh line. If head begins at address zero, a sizeof of sixty-four puts tail’s start at sixty-four. Those two starts are different lines of the common width. The model then charges one, not eight. If you forgot the alignment and the two counters sat at offset zero and offset eight, both offsets live in the line that runs from zero through sixty-three, and the model charges eight. The source difference is a layout difference of eight bytes versus sixty-four. The charge difference, in the model, is one versus eight.

Take a smaller update count and include a failed push, so retry does not stay verbal. Suppose the producer attempts one hundred twenty pushes and twenty of them fail and retry, while the consumer completes one hundred successful pops. The lesson does not give you a separate charge for a failed attempt. It does tell you that a push can fail and retry, and that failure is part of not being wait-free. The stores that do reach head and tail are what hit the line. If those stores share a line, each of them pays the shared tariff in the model, whether or not the logical operation ultimately committed. You do not get to call the bounce free because the algorithm had a retry loop. The retry loop is evidence that progress is conditional. The line is evidence that the counters were neighbors.

Hold the boundary in one place before you move on. Sharing a line is a layout fact. Lock-free is a progress fact. The lab maps the layout fact onto one and eight so you can see it. The capstone separates head and tail by aligning each to sixty-four. None of that separation is a claim about payload bytes inside a cell, which is the next question, and none of it authorizes you to print the teaching eight as if a harness had produced it.

Pad the indexes, not the sermon. Head and tail go on separate lines because both are hot and both are written, by different cores, under the same coherence protocol. The cell is a different pattern. Its sequence and its payload may share a line. The protocol has one writer of the payload at a time, then a release, then the other side reads. That is not two hot counters. Do not pad the payload until a profile says the cell is hot.

Keep the two patterns in separate sentences so you do not “fix” the quiet one with the medicine meant for the loud one. Head is written by the producer on the cadence of new work. Tail is written by the consumer on the cadence of retirement. Those two writes interleave for the whole run. A cell’s payload is written by one side, published, and later read by the other side. The line that holds the payload changes hands across a release, rather than being stored by both cores on every operation. Sharing that line is ordinary publication. Sharing the line that holds head and tail is false sharing. The capstone struct follows the distinction: head and tail are each aligned to sixty-four, and the cell is aligned to sixty-four as well, so a cell starts on a line boundary. Alignment of the cell is about where consecutive cells land. It is not an invitation to split sequence from payload before a profile asks you to.

Predict zero needs a loss so it does not stay a slogan, and so cancellation cannot masquerade as skill. Suppose the label is a simple return, and the five test labels, as an illustration, are one hundredth, negative two hundredths, one hundredth, three hundredths, and negative one hundredth. Predict zero. The squared errors are one ten-thousandth, four ten-thousandths, one ten-thousandth, nine ten-thousandths, and one ten-thousandth. The sum is sixteen ten-thousandths, which is zero point zero zero one six. The mean squared error is that sum divided by five, which is zero point zero zero zero three two. That number is the hurdle on this illustration. A model with a larger mean squared error has not beaten predict zero before costs. A model with a smaller one has beaten the baseline only before costs.

Put a cost in the score so the second half of the sentence is doing work. Suppose you froze a cost of one hundredth on every test decision, in the same units as the return, and you have five decisions. This cost is a supposition for the arithmetic, not a measured fee. Five times one hundredth is five hundredths. Suppose the model's summed signed edge on the five decisions, before cost, is three hundredths. After cost, three hundredths minus five hundredths is negative two hundredths. The model beat predict zero on a raw squared-error story you might tell, or it might not, but on this costed sum it is behind doing nothing, if doing nothing pays no cost and earns no edge. The checklist does not let you report the three hundredths and mention the cost in a footnote you do not subtract. Costs in the score means the number you quote is after the subtraction. If the model cannot beat predict zero after costs, the attention was a picture. A picture can be a correct picture of a weighted average and still be a picture rather than a trade.

Write the cut you would have used if the six lab numbers had been a week, even though the lab has no fit and therefore no split to run. Six days, indexes zero through five. Declare T zero at day zero, T one at day four, and T two at day six. Training would be days zero, one, two, and three. Test would be days four and five. Suppose the label horizon is one day, and the label on a decision uses the next day's move. The decision on day three, the last training day, has a label that uses day four, and day four is test. Embargo at least that horizon drops day three from training, and it drops any earlier day whose label still reaches day four. With a one-day horizon, day three is the overlapping decision, so the last kept training decision is day two, whose next day is day three, still inside training. Test remains days four and five. You have just written a cut, an embargo, and a horizon. You have not fit a model. The lab still has no split, because a split you would have used is not a split you ran. Writing it is the exercise. Reporting a performance number on days four and five, from a weighted average that was not a forecast, would still be a joke.

Apply the hurdle to a model number so the baseline is a comparison and not only a definition. The predict-zero mean squared error on the illustration was zero point zero zero zero three two. Suppose a candidate forecast, still an illustration, produces squared errors whose mean is zero point zero zero zero two. That is smaller than zero point zero zero zero three two, so on squared error, before costs, the candidate beats predict zero. The margin is zero point zero zero zero three two minus zero point zero zero zero two, which is zero point zero zero zero one two. Now put the same one-hundredth cost on each of the five decisions, five hundredths in total, against a summed signed edge of three hundredths. The costed result is still negative two hundredths. Beating the squared-error hurdle did not survive the cost. The checklist is a conjunction. Timestamps, a training-only scaler, a written horizon, an embargo at least that horizon, costs inside the score, and a win over predict zero after those costs. Fail the cost and the attention was a picture, even if the squared error looked better than guessing a flat zero.

If these six lab numbers were a week and you also had a label, the cut you wrote, train through day two after the embargo, test on days four and five, is where that comparison would have to live. You would score predict zero on days four and five only, after costs, and you would not tune the temperature on those two days and then quote them. The lab does not give you the label. Writing the cut is not permission to invent the label's Sharpe ratio. It is permission to see where a Sharpe ratio would have been illegal.

The question to close is whether careful wording could turn that smoothed copy into a Sharpe ratio. No. If someone quotes a Sharpe ratio from this lab, they are joking or they are wrong. A Sharpe ratio is a claim about excess return over a scale of volatility, and this lab does not predict returns. It does not have a position, a costed profit, or a clock of trades. The readout is a smoothed copy of past toy imbalances. You can compute that copy by hand. You cannot divide it by a volatility and obtain a performance statistic, because you never formed the excess return the ratio needs. Careful wording does not help. The missing object is the return, not the adjective.

What the claim does not mean is that attention is useless, or that a later model which does predict returns is forbidden to use a causal weighted average as one of its inputs. The prohibition is on this lab, and on any quote that treats the lab as a backtest. A later model faces the full checklist: timestamps, a training-only scaler, a written horizon, an embargo at least that long, costs inside the score, and a predict-zero baseline it has to beat after those costs. If it fails that list, the attention inside it was a picture, even if the surrounding network was large. The claim also does not mean that keeping the mask is optional once the story is causal in your head. Turn the mask off in your head so you can see that future weights would light up. Keep it on in the lab. The head version is how you understand the cheat. The lab version is how you refuse to commit it.

When you leave, you should be able to say four negatives without looking down. The lab is not a transformer you trained. It is not a return model. It is not a split, because it is not a fit. It is not a source of a Sharpe ratio. What it is, is a weighted sum you can recompute, under a mask, on six numbers, with a cut written beside it in case those six numbers are ever promoted into a week. Promotion is a new experiment. It is not a reinterpretation of the picture you already have.

The sentence you should be able to say without a drawing is the contract, not a product name. One pass, because the tape does not rewind and the decision may precede the next packet. State smaller than the stream, ideally polylogarithmic, because the stream is too long to store. A sketch whose size you chose. An alphabet of eight, small enough that five arrivals, or seven, can be checked by eye, and still processed as if you did not have that privilege. Exact storage only if you admit the alphabet is tiny and you are keeping a histogram. Failure probability reserved for the bound, which is next, and not waved away because the five happened to look plausible.

Approximation with a guarantee is the part that makes a sketch a streaming algorithm rather than a compression trick. You want a bound. With probability at least one minus delta, the estimate sits within epsilon of the truth, in a norm you named. Space then scales like the bound, not like the stream. An algorithm that returns a pretty number is not, by itself, a streaming algorithm. Prettiness has no delta in it.

Read the bound as three choices you owe the listener before you owe them a diagram. First, the norm. Within epsilon of the truth means nothing until you say whether you are talking about a point frequency, a vector of frequencies, or some other distance. Second, epsilon, the slack you are willing to tolerate in that norm. Third, delta, the probability you allow yourself to be wrong. One minus delta is the probability you are promising to be right. If you omit delta, you have stated a certainty the analysis does not give you. The slide is blunt: failure probability is part of the answer.

Put numbers in the three slots so the sentence cannot hide. Let epsilon be one tenth, and let delta be one tenth. Then the promise is: with probability at least nine tenths, the estimate sits within one tenth of the truth, in the norm you named when you chose epsilon. Nine tenths is one minus one tenth. The sketch’s memory is whatever the analysis says this epsilon and this delta require. It is not a function of how many packets were in the file. If the tape doubles and epsilon and delta stay put, the space stays put, under a guarantee of that form. That is the sense in which space scales like the bound and not like the stream. Little-o of the length, ideally polylogarithmic, is the shape you were already promised. Epsilon and delta are how you pick a point on that shape.

Change one input. Keep epsilon at one tenth and change delta from one tenth to one hundredth. One minus delta is now ninety-nine hundredths. You are asking to be right more often. You should expect the sketch to spend more state on that request, because depth, in the structure you will meet next, is the parameter that buys a smaller failure probability. This beat does not yet cash the formula that turns one hundredth into a row count. It does say the direction. A smaller delta is a stronger promise, and stronger promises are not free. If someone offers you the same sketch for both deltas, they have not changed the guarantee. They have only changed the sentence.

You might ask whether a demonstration that matched the truth on all eight futures roots has set delta to zero. It has not. Eight roots are small enough to know the truth, and a match on a toy tape is one tape. The guarantee is a probability over the algorithm’s randomness, or over the hashes it drew, for a bound you stated. A single matching afternoon does not drive delta to zero, and it does not let you delete epsilon from the claim. Comparing estimate to truth is the privilege of the toy. The privilege lets you see an overestimate. It does not promote the overestimate into a calibrated epsilon. The lab grid you will use later is a picture, and this lecture already warns you that pictures are not the bound. Here, the warning is earlier and simpler: do not read a successful audit as a proof that failure became impossible.

What the guarantee does not mean is that every estimate is within epsilon, full stop. The phrase “at least one minus delta” is doing the work. On some draws you are outside the slack, and that is not a broken theorem. It is the delta you budgeted. What it also does not mean is that epsilon is a relative error, or an additive error, until you name the norm. “Within epsilon of the truth” is incomplete on purpose in the opening statement. You complete it by naming the norm. A point-frequency norm and a norm on the whole vector are different promises, and a sketch built for one is the wrong tool for the other. What it also does not mean is that you may add epsilon to a pretty printout after looking at the truth. You state epsilon and delta when you size the sketch, before the tape flatters you.

Connect the bound back to the five arrivals, without pretending five is a general epsilon. The truth is five. An estimate of five sits at distance zero, which is inside any positive epsilon you named. An estimate of six sits at distance one. Whether one is “within epsilon” depends on the norm. If the norm is the raw count, distance one is within epsilon only if epsilon is at least one. If the norm scales by the length of the stream, a distance of one after a long tape is a small fraction, and epsilon might cover it. You do not get to say “close enough” in place of that sentence. After five identical arrivals and nothing else, the length is five. A later mix lengthens the tape and changes the fraction. The norm you named has to be stable enough that those two situations are not described by the same casual word.

Take the nine-tenths promise and ask what a hundred independent sketches would owe you, as arithmetic on the probability, not as a new theorem. If each sketch fails with probability at most one tenth, you do not add the tenths and declare that ten sketches must fail. The bound is per sketch, as stated. A hundred sketches each promising nine tenths are a hundred promises, not a single fused probability, unless you do the combination carefully and state it. The lecture does not ask you to fuse them. It asks you not to drop delta. If you report one number from one sketch, the delta that belongs next to it is the delta you sized, for example one tenth, and the probability you claim is nine tenths, not certainty.

Change the epsilon instead of the delta, so both knobs have moved once. Let delta stay at one tenth, and let epsilon go from one tenth to one twentieth. You are asking for a tighter neighborhood of the truth, with the same failure probability. Space has to answer that request. Width, in the structure that is coming, is the parameter that buys a smaller additive error. Again you are not cashing the ceiling formula yet. You are noticing that epsilon moved and the sketch is supposed to move with it. A picture with a fixed grid does not become a one-twentieth sketch because you wanted a tighter number on the day. The lab will eventually tell you its grid is too small to cash the bound. Believe that when you get there. For now, believe the weaker version: the bound, not the tape length and not your hope, sets the size.

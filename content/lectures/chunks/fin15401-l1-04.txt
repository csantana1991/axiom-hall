The board in front of you is titled the objective you can differentiate. Minimize one half of w transpose capital sigma w, subject to w transpose times the vector of ones equal to 1, and, if a target return is required, w transpose mu equal to mu sub p. The one half is cosmetic. It cancels the 2 that appears when you differentiate a quadratic. Boyd writes this as a quadratic program. For two assets you do not need a solver. Two equalities and two assets pin the weight down. With more assets, the target-return slice has a free subspace. That freedom is what a long-only book spends on constraints.

Hear the cosmetic factor on a derivative you already took. On the previous board the derivative of variance, not of half variance, was twice a linear function of w, and setting it to zero was the same as setting the linear function to zero. Minimize half the variance and the 2 and the one half cancel before you even reach the equation. The root does not move. At the equal-volatility example, both standard deviations 0.20 and rho equal to 0.50, the variance at weight one half is 0.03, and the derivative of variance with respect to w is 0 there, so the derivative of half the variance is also 0. You did not buy a different portfolio by writing the one half. You bought a cleaner gradient, capital sigma times w, when the covariance matrix is symmetric. That is the only reason for the fraction.

For two assets and a target mean, skip the solver and solve the line. Let the means be 0.08 and 0.14. Then w times 0.08 plus the quantity 1 minus w times 0.14 equals the target. That simplifies to 0.14 minus 0.06 w. So w equals 0.14 minus the target, all divided by 0.06. A target of 0.08 forces w equal to 1. A target of 0.10 forces w equal to two thirds. A target of 0.11 forces w equal to one half. A target of 0.14 forces w equal to 0. A target of 0.17 forces w equal to negative one half, because 0.17 sits above both means and the only way to a higher mean is a short of the lower-mean asset together with a long above 1 in the higher-mean asset. Two equalities, two unknowns, one weight. There is no search.

Attach a covariance and the pinned weight has a variance, which is the objective's value even though you did not iterate. Let the standard deviations be 0.20 and 0.25, and let rho equal 0.20. The covariance c is 0.20 times 0.20 times 0.25, which is 0.01. The variances are 0.04 and 0.0625. First find the global minimum-variance weight with no return target, using the formula you already trust. The numerator is 0.0625 minus 0.01, which is 0.0525. The denominator is 0.04 plus 0.0625 minus 0.02, which is 0.0825. The weight is 0.0525 divided by 0.0825, which is 7 over 11, about 0.636. The mean that falls out, with no one having targeted it, is about 0.636 times 0.08 plus about 0.364 times 0.14. Compute it as 7 over 11 times 0.08 plus 4 over 11 times 0.14, which is 0.56 over 11 plus 0.56 over 11, which is 1.12 over 11, which is about 0.1018. The variance at that weight is about 0.02909, and the standard deviation is about 0.1706.

Now impose targets and watch the variance rise as you leave that mean. At a target of 0.10 the weight is two thirds, close to 7 over 11. The variance is two thirds squared times 0.04, plus one third squared times 0.0625, plus 2 times two thirds times one third times 0.01. That is 4 over 9 times 0.04, plus 1 over 9 times 0.0625, plus 4 over 9 times 0.01. Compute 0.16 over 9, plus 0.0625 over 9, plus 0.04 over 9. The numerator is 0.2625 and the denominator is 9, so the variance is 0.029167. The standard deviation is about 0.1708. You paid a little variance, relative to 0.02909, to move the mean from about 0.1018 down to 0.10. The payment is small because the target is close. It is not zero, because the target is not the minimum-variance mean.

At a target of 0.08 the weight is 1, the variance is 0.04, and the standard deviation is 0.20. At a target of 0.11 the weight is one half. The variance is one half squared times 0.04, plus one half squared times 0.0625, plus 2 times one half times one half times 0.01, which is 0.01 plus 0.015625 plus 0.005, which is 0.030625. The standard deviation is 0.175. At a target of 0.14 the weight is 0, the variance is 0.0625, and the standard deviation is 0.25. At a target of 0.17 the weight on the first asset is negative one half and the weight on the second is 1.5. That portfolio is feasible for the unconstrained problem and illegal for a long-only mandate. Its mean is above every long-only mean. The objective can print it. The mandate has to refuse it in a separate sentence.

The one-half in front of the quadratic did not choose any of these weights. The return constraint did, together with the requirement that weights sum to 1. With two assets those two equalities leave a zero-dimensional slice. The weight is pinned. With three assets the same two equalities would leave one free direction, a subspace of portfolios that all hit the target mean and all sum to 1, and only then would the quadratic have something to minimize inside the slice. That freedom is what a long-only book spends on constraints. Each bound you add, a weight at least zero, a weight at most a limit, a sector cap, uses up freedom the two-asset problem never had. Boyd's quadratic program is the sentence you write when the freedom and the inequalities are both present. On two assets with only equalities, the program collapses to the arithmetic you just did, and bringing a solver to it would only hide the pin.

The lab's instruction fits this board. Drag correlation first. The shape of the bullet is the lecture. The marked point is the global minimum-variance mix, shorts allowed. A target-return point is a different mark. It sits on the same curve, higher variance unless you happened to target the minimum-variance mean, and it can leave the long-only arc when the target leaves the range of the two means. If you drag rho toward 1, the interior collapses and the target may become expensive in variance for a boring reason: there is less and less room between the endpoints. If you drag rho toward negative 1, a very small standard deviation becomes available somewhere on the curve. That somewhere is still algebra until the borrow, the basis, and the gap risk are named.

A three-asset sketch shows the free direction without pretending you need a solver for the two-asset pin. Suppose three assets are uncorrelated, with variances 0.01, 0.04, and 0.09, and the only constraint is that the weights sum to 1. With no covariance terms, the global minimum-variance weights are proportional to the reciprocals of the variances. Those reciprocals are 100, 25, and 100 divided by 9. Their sum is 1225 divided by 9. The normalized weights are 36 over 49, 9 over 49, and 4 over 49. About 0.735, 0.184, and 0.082. The quiet asset takes most of the book, and the noisiest asset takes the least, which is the same instinct as the two-asset weight 0.90 on the quieter name, now split across three diagonal entries. The variance of this mix is 9 over 1225, and the standard deviation is 3 over 35, about 0.0857. No target return was imposed. The mean, whatever the three means are, falls out after the weights are chosen.

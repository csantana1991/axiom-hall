A schedule is a list of steps, not a mood. Suppose you take the three deviations in order and you halve the step each time, starting at 0.1, and you start at the minimizer so the only motion is the kick. The first step uses 0.1 on the deviation negative 3 and negative 1.8 and lands at 1.3 and negative 0.22, function value 0.1872, as before. The second step uses 0.05 on the next deviation, which is 0 and 0.6. From 1.3 and negative 0.22 you subtract 0.05 times 0 in x and 0.05 times 0.6 in y, so you land at 1.3 and negative 0.25. You have not repaired the x error of 0.3, because that draw had no x component and the step was spent on y. The third step uses 0.025 on the deviation 3 and 1.2. You subtract 0.075 from x and 0.03 from y, landing at 1.225 and negative 0.28. The kicks are getting shorter. The early kick of size 0.3 in x is still mostly there, because later steps are too small to walk it back in one draw, and this draw only removed 0.075 of it. Decay can recover a point, and it can recover it slowly, and a schedule that falls too fast can freeze you near a place the early noise chose. The lesson's sentence is the slow recovery, not a promise that any decreasing sequence is wise.

That last calculation is also why a constant step and a decaying step answer different questions. With the constant step 0.1, each visit near the minimizer is followed by a kick of the same typical size, so the iteration keeps a radius. With a decaying step, those kicks get smaller, so a point estimate becomes conceivable, and the time you wait for the late, tiny steps to finish the noiseless part of the walk gets longer. Proofs that claim the iterate itself goes to the minimizer have a schedule in them. If the writeup you are reading shows a constant step and a single limiting point, something in the argument is averaging the iterates, or changing the step, or using a hypothesis this lesson did not state. Do not fill that gap with optimism. Name the schedule or name the ball.

You might ask whether the ball is an artifact of starting with large deviations. Change one input, the step, which we already shrunk, or change one deviation and recompute the kick. Replace the first deviation by negative 1 and negative 0.6, and replace the third by 1 and 0.6, leaving the middle at 0 and 0.6. The mean of the x deviations is negative 1 plus 0 plus 1, which is 0. The mean of the y deviations is negative 0.6 plus 0.6 plus 0.6, which is 0.6, not 0. That repair is required before you trust the example. The middle y deviation has to come down. Set the middle y deviation to 0 so the y mean is negative 0.6 plus 0 plus 0.6, which is 0. The new deviations are negative 1 and negative 0.6, then 0 and 0, then 1 and 0.6. They average to zero. At the minimizer, with eta equals 0.1, the three landings are 1.1 and negative 0.34, then 1 and negative 0.4, then 0.9 and negative 0.46. One of the three draws now leaves you exactly on the minimizer, because that deviation was the zero vector. The other two still kick you to function values of the square of 0.1 plus 3 times the square of 0.06, which is 0.01 plus 3 times 0.0036, which is 0.01 plus 0.0108, which is 0.0208. The ball is smaller than the ball from the deviations of size 3, and it is not a single point, because two of the three draws move. Cutting the deviation cut the radius. It did not change the logic of a constant step.

The lab stays full-batch on purpose while you absorb this. Its gradient at a point is the gradient, not a draw. If the path on the lab is running away, the sign or the step is wrong, and noise is not available as an excuse, because you have not added any. Add noise only after that path is heading toward 1 and negative 0.4. The noiseless reference at step size 0.1, after one step from the origin, sits at 0.2 and negative 0.24 with function value 0.7168, and the later noiseless steps keep multiplying the x error by 0.8 and the y error by 0.4. A stochastic path that you cannot compare to that reference is a path you cannot debug. When you later minibatch a real loss, keep this picture as the zero-noise reference: same update direction, no deviation, a point you can sit on once you reach it.

What the claim does not mean needs the same care as the claim. It does not mean constant-step stochastic descent fails to be useful. A small ball around a good parameter can be the object you wanted, especially if the noiseless walk was already an empirical-risk walk rather than a walk on the true risk. It does not mean the radius is the number 0.1872. That was the function value of one draw with one step and one list of deviations. A different step, or the repaired deviations, produced 0.0208. The claim is the existence of a neighborhood that does not collapse while eta stays put, not a universal radius. It does not mean decaying the step removes the need for the unbiased index. A schedule multiplies whatever gradient you hand it. If the index is stuck on one example, you are descending a different function, very precisely, and a tiny late step will sit near that example's minimizer rather than near the minimizer of the average. It does not mean the full-gradient theory's step-size ceiling is automatically the stochastic ceiling. On this bowl the noiseless ceiling was one third, from the curvature 6. Stochastic noise can demand a shorter step than the curvature alone, because the kick is eta times a vector that does not vanish at the minimizer. The lesson does not give you a second formula for that shorter step. It gives you the qualitative split: constant step, ball; decaying step, a point, slowly.

The idea in this block is that a minibatch drawn from a financial time series is not automatically a sample from the distribution you will trade, and that the word random does not license a shuffled tape. Shuffling leaks the future into the batch if you are careless in one direction, and it destroys dependence if you are careless in the other. A scrambled path is not a sample from the trading distribution. Walk-forward splits belong to the sequence-model course. In this course the obligation is narrower and stricter: report the split before the loss.

The theory you just used treated the index as uniform on a list of examples that enter symmetrically. That symmetry is a hypothesis. Independent and identically distributed examples satisfy it. A tape of prices in time does not become a list of that kind because you want a stochastic-gradient theorem to apply. If you permute the tape, you may create a list that looks exchangeable inside the optimizer and that could not have been a training set on the day any one of its members was still in the future. The gradient at an early time then contains a later outcome. That is leakage. If instead you refuse to permute and you also pretend the examples are independent, you have the other failure: the dependence that makes the next outcome resemble the recent ones is the dependence your variance formula just dropped. The remark that variance falls like 1 over batch size needed weak dependence. A strongly dependent tape does not owe you that factor.

The question to answer is why the iteration count has to match, rather than letting the consumer pop as fast as it can in an unstructured second thread. Unstructured popping can be correct and still be a bad measurement. If the consumer is faster and blocks inside a pop you redefined to wait, you are timing the wait. If the consumer is slower and the producer pushes without a bound, the ring fills and you are back to timing false returns. Matching the iterations, one push and one pop, keeps the occupancy from trending upward with the sample. It does not remove startup jitter, which is why the lecture says "aside from startup jitter" instead of "the ring never observes a full slot." The match is a design of the experiment. It is not a property the ring enforces for you.

What the claim does not mean is that a paired Google Benchmark run is automatically a sub-two-hundred-fifty-nanosecond result, or that use-real-time converts JavaScript into metal. The pairing fixes the push-only bug. It does not grant the hypothesis. You still need the CPU, the flags, and the pin, and you still need a counter rather than a browser. The claim also does not mean that a failed push during startup jitter is a protocol failure. It is the full test working, during a head start the capacity could not absorb. Yield, let the pop happen, and keep the sample only if you understand which attempts were refusals. If you cannot say whether a given iteration pushed successfully, you do not yet have a latency sample. You have a mixture, and a mixture is not a mean you may publish as the handoff.

The last idea of the hour is about the instrument in front of you. JavaScript is a different instrument. The lab's burst mode runs the protocol on one thread and counts drops when the producer outruns the consumer. Run the burst with pressure above one and watch the drops. That is the push-only bug, slowed down. Then set the pressure to one and read a JavaScript rate that you will not call a hardware latency.

One thread cannot be the paired experiment, and the lab does not pretend to be. Burst mode interleaves the protocol on a single thread and, when the pressure is above one, lets the producer outrun the consumer. The lab brief does not ask you to treat a particular pressure reading as a calibrated ratio from a paper. It asks you to raise the pressure above one and watch the drops, then to set it to one. Above one, the producer gets ahead, the ring fills, further pushes return false, and the lab counts those drops. The drops are the failed pushes. You are watching the naive benchmark in slow motion, with a counter of refusals instead of a timer wrapped around an infinite spin. A producer that is not matched by a consumer does not have a throughput. It has a growing list of events that never entered.

Pressure one is the other setting, and it is the closest the single thread comes to the paired iteration. The producer does not outrun the consumer. Occupancy does not trend to the capacity from the pressure itself. You may then read a rate, in operations per second, of that JavaScript loop. The rate is real for the loop. It includes whatever the browser does between the steps. It is irrelevant to the C plus plus target. The target was a hypothesis about pinned metal, a warm cache, and the binary you compile with optimization level three, the C plus plus twenty standard, and pthread. The lab was never that binary. Print both sentences if you show the number. Sentence one: this is how fast the JavaScript loop ran. Sentence two: this is not a hardware latency and not the C plus plus handoff. One sentence without the other is how a browser number becomes a fabricated nanosecond. Dividing one billion by the JavaScript rate yields a quotient in nanoseconds that still belongs to the browser. You may not move the quotient onto the metal slide.

Connect the drop count to the checksum so the burst cannot be shrugged off as a display detail. The formula for a complete run still stands. The sum of identifiers zero through N minus one is N times the quantity N minus one, divided by two. For eight identifiers and no drops, that sum is twenty-eight. If the burst dropped two of them, the consumer's sum is the sum of the six that entered, and comparing that short sum with twenty-eight fails the equality. The failure is what the checksum is for. A drop is a push that returned false. The event stayed out. It is not, by itself, a torn cell. You will separate tear from drop in the defense. Here, you respect the drops the burst shows when the pressure is above one, and you do not relabel them as throughput. The lab is not being assigned a specific drop total here, because a single thread can interleave the steps in more than one legal order. The brief's demand is directional. Pressure above one produces drops you can watch. Pressure one does not ask you to call the resulting rate a hardware latency.

A pressure of one can still drop if the single thread clumps pushes by accident, and you should not be surprised into relabeling the rate. The brief's contrast is directional rather than a promise that pressure one is a mathematical proof of zero drops. If you see drops at pressure one, say that you see them, and do not call the rate a clean handoff rate. If you see no drops, the rate is still a JavaScript rate. Either way you print both sentences before the number leaves your mouth. The C plus plus side of the comparison remains the paired benchmark, two threads, one push and one pop per iteration, checksum living in the other build. Nothing you toggle in the browser edits that file. If the temptation is to copy the JavaScript operations per second into the same table as a hypothesis of four million events per second, notice that four million per second was one billion nanoseconds divided by two hundred fifty, an arithmetic restatement of the ambition, not a cell in a table of results. A result has a counter. An ambition has a sentence. The lab has a third thing, an instrument reading, and it stays in its own column.

The question to answer is whether a JavaScript rate at pressure one, if it looks very fast, could at least be a lower bound on the C plus plus rate. It could not, not as a claim you are allowed to make. A lower bound is a theorem about the same program or a proved relation between two programs. These are different languages, different runtimes, and different schedulers. The JavaScript loop can be slower than the binary, and a particular browser moment can also be unrepresentative in either direction relative to a machine you have not pinned. The lecture does not give you an inequality. It gives you two sentences. The operations per second are real for that JavaScript loop and irrelevant to the C plus plus target. Relevance would require the C plus plus measurement. Until you have it, the JavaScript rate does not bound it, does not estimate it, and does not replace it.

Push this same pair toward rho equal to negative 1. The covariance c equals negative 0.03. The weight is 0.09 minus negative 0.03, divided by 0.01 plus 0.09 minus 2 times negative 0.03. That is 0.12 divided by 0.16, which is 0.75. The variance at three quarters is exactly 0, because 0.75 times 0.10 equals 0.25 times 0.30. The long and the short legs of the volatility match, and perfect negative correlation makes them cancel. The mean at that weight is 0.75 times 0.06 plus 0.25 times 0.12, which is 0.045 plus 0.030, which is 0.075. You have a portfolio with mean 0.075 and standard deviation 0 in the model. Say the next sentence with the notes, not instead of them. This is algebra, not a tradable hedge, until the borrow, the basis, and the gap risk have names. A zero on this board is a zero of the quadratic form. It is not a confirmation that the trade can be held.

One intermediate correlation keeps the minimum inside the long-only interval and shows the pinch without a zero. At rho equal to negative 0.50, c equals negative 0.015. The weight is 0.09 plus 0.015, divided by 0.10 plus 0.030, which is 0.105 divided by 0.130, which is 21 over 26, about 0.808. The other weight is about 0.192. The variance is about 0.00519, and the standard deviation is about 0.072, well under the quieter asset, and well above the zero you got at rho equal to negative 1. Dragging correlation from 0 to negative one half moved the minimum from weight 0.90 and standard deviation about 0.095 to weight about 0.81 and standard deviation about 0.072. The bullet opened. Dragging the other way will close it, and it will also push the critical point past a weight of 1. That crossing is the next board's entire subject. I will compute it once here so the hyperbola is finished, and then we will refuse to misname it.

At rho equal to 0.50, c equals 0.015. The numerator is 0.09 minus 0.015, which is 0.075. The denominator is 0.10 minus 0.030, which is 0.070. The weight is 0.075 divided by 0.070, which is 15 over 14, about 1.071. The second weight is negative 1 over 14, about negative 0.071. The critical point is a short of the noisier asset. The variance there is about 0.00964, standard deviation about 0.098, a hair under the long-only endpoint at asset 1. At rho equal to 1, c equals 0.03, and the formula gives weight 1.5 on the first asset and negative 0.5 on the second, with variance 0 again, another algebraic cancellation that requires a short. Restrict yourself to weights between 0 and 1 and the cancellation disappears. The long-only standard deviations at rho equal to 1 are a straight march: at weight 0, standard deviation 0.30. At weight 0.25, 0.25. At weight one half, 0.20. At weight 0.75, 0.15. At weight 1, 0.10. There is no dip. The interior of the bullet has collapsed. Means still run from 0.12 down to 0.06. The segment slopes, in the sense that the change in mean over the change in standard deviation, from the noisy end to the quiet end, is negative 0.06 divided by negative 0.20, which is 0.30. A line segment with slope 0.30 is the whole long-only feasible set. Nothing inside it diversifies.

Hold the weight fixed and drag only rho, so the cross term is isolated from the movement of the minimum. Stay with standard deviations 0.10 and 0.30, and set w equal to one half, which is not the minimum for any of these correlations except by accident. The variance simplifies. One half squared times 0.01 is 0.0025. One half squared times 0.09 is 0.0225. Those two pieces sum to 0.025 no matter what rho is. The cross term is 2 times one half times one half times c, and c is rho times 0.03, so the cross term is 0.015 times rho. Variance equals 0.025 plus 0.015 rho. At rho equal to 1, variance is 0.040 and the standard deviation is 0.20, which matches the line-segment value at weight one half. At rho equal to one half, variance is 0.0325 and the standard deviation is the square root of 0.0325, about 0.180. At rho equal to 0, variance is 0.025 and the standard deviation is about 0.158. At rho equal to negative one half, variance is 0.0175 and the standard deviation is about 0.132. At rho equal to negative 1, variance is 0.010 and the standard deviation is 0.10. The same holdings, five different risks, and the only input that changed was the correlation inside the cross term. Notice that rho equal to negative 1 did not produce a zero at this weight. The zero lived at weight 0.75. An even split still has standard deviation 0.10 when the assets are perfectly opposed, because 0.50 times 0.10 and 0.50 times 0.30 do not match. Diversification at a weight you happened to like is not the same event as the minimum.

You might ask whether the curve is always a hyperbola, including the piece a long-only mandate actually holds. The unconstrained weight, allowed to leave the unit interval, traces the hyperbola, or a degenerate line when the correlation hits an endpoint that flattens the quadratic. A mandate that keeps w between 0 and 1 keeps an arc of that curve, or a single endpoint when the critical point has left the interval. The arc is not a different theorem. It is a restricted picture of the same quadratic. Calling the arc the whole feasible set of the algebra pretends the short was never a critical point. The lab marks the critical point even when it is a short. That mark is the hyperbola's minimum, not the mandate's minimum.

Here is what the shape does not mean. A bullet that pinches when rho rises does not mean the assets became riskier one at a time. In the unequal example their standard deviations stayed 0.10 and 0.30 while rho moved from 0 to 1. The cross term changed. The endpoints stayed put, and the interior filled in until it was a segment. A bullet that reaches standard deviation 0 does not mean you have found a riskless trade you can show a client. It means the quadratic form hit zero at a weight the correlation made available. And a plotted curve does not mean every weight on it is a recommendation. It means those weights are feasible for the two-asset model. The minimum-variance point is a critical point of variance. It is not yet a statement about anyone's objective beyond variance, and it is not a statement about a constraint you have not written down.

The board in front of you is titled long-only is a constraint, not a theorem. The unconstrained frontier allows negative weights. A short is a position. If the mandate forbids it, say so and optimize on w in the closed unit interval. Do not call the constrained answer the minimum-variance portfolio of the textbook problem. The textbook problem, in the sense this lecture uses that phrase, is the critical point of the quadratic with the weights free except that they sum to 1. A sign constraint is an extra sentence. It changes the answer, and it must be allowed to change the name.

Space is the design variable. You say the epsilon you can tolerate on the rank, then you pick the structure. A latency histogram with fixed buckets is allowed. You call it a histogram. If the buckets are linear in the tails you care about, the tail buckets are the whole instrument. An unbounded vector of every latency is a stream you failed to sketch. The capstone reports a JavaScript timing, not a quantile sketch of nanoseconds. You do not launder a timing number through a sketch you did not build.

Start by naming the tolerance, because the structure is a consequence. Suppose you will accept a rank error of one tenth on a latency stream, and you expect about one thousand observations. One tenth of one thousand is one hundred ranks. You need a structure whose stated guarantee is on the order of one hundred ranks, or tighter if you want margin. A count-min grid does not become that structure by allocating more columns. Columns buy frequency error. Rank error is a different purchase. If you cannot name a rank epsilon, you are not ready to pick GK or t-digest or anything else, and those two names remain different compressions you have not been shown the internals of. You pick after the tolerance, not before.

A histogram is the honest cheaper object when you are willing to freeze edges. Suppose you choose edges at zero, ten, twenty, forty, eighty, and one hundred sixty, in the same time unit as the latencies you measure. That is five buckets: from zero up to ten, from ten up to twenty, from twenty up to forty, from forty up to eighty, and from eighty up to one hundred sixty. The widths are ten, ten, twenty, forty, and eighty. They widen. You store five counters plus the definition of the edges. Space is five numbers and a short list, independent of how many latencies arrive. A latency of fifteen increments the second bucket. A latency of one hundred increments the last bucket. A latency of two hundred falls outside every edge you chose, and you need a policy for that, stated in advance, because an unnamed overflow is a missing bucket. Fixed bins are honest if you show the bin edges. These are the edges. Anyone can see that the last bucket is eighty units wide and the first is ten.

Now make the buckets linear in the region you claimed to care about, and watch the instrument collapse. Edges every ten units from zero to one hundred: zero, ten, twenty, and so on up to one hundred. That is ten buckets, each of width ten. Space is still small, ten counters. Suppose the tail you care about is everything at or above ninety. That entire tail is one bucket, from ninety to one hundred, and anything beyond one hundred is outside the edges. One counter is then “the whole instrument” for the region you said mattered. You cannot tell ninety-one from ninety-nine. You cannot tell a pile at the edge from a pile at the top. The lecture’s sentence is literal here. Linear buckets in the tail you care about make the tail bucket the instrument. Widening was the earlier design, not a decoration. Equal widths are allowed. They are a choice to spend your resolution uniformly, including in the middle, and to be nearly blind inside the last bin.

Change one input. Keep the equal width of ten, and extend the last edge from one hundred to two hundred, adding ten more buckets. You now have twenty counters covering zero to two hundred. The region at or above ninety is no longer a single bucket. It is eleven buckets if you count from ninety to two hundred in steps of ten. You spent space, ten extra counters, to stop the tail from being one cell. That spend is the design variable moving. If instead you keep ten counters and change the edges to the widening set, you spend the same ten counters differently: coarse in the tail, fine at the left, or whatever your edges actually do. Five widening buckets and twenty equal buckets are different instruments. Neither is a count-min sketch. Both are histograms if you show the edges and call them histograms. The unbounded alternative, a vector that grows with every latency, has space equal to the stream. That is the failure mode the contract already forbids. You chose edges so you would not keep the vector.

You might ask whether the capstone’s timing output is already a quantile sketch, because a timing is a latency. It is not. A timing printed from that JavaScript harness is the run you measured, not a sketch of ranks and not a statement in nanoseconds. A single timing, or a handful of timings printed by a harness in that language, is a report of those runs. It does not compress ranks. It does not carry a rank epsilon. It does not become one by being stored in a growing list. If you keep every timing in a vector, you have the unbounded sample the notes warn you about. If you keep one number, you have one number. Either way, say what you stored. The frequency lab does not fill the gap. Streaming symbols through count-min estimates how often a symbol appeared. A timing is not a symbol in that lab unless you deliberately bin it, and once you bin it you have built the histogram, and you should say so.

What a histogram does not mean is that you have approximated a quantile to a stated rank epsilon. You have counted fixed bins. The bin counts are exact for those edges, assuming you increment the right bin, and they are silent inside a bin. Exact bin counts are not an approximate median. You can define a bin-based summary, for example the smallest edge whose cumulative count reaches half the observations, and that summary’s error is bounded by the bin width, which is a statement about your edges, not about GK. Call it a histogram summary. Do not call it a t-digest. What the widening edges also do not mean is that the tail is solved. An eighty-unit last bucket still conflates every latency inside it. You sized it on purpose. Purpose is not the same as fine resolution. If the tail is the instrument, you may need still more edges out there, and the space cost is the extra counters. You pay it or you admit the bin is coarse.

Take one thousand latencies and drop them into the ten equal buckets of width ten between zero and one hundred, and suppose nine hundred land below ninety and one hundred land in the last bucket. The last bucket’s count is one hundred. Its share of the stream is one hundred divided by one thousand, which is one tenth. You know that one tenth of the mass sits somewhere from ninety to one hundred. You do not know the median of that tenth. The overall median, rank five hundred in a list of one thousand, sits somewhere in the lower buckets, and you can find which bucket by a cumulative walk over exact bin counts. Suppose the cumulative count first exceeds five hundred inside the bucket from forty to fifty. Then you know the median’s bin, and you know the value is between forty and fifty. The uncertainty is the width, ten. That is a clear, modest claim. It is not a rank-epsilon theorem, and it is not a frequency-sketch theorem. It is the histogram doing the job you built it for. Change the last bucket’s count from one hundred to four hundred, one changed input, and the lower nine hundred becomes six hundred. The median rank is still five hundred. Whether it still falls in the forty-to-fifty bucket depends on the lower bins, which you must recompute. The tail got heavier. The median may or may not move. The last bucket still does not tell you the upper quantile’s value inside its range. More mass in a coarse bin is still a coarse bin.

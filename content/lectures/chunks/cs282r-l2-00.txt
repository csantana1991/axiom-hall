Name the idea. A temporal convolution is a local, shared filter. The same weights slide across time. That is a strong assumption. The pattern you care about has a width, and it means the same thing at ten oh one as at ten oh four. The filter is cheap, and it is biased toward locality. Sometimes that bias is the regularizer you wanted. Sometimes it is a mistake you have dressed up as an architecture.

Take the assumption apart before any arithmetic. Shared weights mean the kernel does not get a new set of coefficients because the clock advanced. If the kernel is three taps long, you own three numbers, not three numbers per time. Those three numbers multiply three neighboring inputs, then you step forward one time and multiply again. Local means the output at a time is a function of a window, not of the whole day, unless you build a wider window by stacking or by dilation. The phrase "beyond what they multiply" matters. The weights do not look at the input and then rewrite themselves. Attention will do a cousin of that rewrite. A convolution does not. The input enters as the thing being multiplied. It does not enter as a second program that chooses the multiplier.

The times in the claim are there so you do not treat the assumption as poetry. Ten oh one and ten oh four are three minutes apart. A pattern that means the same thing at both instants is a pattern whose shape, not whose clock position, carries the meaning. A burst of signed imbalance that lasts a few ticks can be such a pattern. A pattern whose meaning is "this is the open" or "this is the minute before an announcement" is not such a pattern, because the same local shape at those two clocks is not the same event. The convolution will still fire. It has no other way to behave. The bias toward locality is then not a regularizer. It is a refusal to let the clock position change the weights.

Work a receptive field with a width you state as a choice for the calculation. The lab does not hand you this kernel. The slide states the geometry: stacking widens the receptive field linearly in the depth, or exponentially with dilation. Let the kernel width be three, the stride be one, and the dilation of each layer be one. One layer sees three consecutive times. Call the receptive field three. A second layer of the same kind sits on the outputs of the first. Each of those outputs already summarizes three times, and neighboring outputs overlap. The earliest time that can affect the second-layer output is two steps before the earliest time that affects the central first-layer output. Count it as one plus two times the depth. Depth one gives one plus two, which is three. Depth two gives one plus four, which is five. Depth three gives one plus six, which is seven. Depth four gives nine. Depth five gives eleven. Each added layer buys two new times, one on each side, not a new copy of the whole past. The growth is linear in the depth. If you wanted a receptive field of eleven one-second ticks, five layers of width three give it to you, and five layers of width three is not the same object as one layer of width eleven. The single wide layer owns eleven coefficients. The stack owns three coefficients per layer, fifteen coefficients if nothing is tied across layers, and the intermediate maps are forced to be local compositions. That force is the bias.

Check the linear claim against the clock in the opening sentence. From ten oh one to ten oh four is three minutes. If your ticks are one second, three minutes is one hundred eighty ticks. A width-three stack grows the receptive field by two ticks per layer. To reach one hundred eighty one, because the field has the form one plus two times depth, you would need a depth of ninety. Ninety layers to cover three minutes is a particular design, and it may be an absurd one. The arithmetic is not an order to build ninety layers. It is a way to see that "local" does not become "the whole morning" just because you stacked twice. Depth two sees five ticks. At one-second ticks, five seconds is not the distance from ten oh one to ten oh four.

Dilation is the other clause of the slide, and it changes the slope. Keep the kernel width at three, so each layer still owns three taps, but space the taps. The extra reach of a layer is two times its dilation, the two being the kernel width minus one. Start from a receptive field of one, which is a single time before any layer. A dilation of one adds two, and the field is three. A dilation of two adds four, and the field is seven. A dilation of four adds eight, and the field is fifteen. A dilation of eight adds sixteen, and the field is thirty-one. The dilations themselves were one, two, four, and eight, a doubling schedule. The receptive field went three, seven, fifteen, thirty-one, which is one less than two, eight, sixteen, and thirty-two. Each doubling of the dilation roughly doubles the field. That is the exponential clause. Four layers did what a plain stack would need a depth of fifteen to do, because a plain field of thirty-one is one plus two times fifteen. You spent four layers of three taps, twelve coefficients if each layer has its own kernel, and you can see thirty-one ticks. You have not cancelled the shared-weight assumption. You have only changed how fast locality expands. The taps are still the same weights at ten oh one and at ten oh four, only farther apart.

Do one more width so the number three does not look magical. Suppose the kernel width is five, stride one, dilation one. Each layer adds four times of context, two on each side. The receptive field is one plus four times the depth. Depth one sees five. Depth two sees nine. Depth three sees thirteen. Compare that with the width-three stack, which needed depth four to see nine and still saw only seven at depth three. A wider kernel buys field faster and spends more coefficients per layer. Five coefficients instead of three, every layer. The assumption gets stronger in a different direction: the pattern's width is five, and those five weights still mean the same thing wherever the window sits. If the true pattern is two ticks wide, three of the five taps are an invitation to fit noise. If the true pattern is fifty ticks wide and changes shape after lunch, neither the width-three stack nor the width-five stack has abandoned the shared-weight claim. They have only chosen different neighborhoods.

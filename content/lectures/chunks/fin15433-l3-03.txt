Put a number on the cosine only where the identity forces a bound, not where it would require a table the lesson does not contain. The hyperbolic cosine is at least one. It is also at least the average of an exponential and its reciprocal, which is the definition you may already know, and this lecture does not ask you to derive further inequalities from it. What you can say from the midpoint formula alone is a bound. Remaining shares at the midpoint equal X over two cosh, and that is at most X over two. Suppose you want a schedule that still holds at least three quarters of the original half, meaning midpoint inventory at least three eighths of X. Then one over two cosh is at least three eighths, so two cosh is at most eight thirds, so cosh is at most four thirds. Any kappa large enough that the hyperbolic cosine of kappa T over two exceeds four thirds has already pushed the midpoint below three eighths of X. You do not need the value of kappa to use the test. You need the cosine the slider implies, or you need to refuse the percentage if you have not evaluated the cosine. The lab moves the curve from a straight line to a front-loaded drop. Your test is whether the midpoint has fallen below the straight line's X over two, which it must, and by how much only when the cosine is known.

Apply the same identity one quarter of the way through the horizon, so the midpoint is not the only interior point you can touch. Let the time be T over four. Open time is three T over four. Remaining shares equal X times the hyperbolic sine of three kappa T over four, divided by the hyperbolic sine of kappa T. You can split kappa T into four times kappa T over four, but the triple angle is more bookkeeping than this lecture needs. Use a coarser comparison. Three T over four is later than T over two only in the sense that less time remains. Open time at the quarter mark is longer than open time at the midpoint, and the hyperbolic sine is increasing on positive numbers, so the numerator at the quarter mark is larger than the numerator at the midpoint, and the denominator is the same. Remaining shares at T over four are therefore larger than remaining shares at T over two. That is only the statement that a liquidation trajectory that falls from X to zero is still higher earlier than later. It does not, by itself, tell you whether the first quarter sold more than a quarter of the block. For that, compare to the linear mark, which at T over four still holds three quarters of X. The sinh schedule holds less than the linear mark at the midpoint. Whether it has already crossed below the linear mark by T over four depends on kappa. Small kappa stays near the line the whole way. Large kappa has sold heavily before the quarter mark. The closed form will answer a specific kappa. The lesson does not hand you that kappa, so the honest interior facts you can state for every positive kappa are the ones that do not need it: you start at X, you end at zero, and at the midpoint you are strictly below half.

The idea in this block is that three policies meet one objective. The lab scores dump-now, linear, and the sinh schedule on the toy you already wrote down. Dump-now wins the variance proxy and loses the temporary term. Linear does the opposite. The sinh curve is the compromise the parameter asked for. Which policy has the lower total flips when you move the parameter. You do not crown a winner independent of kappa.

Kappa itself is not a separate taste. In words, kappa equals the square root of lambda times sigma squared, divided by eta. Say the monotonicity in each input, one at a time. Increase lambda and the quantity inside the square root increases, so kappa increases. Increase sigma and sigma squared increases, so kappa increases. The square is easy to miss: if sigma doubles, sigma squared quadruples, and kappa doubles, because the square root of four is two. Increase eta and the denominator increases, so kappa decreases. If eta quadruples, kappa halves, because you divide by four inside the square root and the square root of one fourth is one half. Those are the slide's three arrows: kappa increases with lambda and with sigma, and it decreases with eta. Permanent impact still does not enter this square root. The lab's score remains the temporary-plus-variance toy even while the trajectory you plot is the sinh trajectory from the linear-impact problem.

Tie the square root back to the two-step comparison, without pretending the threshold you computed is the definition of kappa. On that grid, dump became more attractive when lambda grew, and less attractive when eta grew. Those are the same two directions kappa has. Larger lambda, larger kappa, more front-loaded path, closer in spirit to dump, because you are paying temporary cost to cut inventory sooner. Larger eta, smaller kappa, path closer to the straight line, because child-order squares got expensive and you would rather spread them out. Volatility points the same way as lambda. A larger sigma inflates the variance score, which is sigma squared in J, and it inflates kappa through that same sigma squared. The schedule responds by selling earlier, so the inventory that sigma is punishing does not sit as long. You can say all of that without a single fitted number.

The sinh path's children sit between the two extremes in a sense you can say with the midpoint, and then with the squares. At a small kappa the remaining-inventory curve is almost the line, so the child orders are almost equal, and the sum of squared children is almost the linear policy's sum. At a large kappa the curve drops early, so the early children are large and the later children are small. Squares punish those early children, and the temporary score rises toward the dump's temporary score. The variance score moves the other way: early selling cuts the inventory squares. J adds those two movements with the weight lambda already baked into kappa. The curve is not a third aesthetic. It is the trajectory that the tradeoff selects when impact is linear. The lab then scores that trajectory on the toy, next to dump and next to linear, so you can see the compromise instead of only trusting the derivation.

Change one input inside kappa and watch both the shape and the toy. Take the two-step totals you already have, and multiply lambda by four. Kappa doubles, from the square root. On the grid, the variance piece of each total scales by four and the temporary piece does not. Dump's variance advantage over linear, which was one fourth sigma squared delta t X squared before multiplying by lambda, becomes four times as important in J. The temporary gap does not change. If you were previously on the side of the threshold where linear had the lower total, multiplying lambda by four can push you over the threshold, and dump's total becomes the lower one. The sinh schedule, facing the same multiplied lambda, doubles kappa and front-loads. It does not jump to the dump unless kappa is taken to the extreme. The policy that wins the toy can flip while the sinh curve only bends. That is what the lab brief means when it says to read which policy wins, and why that flips. The flip is a property of J at the parameter you set. It is not a property of the hyperbolic sine alone.

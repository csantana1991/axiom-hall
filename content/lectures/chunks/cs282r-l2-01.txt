A numerical slide of one shared kernel will keep the geometry from floating above the arithmetic. Again the sequence is a paper example, not the six lab numbers. Let the inputs be one, three, two, three, one, and four. Let the kernel be one, zero, and negative one, applied causally to the window that ends at the current time: the output is the value two steps back, minus the current value. The zero in the middle means the middle tap never contributes, which is a legal kernel, not a hole in the method. At the third position, counting from one, the window is one, three, and two, and the output is one minus two, which is negative one. At the fourth position the window is three, two, and three, and the output is three minus three, which is zero. At the fifth the window is two, three, and one, and the output is two minus one, which is one. At the sixth the window is three, one, and four, and the output is three minus four, which is negative one. The four outputs are negative one, zero, one, and negative one. The weights never changed. Only the window did. That is the shared filter, written as four subtractions.

Replace the kernel with a local average, one third, one third, and one third, on the same causal windows. The third position averages one, three, and two, which is two. The fourth averages three, two, and three, which is eight thirds. The fifth averages two, three, and one, which is two. The sixth averages three, one, and four, which is eight thirds. Same windows as the difference kernel, different weights, different outputs. The average did not become a difference by sliding. Sliding reused the average. If you had allowed a new kernel at the sixth position, say one, zero, and zero, the output there would have been three, the first element of that window, and you would no longer be describing a temporal convolution. You would be describing a list of unrelated filters that happen to be short. The cheapness came from refusing that list.

Put the two clocks on these outputs without pretending the toy tape is three minutes long. The claim is that the same weights mean the same thing at ten oh one and at ten oh four. In the arithmetic you just did, the same weights meant the same linear function at the third position and at the sixth. If those positions were ten oh one and ten oh four, the convolution's promise is that "two steps back, minus now" is still the feature you want at both clocks. If at ten oh four the economically meaningful comparison is against the open rather than against two steps back, the shared kernel will not discover that. It will subtract the current value from three steps earlier in the window and hand you negative one, faithfully, under the wrong assumption.

The question to answer is why you would stack at all, instead of writing one wide kernel and being done. You stack when you want the intermediate computation to be local and shared, and when you want depth, rather than a single inner product, to build the field. You write one wide kernel when you really believe the pattern is a single dot product against a long window and you are willing to estimate every tap separately. Stacking is not free accuracy. A stack of width three, depth five, sees eleven ticks and never represents an arbitrary pattern on those eleven ticks. It represents the patterns that can be built by composing local shared filters. That restriction is sometimes the regularizer you wanted, because an arbitrary eleven-tap kernel has eleven free numbers and will use them. The restriction is a mistake when the event is not local, or when ten oh one and ten oh four do not share a meaning. Cheap is a statement about reused weights. It is not a statement about being right.

What the claim does not mean is that a convolution is a small attention mechanism, or that a transformer is a convolution that grew up. The weights of the convolution do not depend on the input except as the values being multiplied. There is no score, no softmax, and no data-dependent average inside the filter itself. The claim also does not mean that locality is a moral good. A local filter can miss a dependence that runs from the open to the afternoon. Dilation can reach farther, and the exponential growth is real, as fifteen and then thirty-one showed, but the weights remain shared. If that sharing is false at the two clocks named in the idea, a larger field repeats the falsehood over more taps. It does not repair it.

Hold the lab still while you keep this distinction. The six signed imbalance numbers are a sequence in time, and the tool you are about to use on them is not a convolution. Do not describe a sliding kernel you have not fit and call it the lab. The convolution is the assumption you are setting beside attention so you can say, later, what the weighted average refuses to assume.

The idea now is that attention is a data-dependent weighted average. For a query q and keys k sub i, the scores are the product q times k sub i, divided by the square root of d, and then you take a softmax. The output is a sum of values weighted by those probabilities. In the lab, d equals one, the value is the number itself, and temperature plays the role of the scale. You can do one row on paper. That is the whole ambition of the calculation. It is not a trained transformer, and you should not talk as if a paper row were a forecast. The forecast question comes after the mask. Here you learn to produce the weights.

Say the three moves in order, because the order is the method. First you score. Second you turn scores into non-negative weights that sum to one, by a softmax. Third you form the weighted sum of the values. If you skip to the weighted sum without the scores, you have a smoother you chose by hand. If you stop at the scores, you have not yet averaged anything. The dependence on the data sits in the score. Change the query, or change a key, and the weights move. A convolution would have kept its weights and only changed the inputs being multiplied. This is the contrast you came in with.

The scale has a job when d is large. A dot product of d independent-ish coordinates grows like the square root of d, and a softmax of large scores saturates. One coordinate wins, the others go to zero, and you stop learning anything useful from the gradient of a nearly hard maximum. Dividing by the square root of d pulls the scores back toward a range where the softmax still has slope. In the lab, d equals one. The square root of one is one. Dividing by one does nothing, which is why the lecture calls it a yawn. Temperature replaces that yawn with a knob you can turn. The weight on index i is proportional to e to the power of q times k sub i, divided by tau, where tau is the temperature. A small tau sharpens. A large tau flattens. The proportionality becomes an equality when you divide by the sum of those exponentials, which is the softmax.

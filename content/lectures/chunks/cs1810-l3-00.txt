The idea in this block is that a penalty is part of the objective. Ridge adds lambda over 2 times the squared length of the parameter. You are no longer minimizing the original loss. Saying the penalty only stabilizes the numerics is sometimes true and often a way to avoid writing the new minimizer. This block writes the new objective. The next block solves it. The distinction has to be in place before the algebra, or the algebra looks like a printing trick.

The original lab function, with the penalty left at zero, is the square of x minus 1, plus 3 times the square of y plus 0.4. Its minimizer is x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.4, and the value there is 0. The gradient is 2 times x minus 1, and 6 times y plus 0.4. Every path you trusted in the step-size discussion was a path on that function. Turning on a penalty does not tweak those paths. It replaces the function.

The lab adds lambda over 2 times the sum of the squares of x and of y. In words, the penalized function is the old bowl plus lambda over 2 times x squared plus y squared. Lambda equals 0 recovers the unpenalized minimizer, because the added term is zero everywhere and the gradient of a zero term is zero. A large lambda makes the origin win, because the added term charges every point in proportion to its squared distance from the origin, and the charge grows without bound as lambda grows, except at the origin where the charge stays zero. Between those two extremes the minimizer is a compromise. You do not get to describe the compromise as the old answer with a smaller font.

Look at the origin and at the old minimizer as two candidates, before you solve anything, so the penalty is visible as a number. At the origin the old bowl equals 1.48, and the penalty term is zero, so the penalized value is 1.48 for every lambda. At x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.4 the old bowl equals 0, and x squared plus y squared equals 1 plus 0.16, which is 1.16. The penalty term is lambda over 2 times 1.16. For lambda equals 2 that term is 1.16. The penalized value of the old minimizer is 1.16, which is already better than the origin's 1.48, so lambda equals 2 has not crowned the origin. For lambda equals 4 the penalty term is 2 times 1.16, which is 2.32, and 2.32 is worse than 1.48. The old minimizer has become a worse candidate than the origin on the penalized objective, even though it is still the best point on the original bowl. That reversal is the penalty doing its job. You are ranking points by a different number.

The gradient changes whether or not you have located the minimum. Differentiating lambda over 2 times x squared adds lambda times x to the x partial. Differentiating lambda over 2 times y squared adds lambda times y to the y partial. The new gradient is 2 times x minus 1, plus lambda x, and 6 times y plus 0.4, plus lambda y. At the origin those extra terms vanish, so the first step away from the origin matches the unpenalized first step. That coincidence is a frequent source of false comfort. The first step looks familiar, and the destination is not. At the old minimizer, x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.4, the old partials vanish and the new gradient is lambda times 1 and lambda times negative 0.4. For lambda equals 2 the new gradient is 2 and negative 0.8. It is not the zero vector. The old minimizer is not stationary for the new objective. If your iteration sits there after you raised lambda, the iteration is not minimizing the function you claim to be minimizing.

You might ask why anyone adds a term that makes the old best point illegal as a stationary point. The honest answer in this lesson is small. The estimate you get is a different estimate. It is biased for the unpenalized minimizer, which means it does not target that point. The lesson also says that bias can lower risk, and that it does not lower training loss. Training loss, here, means the original bowl, the function you were minimizing before the penalty. Risk means the expected loss under the distribution you will be scored on, from the first lecture of this sequence. Those are not the same number, and the penalty does not automatically improve either of them by existing. What it automatically does is change the objective. Any claim that risk fell is a separate computation you have not done by writing lambda. What you can already see is that the original bowl, which was 0 at its own minimizer, will not be 0 at a different point. The penalty does not lower that training loss. If you report only the penalized value, you are hiding the purchase.

What the claim does not mean is worth pinning down before the closed form makes it look obvious. It does not mean the penalty is a debugger flag with no effect on the answer. You just computed a gradient of 2 and negative 0.8 at the old minimizer when lambda is 2. A flag that changes the gradient changes the problem. It does not mean every positive lambda makes the origin the minimizer. At lambda equals 2 the old minimizer still beats the origin on the penalized scale, 1.16 against 1.48. It does not mean the original bowl's step-size ceiling survives unchanged. The new second derivatives are 2 plus lambda in x and 6 plus lambda in y, because each penalty term contributes lambda to the curvature. The stiff curvature is now 6 plus lambda, so a safe step lies below 2 over the quantity 6 plus lambda. At lambda equals 0 that ceiling is one third, the number you already know. At lambda equals 2 the ceiling is 2 over 8, which is 0.25. A step of 0.3 was legal on the unpenalized bowl and is past the new ceiling. Stabilizing the numerics, if that is what you meant, can require a shorter step, not a longer one, because you made the bowl steeper. It does not mean an L1 penalty is the same object with a different letter. An L1 penalty has a kink at zero and a subgradient, and this lab does not show it. Do not import that geometry into a quadratic penalty. The quadratic penalty is differentiable, and its stationarity condition is an ordinary equation, which is what we solve next.

Change one input, the weight inside the penalty, and recompute the value at the old minimizer so the charge is not tied to the single factor one half that the formula already chose. The lesson's penalty is specifically lambda over 2 times the squared length, and the one half is the same cosmetic choice you have seen on a quadratic, arranged so the derivative carries lambda rather than lambda over 2 or 2 lambda. If you forgot the one half and charged lambda times 1.16 at lambda equals 2, you would report 2.32 instead of 1.16, and you would wrongly say the old minimizer already loses to the origin. The derivative would also be wrong: you would add 2 lambda x instead of lambda x. At lambda equals 2 and x equals 1 that mistaken partial is 4, not 2. The one half is not decoration. It fixes the scale of both the value and the gradient. Use the formula as written. Lambda over 2 times 1.16 at lambda equals 2 is 1.16. The gradient contribution is lambda x, which is 2. The old point is not stationary, and it is not yet beaten by the origin. Both facts come from the same term, read carefully.

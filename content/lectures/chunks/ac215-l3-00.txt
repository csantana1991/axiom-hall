The idea in this opening block is that a log is the source of truth or it is not. Kafka-style logs give you a replayable sequence. They do not give you a nanosecond. Use a log for recovery and audit. Use shared memory or a ring for the hot handoff. Pretending one mechanism does both jobs is how budgets die.

Two transports, and only two, are on the slide. A durable log replays and audits, and it is slower. An in-process ring is the hot path, and it is not a system of record. Write the handoff down. Memory is not an audit. The ring can move a market event from a producer to a consumer without becoming the place you go, next week, to prove what was sent. The log can prove what was sent, in the order the log recorded, without being the place you go for the next event on a nanosecond path. Each mechanism has the job the lecture gave it. The failure mode is the sentence that says one of them does both.

A replayable sequence means you can read the events again, in order, and rebuild a state the log is willing to stand behind. That is recovery and audit. It is not a latency. The lecture's limit is blunt: the log does not give you a nanosecond. If your budget for the hot handoff assumes the durable log delivers the next event on that clock, you have assigned the log a job it was not given, and the budget dies in the way the opening sentence describes. Shared memory or a ring is the hot handoff. The capstone ring moves a market event. It is in-process. It is not, by that fact, an audit trail. If the process restarts and the memory is gone, the ring is gone. The log, if you kept one, is what remains. If you kept neither, you do not have a source of truth. You have a machine that traded and then forgot.

The budget arithmetic from the pipeline still applies, and it should be attached to the transport you actually wait on. Suppose the hot handoff is the ring, and the log write is cold, off the path. The hot sum does not include the log. The mean of the hot sum is the sum of the hot stages only. Suppose instead you wait for the log append before you send. Then the log is a stage that blocks, and its mean and its spread enter the sum. You do not get the replay property for free inside the nanosecond claim. You paid a stage for it. The lecture's recommendation is to pay that stage off the hot path: log for recovery and audit, ring for the handoff. The recommendation is not a claim that the log's latency is zero. It is a claim that the log's latency should not be inside the handoff budget, because the log is not the handoff.

Work the five stage names the lab brief asks you to write, the ones the capstone note already listed, and mark which transport each one uses. Gateway read. Ring push. Strategy decision. Ring pop. Gateway write. The ring push and the ring pop are the in-process ring. They are the hot handoff between the read and the decision, and between the decision and the write. They are not a durable log. If you replace the ring push with a Kafka-style append and you wait for it, you have changed the transport and you have changed the budget. The name "push" surviving in a diagram does not keep the latency. The mechanism under the name is the mechanism you have to budget. Write the five names you would actually defend, even if you leave the lab's defaults in the model. The defaults are invented teaching values. The names are the contract. A slider is not a slogan. It is a stage with a spread, or it is a teaching number you must replace before you defend it.

Change one input. Keep the five names and change only the push, from the ring to a durable append you wait on. The sum of the means gains the difference between the append's mean and the ring push's mean. This lecture does not give you those two means, so you do not invent milliseconds and subtract them. You write the sign. Waiting on a durable append, where you previously waited on an in-process ring, adds a stage the lecture classified as slower. The hot budget gets worse by that difference, or you stop waiting and the append is no longer on the hot path. There is no third option in which the append stays on the path and costs the ring's time. That third option is pretending one mechanism does both jobs. The budget dies there. You either pay the slower mean or you take the log off the handoff.

You might ask whether a log can be fast enough, on some hardware, that the distinction collapses. The lecture does not give you a measurement that collapses it, and it states the jobs as different. A fast log is still the recovery and audit mechanism, and the ring is still the hot handoff, unless you have consciously chosen to wait on the log and put that wait in the budget. Speed does not rewrite the source of truth. If the log is fast and you still do not treat it as the record, you have a fast unused record. If you treat it as the record and you do not wait for it, you have a record that can lag the handoff, and the lag is a fact about recovery, not a nanosecond. Say which clock you are on. The log's clock is replay order. The ring's clock is the handoff. They can both be present. They are not the same term in the sum.

What the claim does not mean is that Kafka-style logs are a mistake, or that rings are a system of record if you want them hard enough. The log is the right tool for replay and audit. The ring is the right tool for the hot handoff. It does not mean memory becomes an audit if you pin a thread. Memory is not an audit. It does not mean the five names are a latency measurement. They are the list you must be willing to defend, with spreads you replace over the teaching defaults. It does not mean a gateway write is a log. The gateway write is a stage in the hot list. A durable copy of what you wrote is a different stage, cold unless you waited. It does not mean the nanosecond the log fails to give you is a number you may attach to the ring without saying so. If you claim a nanosecond, the earlier note told you to say it is about the ring alone whenever a heavy stage shares the path. A log you wait on is a heavy stage in exactly that sense. Leave it off the handoff or put it in the sum. Do not leave it off the page and on the path.

The idea in this block is that schemas are risk controls. A market event with a double price and an implicit tick size will eventually compare unequal to itself. The capstone struct is explicit. The teaching file still has a double in one field, and the note tells you what to change before production. Add a schema version when the struct changes, or replays will lie.

Read the fields the note names. Timestamp, in nanoseconds. Order id. Price. Quantity. Side. Five fields. That list is the contract the teaching file is willing to write down. Side is a char, in the lecture's description of the explicit struct. Sizes should fit. The layout is fixed, and the padding is written down. The struct is trivially copyable, or the ring's store is a data race. The trivial-copy assertion is the schema check the compiler can do. A compiler check is not a prose comment. It fails the build when the type stops being a plain record you can copy as bytes. That is the risk control you get without a review meeting. You still version the layout when you change it, because a compiler on the new code does not police an old replay.

Name the idea before you name a speed. This is a handoff, not a marketplace. The capstone moves a market event from a producer to a consumer without a lock and without allocating. It is not an exchange, a matching engine, or a broker. Scope is how this stays finishable. If you add a venue, you have left the practicum and started a different project, and you will not finish either one by describing them as the same object.

Say what crosses the handoff. One producer thread produces events. One consumer thread takes them. The capacity is fixed before the first event, and it stays fixed. The event is trivially copyable, which means the language can copy the bytes of the value without running a custom constructor in the middle of the transfer. You do not allocate after startup. The slots exist. The producer writes into a slot it is allowed to write. The consumer reads a slot it is allowed to read. Nothing in that sentence discovers a price, matches two orders, or routes anything to a venue. The event may describe a market. The ring does not become the market.

The finish line follows from that narrowness. You can draw one cell, one ticket, and one mask, and a classmate can push back on the drawing. You can run a checksum on a known sequence of order identifiers and get an equality you can recompute by hand. You can say, without a new subsystem, whether the producer was permitted to write. A matching engine has none of those properties at the scale of a semester. It has order types, priority, partial fills, and a notion of a trade. None of those are in the handoff. Leaving them out is not a missing feature you apologize for. It is the decision that makes the defense a defense of an engine rather than a tour of a venue you did not build.

Work the checksum identity on a sequence short enough to add in your head, so the later hundred-thousand case is not a magic formula. The default build pushes order identifiers zero through N minus one, and the consumer sums them. The sum of the integers from zero through N minus one is N times the quantity N minus one, divided by two. For N equal to four, the identifiers are zero, one, two, and three. The sum is six. The formula gives four times three, divided by two, which is twelve divided by two, which is six. For N equal to five, the identifiers add to ten, and five times four divided by two is ten. The equality is the test. If the consumer's sum is six when N is four, every identifier arrived once. If the sum is five, something was dropped or torn or double-counted, and you stop. You do not time a ring that has already failed this arithmetic.

Scale the same identity to the default run, which is a threaded checksum of one hundred thousand events. N is one hundred thousand. The sum is one hundred thousand times ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine, divided by two. Divide the hundred thousand by two first, which gives fifty thousand, then multiply by ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine. Fifty thousand times one hundred thousand is five billion. Fifty thousand times one is fifty thousand, so fifty thousand times ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine is five billion minus fifty thousand, which is four billion nine hundred ninety-nine million nine hundred fifty thousand. That is the number the consumer must produce. It is not an approximation and it is not a tolerance. A torn or dropped event fails the equality. If the checksum mismatches, you stop. You do not interpret a timing of a broken ring.

There are two builds, and they answer two different questions. The default build is that threaded checksum of one hundred thousand events. You compile with g plus plus, optimization level three, the C plus plus twenty standard, and pthread, producing an executable named spsc test from the file spsc ring buffer dot cpp, and then you run it. The command is a check of the sum, not a latency distribution. The second build adds the define AXIOM WITH BENCHMARK and links the benchmark library. It runs two threads, one pushing and one popping. A push-only loop is a bug, not a benchmark. You will spend a later hour on why that loop times a livelock. In this hour it is enough to refuse the phrase "the benchmark" for a loop that never consumes.

Compute one intermediate N so the hundred-thousand figure is not the only value your hand remembers. For N equal to one thousand, the identifiers are zero through nine hundred ninety-nine. The sum is one thousand times nine hundred ninety-nine, divided by two. One thousand divided by two is five hundred, and five hundred times nine hundred ninety-nine is five hundred times one thousand minus five hundred, which is five hundred thousand minus five hundred, which is four hundred ninety-nine thousand five hundred. You can check a short prefix of that run by hand. Identifiers zero through nine sum to forty-five, and ten times nine divided by two is forty-five. If a consumer reports forty-four on that prefix, the equality has already failed and the remaining nine hundred ninety identifiers cannot repair the story you tell about the program. You stop. The same rule scales without a change in meaning from ten events, to one thousand, to one hundred thousand. Only the integer gets longer.

The event those identifiers ride in has a shape you should be able to say, because "trivially copyable" is not a mood. A reasonable event for this handoff carries an integer order identifier, a side, a size, and a price in integer ticks. You freeze that layout before the timed run, and you do not allocate a string for the symbol on every push. The identifier is what the checksum adds. The other fields are along for the copy. If the layout is twelve bytes or sixty-four bytes or something larger, the protocol does not change, and the time, if you ever measure it, does. Event size belongs on the same line as the flags when a clock exists. Until a clock exists, the layout still belongs in the talk, because a handoff of an unspecified blob is not yet a handoff of a market event. It is a handoff of a slot. Say what the bytes mean, and say that the ring does not interpret them. Interpretation would be a strategy, or a matcher, and both of those are outside the producer-consumer contract.

The question from the room is whether a clean handoff is secretly a matching engine you can grow later by adding a book. It is not. A book remembers resting interest and decides who trades with whom. This ring forgets an event once the consumer has taken the bytes, except insofar as the consumer, which is your code on the other side, chooses to remember them. The ring's job ends at the handoff. If your consumer is a strategy, the strategy is not the ring. If your consumer is a logger, the log is not a venue. Scope is how the work stays finishable. Growing a matcher inside the slot turns the trivially copyable event into a protocol of cancels and replaces, and you will no longer be able to say, in one whiteboard drawing, what the ticket means. You can build a matcher some other time. You cannot smuggle it in and still claim you are testing this handoff.

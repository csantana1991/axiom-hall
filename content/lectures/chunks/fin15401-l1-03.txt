Use the case already on the board. Sigma 1 is 0.10, sigma 2 is 0.30, rho is 0.50, and the unconstrained weight on the first asset is 15 over 14, about 1.071, with the second weight about negative 0.071. The variance at that point is about 0.00964, so the standard deviation is about 0.098. That point is the global minimum-variance mix of the unrestricted problem. It shorts asset 2. The lab is entitled to mark it. A mandate that forbids shorts is a different problem, and you can solve the different problem by looking at the two endpoints and the interior of the unit interval, because a smooth curve on a closed interval either bottoms at the critical point or bottoms at a boundary. The critical point is outside the interval, to the right of 1. Inside the interval the variance is still decreasing as w increases, which you can see from the derivative, or from two comparisons. At w equal to 0 the variance is 0.09. At w equal to 1 the variance is 0.01. At w equal to 0.90, which was the zero-correlation hero and is still inside the interval, the variance at this new correlation is 0.90 squared times 0.01 plus 0.10 squared times 0.09 plus 2 times 0.90 times 0.10 times 0.015. That is 0.0081 plus 0.0009 plus 0.0027, which is 0.0117. The standard deviation is about 0.108. Compare 0.0117 with the endpoint variance 0.01. The endpoint is better. The long-only minimum sits at w equal to 1, fully in the quieter asset, variance 0.01, standard deviation 0.10. You will mislead a listener if you call that endpoint the global minimum-variance portfolio. It is the minimum-variance portfolio among long-only weights. The global critical point is the short, and its variance, about 0.00964, is lower than 0.01 by a small amount. The short is doing real work in the quadratic, and the work is small. Both facts belong in the answer.

Change one input, the correlation, and watch the short appear and disappear. At rho equal to 0 the critical weight was 0.90, inside the interval, so the long-only minimum and the global minimum were the same portfolio. At rho equal to 0.25, c equals 0.0075. The numerator is 0.09 minus 0.0075, which is 0.0825. The denominator is 0.10 minus 0.015, which is 0.085. The weight is 0.0825 divided by 0.085, which is about 0.971, still inside the interval. Variance is about 0.00993, standard deviation about 0.0996, and the second weight is still positive, about 0.029. At rho equal to 0.50 the weight has crossed 1. The constraint binds only after the correlation is high enough to push the critical point through the boundary. There is no moral line at which a short becomes thinkable. There is a formula, and then there is a mandate that either accepts the formula's sign or replaces the problem.

At rho equal to 1 the unconstrained critical point is a weight of 1.5 and a short of 0.5, with algebraic variance 0. The long-only set is the segment you already priced, whose quietest point is again w equal to 1, standard deviation 0.10, not 0. If a slide marks only the long-only endpoint, it is answering the mandate. If the lab marks the short, it is answering the textbook problem. The notes say the lab marks the critical point even when it is a short. Believe the lab's mark about the algebra, and do not relabel it as the book you are allowed to hold.

A negative weight is a position with a financing story this board has not written. In the model it means you are short the asset in the amount the weight says. Weight negative 0.071 on a unit of capital is a short notionally equal to about seven cents per dollar of the portfolio, against a long of about 1.071 dollars in the other asset. The weights still sum to 1. You financed the extra long with the short. Whether you can borrow the asset, what it costs, and what happens when the hedge gapes, are not entries in the quadratic. The notes already refused to call a zero-variance cancellation a tradable hedge until the borrow, the basis, and the gap risk are named. A small short at rho equal to 0.50 deserves the same discipline even though its variance is not zero. The algebra has produced a position. It has not produced a prime broker.

The short also moves the mean, and the move is the detail a variance-only glance will miss. At weight 15 over 14 on the first asset and negative 1 over 14 on the second, with means 0.06 and 0.12, the portfolio mean is 15 over 14 times 0.06 plus negative 1 over 14 times 0.12. That is 0.90 over 14 minus 0.12 over 14, which is 0.78 over 14, about 0.0557. The quiet asset alone has mean 0.06. The global minimum-variance mix has a lower mean than the quiet asset, and a lower variance, 0.00964 against 0.01. You gave up expected return to shave a little variance. That trade is what minimum variance means. It is not a defect in the arithmetic, and it is not a bonus. If your listener wanted the mean kept near 0.06, this critical point is the wrong object, short or not. Global minimum variance does not have a return constraint. The return is whatever falls out. Here what fell out is about 0.0557, together with a short.

Put the long-only endpoint beside it in both coordinates so the renaming is visible. Weight 1, mean 0.06, variance 0.01, standard deviation 0.10. Unconstrained critical point, mean about 0.0557, variance about 0.00964, standard deviation about 0.098, and a short. These are different portfolios. They answer different problems. The distance between them is small on this particular rho, and the names are still not interchangeable. If you report 0.10 as the minimum standard deviation of the textbook problem, you have rounded the short away and you have also replaced 0.0557 with 0.06 without saying that you changed the objective's answer.

You might ask whether a tiny short can be rounded away, since 15 over 14 is only a little above 1 and the variance gain over the endpoint is only about 0.00036. Rounding is a mandate. If you set the weight to 1, you are choosing the boundary solution and you should say that you imposed nonnegativity. The variance you then report is 0.01, not 0.00964. The difference is small on these inputs and enormous as a matter of labeling. A later input, a higher correlation or a different pair of volatilities, can push the short far from the boundary. At rho equal to 1 you already saw a short of one half. Rounding that one to a long-only endpoint throws away a zero of the quadratic and pretends the segment was the whole story. Do the rounding, if the mandate demands it, in the open.

Here is what the constraint does not mean. It does not mean shorting is a theorem, and it does not mean long-only is a theorem. Each is a constraint you add or refuse to add. It does not mean the endpoint variance is the minimum the derivative found. The derivative found 15 over 14. It does not mean a printed negative weight is advice to short. It is the critical point of variance for the inputs you used. Change the correlation back to 0 and the advice, if you were careless enough to treat it as advice, would reverse sign on the second asset for no reason a client can see except the correlation you typed. It does not mean the lab is wrong to mark a short. The lab's marked point is the global minimum-variance mix with shorts allowed. Your mandate, if you have one, is a sentence you still have to say out loud.

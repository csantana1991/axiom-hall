Here is what the two-moment claim does not mean. It does not mean you believe returns are normal. Elliptical is already a wider phrase than normal, and the notes allow a rough map even when you are outside that family. It does not mean a positive semidefinite matrix is evidence that the world is benign. It is a consistency condition on the quadratic form. The illegal matrix with determinant negative 0.000396 is rejected because it cannot be a covariance, not because the assets were dangerous. It does not mean variance is the only risk you will ever manage. It means this board will not carry any other risk. If you need a third moment, you have left the model, and you should say so rather than secretly reading a crash into a standard deviation. The rest of the hour is the algebra inside the choice, not a defense of the choice against every objection a trading desk can raise.

The board in front of you is titled the feasible set is a hyperbola. For two assets, the weight w on the first traces a curve in standard-deviation, mean space. The variance along that curve is w squared times sigma 1 squared, plus the quantity 1 minus w, squared, times sigma 2 squared, plus 2 times w times 1 minus w times rho times sigma 1 times sigma 2. Correlation opens or pinches the bullet. At rho equal to 1 there is no diversification, only a line segment. The lab scans the curve so you can see the critical point. You should be able to produce the same curve with a pencil before you trust the scan.

Expand the quadratic once, because the slide asks for that and because the minimum falls out of the expansion. Let c denote the covariance, rho times sigma 1 times sigma 2. Write the variance as w squared times sigma 1 squared, plus 1 minus 2 w plus w squared, times sigma 2 squared, plus 2 c times w minus 2 c times w squared. Collect the coefficient of w squared. It is sigma 1 squared plus sigma 2 squared minus 2 c. Collect the coefficient of w. It is negative 2 times sigma 2 squared plus 2 c. The constant term is sigma 2 squared. Differentiate with respect to w. You get 2 w times the quantity sigma 1 squared plus sigma 2 squared minus 2 c, plus the quantity negative 2 sigma 2 squared plus 2 c. Set the derivative to zero and divide by 2. Then w times the quantity sigma 1 squared plus sigma 2 squared minus 2 c equals sigma 2 squared minus c. Solve for w. The global minimum-variance weight on the first asset is sigma 2 squared minus c, divided by sigma 1 squared plus sigma 2 squared minus 2 c. The notes write the same thing with the covariance named outright. w 1 star equals sigma 2 squared minus the covariance, divided by sigma 1 squared plus sigma 2 squared minus 2 times the covariance. The second derivative is twice the denominator. The denominator is the variance of the difference of the two risky returns, up to scaling, and it is positive whenever the two assets are not perfectly locked. Then the critical point is a minimum.

Start with equal volatilities so the minimum is obvious and the correlation still has work to do. Let both standard deviations equal 0.20, so both variances equal 0.04, and let rho equal 0.50. Then c equals 0.50 times 0.20 times 0.20, which is 0.02. The numerator of the weight is 0.04 minus 0.02, which is 0.02. The denominator is 0.04 plus 0.04 minus 0.04, which is 0.04. The weight is one half. Whenever the two volatilities match and the denominator is not zero, the minimum-variance mix is an even split, for any correlation that is not exactly 1. The variance at that split, with rho equal to 0.50, is one half squared times 0.04, twice, plus 2 times one half times one half times 0.02. That is 0.01 plus 0.01 plus 0.01, which is 0.03. The standard deviation is the square root of 0.03, about 0.173. Each asset alone has standard deviation 0.20. The mix is quieter, and it is not dramatically quieter, because the correlation is still one half.

Change only the correlation, which is the input the lab wants you to drag. At rho equal to 0, with the same volatilities, c is 0 and the weight is still one half. The variance is 0.02, and the standard deviation is the square root of 0.02, about 0.141. At rho equal to negative 1, c equals negative 0.04, the weight is still one half, and the variance formula returns 0. The two assets cancel. A standard deviation of 0 is algebra. The notes are explicit about the next sentence you do not get to say. Push rho toward negative 1 and the bullet can reach a very small standard deviation. That is algebra, not a tradable hedge, until you have named the borrow, the basis, and the gap risk. At rho exactly equal to 1 with equal volatilities, the denominator sigma 1 squared plus sigma 2 squared minus 2 c is 0.04 plus 0.04 minus 0.08, which is 0. The weight formula breaks because it is dividing by zero. Every mix has variance 0.04, the same as either asset. There is no interior point that improves on them. In the plane of standard deviation and mean, if the means differ, you get a vertical segment at standard deviation 0.20, with the mean running between the two asset means. If the means agree, the segment collapses to a point. That is the line segment the board promised. No diversification.

Now use unequal volatilities, because an even split would be a waste of the formula. Let sigma 1 equal 0.10, sigma 2 equal 0.30, and give them means 0.06 and 0.12 so the curve can sit in both coordinates. At rho equal to 0, c is 0. The minimum-variance weight is 0.09 divided by 0.10, which is 0.90. Ninety percent goes to the quieter asset, ten percent to the noisier one. The variance is 0.90 squared times 0.01 plus 0.10 squared times 0.09, which is 0.0081 plus 0.0009, which is 0.009. The standard deviation is the square root of 0.009, about 0.0949, a little under the quieter asset's 0.10. The mean at that mix is 0.90 times 0.06 plus 0.10 times 0.12, which is 0.066. The quieter asset alone has mean 0.06 and standard deviation 0.10. The minimum-variance mix has a slightly higher mean and a slightly lower standard deviation than asset 1, because asset 2 was not pure noise relative to a zero correlation. Do not generalize that gift. It depended on the means I assigned and on rho equal to 0.

Plot a few other weights at this same rho so the bullet is a list and not a metaphor. At w equal to 0 you hold only asset 2, mean 0.12, standard deviation 0.30. At w equal to 0.25, the mean is 0.105, and the variance is 0.25 squared times 0.01 plus 0.75 squared times 0.09, which is 0.000625 plus 0.050625, which is 0.05125. The standard deviation is about 0.226. At w equal to one half, the mean is 0.09 and the variance is 0.025, standard deviation about 0.158. At w equal to 0.75, the mean is 0.075 and the variance is 0.01125, standard deviation about 0.106. At w equal to 0.90 you are at the minimum already computed, mean 0.066, standard deviation about 0.0949. At w equal to 1, mean 0.06, standard deviation 0.10. Read the standard deviations from the left asset across to the right. They fall from 0.30 toward about 0.0949 and then rise again to 0.10. That dip is the hyperbola's useful part, the reason a blend can be quieter than either endpoint. The means fall in a straight line the whole way, from 0.12 down to 0.06. The curve in the plane bends because only the horizontal coordinate bends.

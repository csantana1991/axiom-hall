You might ask whether a huge sample size makes the distinction dissolve. This lesson does not give you a rate, so I will not invent one. Empirical risk at a frozen parameter is a sample average of losses. A long average can sit near an expectation, and it can fail to, and neither sentence is proved on this board. Even if you grant that the average loss is close to its expectation at one fixed parameter, you did not evaluate one fixed parameter. You minimized. The parameter you return depends on the list. Closeness that holds uniformly over the parameters you might have returned is a stronger sentence, and it is not the sentence in the definition. A large n is not a license to erase the hat. What you may say is smaller, and it is exact. You computed an average. You minimized it. Risk is a different average. Its minimizer is a different object until some later argument, which you do not have yet, identifies the two.

Here is another list, so the gap is not tied to a single mean. Take the outcomes 10, negative 2, and 4. The sum is 12 and the mean is 4, the same location you got from 2, 4, and 6. The value of the empirical risk is not the same. Squared errors at 4 are 36, 36, and 0, sum 72, average 24. Earlier the average at the minimizer was 8/3. If you report only that the fitted parameter is 4, you have hidden which list you fit and what training loss you achieved. The formula in this lesson keeps the average, not only the identity of the argmin. Move one unit away, to 5. For squared error around a sample mean, a shift of size d raises the average squared error by d squared, because the residuals around the sample mean sum to zero and the cross term drops. A shift of 1 raises 24 to 25. Check it. Squared errors at 5 are 25, 49, and 1, sum 75, average 25. A shift of 3, to the parameter 7, raises the average by 9, from 24 to 33. Squared errors at 7 are 9, 81, and 9, sum 99, average 33. The algebra and the direct sum agree.

A one-point list shows the fooling in the smallest legal case of the definition. Let the only outcome be 5. Then n is 1, the average is the single squared error, and the minimizer is 5, with empirical risk 0. A training loss of 0 is not a risk of 0. Risk is still an expectation under the scoring distribution. If that distribution is not a point mass at 5, the variance term is positive at every parameter, including at 5. Memorizing the single training point drove the sample average to zero and did nothing, by itself, to that variance. This is the opportunity named at the start, with no extra machinery: the substitution of an average for an expectation can look perfect on the list and still be the wrong object.

What the claim does not mean needs a clean list of its own, said in sentences rather than slogans. It does not mean the sample average was mis-added. The averages above are the definition, carried out. It does not mean you should refuse to minimize training loss. That minimization is the method. It does not mean the training minimizer always misses the scoring minimizer by a fixed distance. You already saw a supposition where the distance was 0 and suppositions where it was 1 or 2. It does not mean squared error is the only loss the definition can host. The definition is one over n times a sum of whatever loss you wrote down. Logistic loss is the other loss this lesson names as convex in the linear predictor. The same hat would sit on an average of logistic losses. This lesson does not give a numeric logistic table, so I will not invent one. The structural warning transfers without that table: minimizing the average is still not the act of minimizing the expectation. It does not mean the surface you will walk in the lab is an empirical risk. That surface is a function of two coordinates and it contains no sample. There is no n in it. Do not narrate it later as a training average. We will use it because a failure on it cannot be blamed on a second basin.

One further worked average, because the factor one over n is where a 2 gets dropped and then repaired in the wrong place. Let the outcomes be 0, 0, and 3. The sum is 3, n is 3, and the sample mean is 1. Empirical risk at 1: squared errors 1, 1, and 4, sum 6, average 2. The residuals in the sign convention parameter minus outcome are 1, 1, and negative 2, and they sum to 0, so the gradient of the average is 0. At the parameter 0, which is a shift of 1, the average rises by 1 squared, from 2 to 3. Directly: squared errors 0, 0, and 9, average 3. At the parameter 1.5, the shift is one half, and the average rises by one quarter, from 2 to 2.25. Directly: each squared error is 2.25, the sum is 6.75, and 6.75 divided by 3 is 2.25. Look at the individual losses. At the minimizer they were 1, 1, and 4. At 1.5 they were equal, and the worst of them was 2.25, which is kinder than a worst loss of 4. Empirical risk did not choose the kinder worst loss. It chose the smaller average. If the quantity you will be scored on is a worst case rather than an expectation, you are not computing the risk written in this lesson, and you should say that before you fit.

There is a useful decomposition you should be able to say without notes. For any list and any parameter, the average squared error equals the average squared error at the sample mean, plus the square of the parameter minus the sample mean. On the list 0, 0, 3, that says the empirical risk equals 2 plus the square of the parameter minus 1. At the parameter 4, the formula gives 2 plus 9, which is 11. Directly, the squared errors are 16, 16, and 1, sum 33, average 11. The decomposition is why changing the parameter and changing an outcome are different edits. Changing the parameter moves you along a parabola whose bottom is fixed by the list. Changing an outcome moves the bottom. Empirical risk minimization, given the list, only does the first edit. It cannot reach into the scoring distribution and move m.

Carry one sentence into the next block. We are about to minimize a particular function by walking downhill one step at a time. The function is not a sample average. The reason this block came first is so you do not narrate the bottom of that surface as if it were automatically the parameter you will be scored on. The walk finds a minimizer of the function you differentiated. Whether the function you wrote down is the risk or the empirical risk is a choice that lives in the loss. If you put a hat on it, the hat stays in the claim.

The idea in this block is that convex losses behave, and that on a convex bowl the failure mode of gradient descent is the step size, not a mysterious second basin. Squared error and logistic loss are convex in the linear predictor. A convex function has no spurious local minimum. If the walk on this lab misbehaves, you cut the step. You do not go looking for another valley.

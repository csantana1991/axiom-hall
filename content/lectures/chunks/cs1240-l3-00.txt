The board in front of you is titled modulo is a division. A ring buffer maps a monotonic counter onto a slot by taking i mod capacity. The counter i only moves forward. The slot has to live in a fixed array, so something has to fold that growing counter back into the legal indexes. The fold you were taught first is the remainder after division. Integer division is the wrong instruction on a nanosecond path. It is a real division, or the library sequence that implements one, and you do not want that latency in the indexing step if a cheaper instruction computes the same remainder.

Say what the remainder is, before you replace it. i mod m is the unique integer r such that i equals q times m plus r, with r at least 0 and strictly less than m, when i is nonnegative and m is positive. The quotient q is how many whole capacities fit into i. The remainder r is the slot. For a capacity of 8, the counter 0 sits in slot 0, the counter 7 sits in slot 7, and the counter 8 sits in slot 0 again, because 8 equals 1 times 8 plus 0. The counter 19 sits in slot 3, because 19 equals 2 times 8 plus 3. You can check that by division, and you should, because the later identity is only interesting if you still know what the division would have answered.

The cheap replacement exists only for a special capacity. If the capacity m is a power of two, say m equals 2 to the k, the low bits of i are already the remainder. Dividing by 2 to the k shifts the bits of i right by k places. The bits that fall off that shift are exactly the remainder, and there are k of them. You do not have to perform the shift and then look at what fell off. A mask keeps those low bits and clears the rest. The mask is m minus 1, which is a string of ones in the low k bits and zeros above them. Bitwise and with that mask is the remainder. The formula, spoken carefully, is i mod 2 to the k equals i bitwise-and 2 to the k minus 1, for i at least 0. The lab exists so you can move the counter and watch the two sides agree while the capacity stays on a power of two.

Try the mask on the same numbers you already divided, with capacity 8. Then k is 3, because 8 is 2 to the third, and the mask is 7. In bits, 8 is 1 followed by three zeros, and 7 is three ones. The counter 0 and-ed with 7 is 0, and 0 mod 8 is 0. The counter 7 and-ed with 7 is 7, and 7 mod 8 is 7. The counter 8 and-ed with 7 is 0, and 8 mod 8 is 0. The counter 19 in bits is 10011. And with 00111 leaves 00011, which is 3. And 19 mod 8 is 3. The counter 100: 100 divided by 8 is 12 with remainder 4, because 12 times 8 is 96, and 100 minus 96 is 4. In bits, 100 ends with the low three bits 100, which is 4. The mask and the division agree.

Move the same counters to capacity 16, which is also a power of two. The mask is 15, four ones. 19 and-ed with 15 is still 3, and 19 mod 16 is 3, because 16 plus 3 is 19. 100 and-ed with 15 is 4, and 100 mod 16 is 4, because 6 times 16 is 96. Changing the power of two changed the mask from 7 to 15 and changed which bits survive, and both remainders followed. That is the whole trick. The low bits are the remainder precisely because the modulus is a pure shift.

Run the counter from 0 through 16 at capacity 8 and say the slot out loud, because a formula feels finished before your ear trusts the wrap. Mask 7. Slot of 0 is 0. Slot of 1 is 1. Slot of 2 is 2. Slot of 3 is 3. Slot of 4 is 4. Slot of 5 is 5. Slot of 6 is 6. Slot of 7 is 7. Slot of 8 is 0. Slot of 9 is 1. Slot of 10 is 2. Slot of 11 is 3. Slot of 12 is 4. Slot of 13 is 5. Slot of 14 is 6. Slot of 15 is 7. Slot of 16 is 0. Each of those matches the remainder, and the wrap happens exactly when the counter crosses a multiple of 8. The second lap is not a new array. It is the same eight addresses, visited by a counter that did not itself become small.

Change the power and do three checks at capacity 32, so the mask is 31, five ones, and not only the masks 7 and 15. The counter 40 divided by 32 leaves remainder 8, because 32 plus 8 is 40, and 40 bitwise-and 31 is 8. The counter 63 is one less than 64, so 63 mod 32 is 31, and 63 bitwise-and 31 is 31. The counter 64 is exactly two times 32, so the remainder is 0, and 64 bitwise-and 31 is 0. The identity is not getting tired as the power grows. It is the same low-bit story with a longer string of ones.

You might ask why anybody still writes the division if the mask is a single instruction. The division is the definition of the slot for an arbitrary positive capacity. The mask is an implementation of that definition under an extra hypothesis you have to earn. If you write only the mask, a reader cannot see which mathematical fold you meant, and a later edit that changes the capacity can keep the mask and lose the meaning. If you write only the division, the nanosecond path pays for a generality the array does not use. The lecture's resolution is not to pretend the operations are identical for every m. It is to restrict m until they are identical, and to make that restriction something the compiler can see.

Here is what the opening claim does not mean. It does not mean integer division is mathematically suspect. The remainder is well defined, and every example above used it as the standard. It does not mean a power-of-two capacity makes the counter itself wrap. The counter 100 is still 100. Only the slot folded. It does not mean every bitwise and is a modulus. And with an arbitrary mask is just a bit operation. It equals a remainder only for the mask that is one less than a power of two, and only for a nonnegative i, which is the hypothesis we have not yet broken on purpose. The next board breaks it.

The board in front of you is titled the identity. For m equal to 2 to the k, and for i at least 0, i mod m equals i bitwise-and m minus 1. The mask m minus 1 is a string of ones in the low bits. You already watched that string work for m equal to 8 and m equal to 16. The identity is false as soon as m is not a power of two. The lab has a switch that breaks it on purpose. This board is that switch, done on paper, so the lab does not surprise you.

The counterexample on the slide is small and final. 19 mod 12 equals 7. 19 bitwise-and 11 equals 3. Same style of instruction, wrong slot. Twelve is not a power of two. Eleven is not a string of ones. In bits, 12 is 1100, so it has more than one bit set. The mask 11 is 1011. The counter 19 is 10011. And with 1011 keeps 00011, which is 3. Division says otherwise. Two twelves are 24, which is already past 19, so the quotient is 1 and the remainder is 19 minus 12, which is 7. Three and 7 are different slots. If a ring of capacity 12 used the mask, the counter 19 would address slot 3 while the mathematical ring addresses slot 7. The notes call that a torn book. The word is not decoration. Two readers of the same counter would disagree about which cell holds the item.

Change the counter and keep the bad capacity, so the failure is not a quirk of the number 19. Take i equal to 30 and m equal to 12. The mask is still 11. 30 in bits is 11110. And with 01011 leaves 01010, which is 10. Now divide. Two times 12 is 24, and 30 minus 24 is 6. So 30 mod 12 equals 6, while 30 bitwise-and 11 equals 10. Again they diverge, and they diverge to a different pair than 7 and 3. The bug is not "always off by four." There is no stable offset you could subtract to repair the mask. The low bits are simply not the remainder when the modulus has more than one bit set.

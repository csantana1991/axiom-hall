The idea in this block is the partial differential equation itself, read as a financing identity and not as a forecast. The equation is the time partial of f, plus r S times the first S partial, plus one half sigma squared S squared times the second S partial, minus r times f, equals 0. The terminal condition for a call is f at T and S equals the maximum of S minus K and 0. Mu does not appear. A forecast of the drift does not enter this equation, and moving a forecast slider must not move the price that solves it.

Read each term as a job. The time partial is the explicit clock inside the option price, the part that would change even if S were held fixed. The term r S times the first S partial is the financing adjustment on the share hedge, the piece that replaced mu S times the first S partial. The term one half sigma squared S squared times the second S partial is the Itô correction, the same curvature term that produced minus one half sigma squared in the log. The term minus r f is the financing of the option premium. Set their sum to zero and you have said that the hedged book earns r and nothing more. If your written equation is missing the minus r f, you have priced an object that is not charged for the premium. If it still contains mu, you have undone the cancellation from the previous block.

The terminal condition is not a detail you add after the price looks reasonable. It is the other half of the problem. At expiry the call pays the maximum of S minus K and 0, and it pays it once, with no remaining optionality. Any solution of the differential equation that ends somewhere else is a solution for a different contract. A contract that pays S at expiry, rather than the maximum of S minus K and 0, solves the same differential equation with a different terminal condition, and its value is not the call value. On this model that pure claim is worth S, if there is no dividend, because holding the share itself replicates a payoff of S and the share price is S. The call pays less than S whenever S is below K, and pays S minus K when S is above K. Its value lies below the share price. The equation does not by itself know this. The terminal condition tells it.

Mu’s absence is worth a second pass, because the slider in the lab is built to test it. The physical skeleton is still dS equals mu S dt plus sigma S dW. The paths fan up when mu rises and fan down when mu falls. The mean of S at a horizon t is still S at 0 times e to the mu t, from the previous lecture. None of that was revoked. What was revoked is the right of mu to appear in the hedged price. The derivation canceled mu between df and the short shares before r was imposed. There is nowhere left for a forecast to sit. If you move mu and the call price moves, the lab is wrong. That is the exercise. The bug, if it appears, is a wiring bug, not a subtlety of the Wiener process. A correct implementation can show a violent change in the fan and a call price that does not change in the fourth digit.

Dividends are the nearby modification the lecture bothers to name, so you do not learn the equation as a law of nature. The lab pays none. If the underlying paid a continuous dividend, the cash thrown off by the short shares would alter the dt coefficient of the hedge, and r in front of S f sub S would not be the whole story. This lecture does not write the dividend equation, and you should not pretend a verbal “plus a yield” fixes the formula without re-deriving the financing. The honest statement in the lab is the equation as written, with no dividend term. When you leave the lab, the absence of a dividend is a hypothesis you have to restate.

The same narrowness applies to the terminal contract. The maximum of S minus K and 0 is a call. A put would be the maximum of K minus S and 0. The lab only prices the call. Put-call parity, with no dividend, says the call minus the put equals S minus K times e to the minus r T. That identity is the test you will use if you add a put later, not another copy of the same bug. If both your call and your put are wrong by the same additive amount, their difference can still match the parity value, so parity is not a complete test. If their difference fails to match S minus K e to the minus r T, at least one of them is wrong. The lecture gives you the identity so the test is available. It does not ask the lab to display the put.

You might ask whether the equation determines a unique price, or whether many functions satisfy it. With the terminal condition, and with the usual growth restrictions this lecture does not spell out as a separate theorem, the closed form in the next block is the solution the course wants you to use. Two different functions cannot both be the call unless they agree. If a numerical scheme and the closed form disagree, one of them is not solving this problem. The usual failures are a terminal condition applied at the wrong time, a discount rate that is not r, a volatility that entered as variance in one place and as standard deviation in another, and a mu that was allowed into the drift of the price. The equation itself is a short sentence. The closed form is the solution of that sentence for the call. You should be able to point at a term in the equation and at the corresponding habit in the closed form. The r in front of S f sub S becomes the carry inside d1. The minus r f becomes the discount on the strike. The one half sigma squared term becomes the one half sigma squared inside d1 and the gap between d1 and d2. The next block computes those pieces. This block’s job is to refuse any solution procedure that still asks for mu.

What the claim does not mean is a list of neighboring models you have not solved. It does not mean every derivative contract satisfies this equation regardless of payoff. Change the terminal condition and you change the contract. It does not mean the equation prices the stock’s drift. The stock’s mean path can be anything mu says, and the call price does not consult it. It does not mean a low price from the equation is a bargain. The equation is a hedging price under the stated assumptions. A market price that differs from it is a different object, and this lecture does not give you a trading rule that converts the difference into a position. It does not mean the fan of paths is irrelevant to a person who cares about the physical distribution of S. The fan is the previous lecture. The equation is this one. Keeping them in different sentences is the discipline. It does not mean you may drop the curvature term if the option is far from the strike and “almost linear.” Far from the strike the second derivative of the call price becomes small, and the term shrinks for that reason, which you can see later when a deep in-the-money call approaches S minus the discounted strike. You do not get to drop the term at the start of the derivation because you hope you are far from the strike. The derivation did not assume a region. The curvature term is in the equation everywhere. It is merely small in places, after you have solved it.

[[BEAT 0]]
Three orders cover this protocol. You do not need a fourth word to publish a payload through the ring. Relaxed is enough for a counter that only one thread writes, when no payload depends on that counter. Acquire on a load pairs with release on a store. If you see the release, you see the writes that happened before it in that thread. The payload store happens before the sequence release. The consumer’s acquire of the sequence happens before the payload read. That pairing is the whole publication story. The reference you trust for the orders is the language reference, not these lecture notes. The notes are placement. The reference is the definition.

Say the placement as a sequence of actions you could point at in the listing. The producer writes the data. Then it release-stores the sequence ticket, and the value it stores is head plus one. The consumer acquire-loads that sequence. If the load observes the stored ticket, the consumer then reads the data. Writes that happened before the release, in the producer’s thread, become visible to the consumer after the acquire. A release store synchronizes with an acquire load that reads the value it stored. That sentence is the one the notes tell you to keep. Everything else is where you put the two operations.

Put numbers under the sentence so it cannot stay a slogan. Let head be five. The producer writes the payload for that ticket, then release-stores the sequence as head plus one. Five plus one is six. The ticket value six is what the release publishes. Suppose the consumer’s tail is also five. The consumer is allowed to read when the sequence has reached tail plus one. That sum is six as well. If the acquire load returns six, the ticket has arrived, and the payload writes that preceded the release are visible to this consumer. If the acquire load returns five, the sequence has not reached six. The slide’s empty test is exactly that: the sequence has not reached tail plus one, so the consumer treats the slot as empty. The difference is five minus six, which is negative one. A negative difference means the ticket is not yours yet. You do not read the payload on a negative difference. The order pair never got a chance to fire, because the acquire did not observe the release’s value.

Now the producer’s mirror of the same test. The slide says that on the producer side, full means the sequence is still behind head. Let the sequence sitting in the cell be four, and let head be five. Four is behind five. The difference, sequence minus head, is negative one, which is less than zero, so the ticket is not yours yet and the producer must not overwrite the cell. Full and empty can both be a negative difference. They are negative against different references: head for the producer, tail plus one for the consumer. The order words do not decide which reference. The protocol does. Relaxed, acquire, and release decide what else becomes visible once a test has passed.

Change one input. Move head from five to eight, and recompute the release and the empty test. The release store publishes head plus one, which is nine. If the consumer’s tail is eight, tail plus one is nine, so an acquire that returns nine may proceed to the payload, and an acquire that returns eight may not. Eight minus nine is negative one, still not yours. If instead the cell’s sequence is seven while head is eight, the producer sees seven behind eight, difference negative one, and the cell is full. The arithmetic shifted by three because head shifted by three. The shape of the tests did not change, and the placement of the orders did not change. Payload store, then release of the new ticket, then, on the other side, acquire of that ticket, then payload read.

You might ask why the compare-and-swap on head is relaxed, if publication is so delicate. Because the ticket, not the counter, publishes the data. The slide says this directly. The counter head is a value only one side updates in the single-producer case, and no payload rides on that counter’s store. Relaxed is the order the lecture assigns to that situation. The release is on the sequence store that happens after the payload write. If you moved the release onto head and left the sequence relaxed, you would be publishing a different variable from the one the consumer acquires. The consumer is told to acquire the sequence. A release the consumer never acquires does not bring the payload across. Placement is the design. The relaxed counter is relaxed on purpose.

The weak compare-and-exchange is why the loop exists. The slide names spurious failure as the reason. The operation is allowed to fail even when you might have expected it to succeed, so a single attempt is not a protocol. The loop retries. One producer could skip the compare-and-swap entirely, because a single writer does not need to race itself for head. The listed code keeps the compare-and-swap so the protocol stays obvious. Keeping it is a teaching choice about the listing, not a claim that the single producer is logically required to fail and retry. If you delete it, you must still publish with the release on the sequence after the payload store. If you keep it, the loop stays, and the order on that compare-and-swap stays relaxed.

What these three orders do not mean is that every atomic in the program becomes correct once the words appear somewhere. Relaxed on the publishing store would drop the pairing. Acquire on a load that reads the wrong variable would pair with nothing. Release of sequence equal to head plus one, with the payload write still in the future, would publish a ticket for data you have not stored. The formula is an order of actions, not a set of decorations. What they also do not mean is that the notes have replaced the language reference. The notes say the reference is the source and the lecture notes are not. You use the notes to remember placement: write data, release the sequence as head plus one, acquire the sequence, read data. You use the reference when you need the definition of synchronizes-with, not a paraphrase from a classroom.

Walk one consumer step that fails and one that succeeds, with tail held at five and the released value at six, so success and failure are adjacent integers. Failure: the acquire observes five. Tail plus one is six. Five has not reached six. Difference negative one. The consumer does not read the payload. There is no claim, on this attempt, that any particular producer write is visible, because the acquire did not observe the release. Success: a later acquire observes six. The load read the value the release stored. Writes before that release, in the producer thread, are visible after this acquire. The consumer may read the payload, and it must do that read after the successful acquire, not before. Reordering the read upward, ahead of the acquire, would look at the payload without the pairing the protocol relies on. The order is a constraint on your code, not a mood you add in a comment.

A second small case, on the full side only, so the producer’s test is not just the mirror sentence. Head is twenty. The cell sequence is twenty. Twenty is not behind twenty. The difference is zero, which is not less than zero. The “still behind” test does not fire. The producer may treat the ticket as available, write the payload, and only then release-store twenty-one. If the sequence were nineteen, nineteen is behind twenty, the difference is negative one, and the producer waits. Zero and negative one are the whole local distinction in this example. You do not need a third status. The slide gives you full as “sequence is still behind head,” and the notes give you negative difference as “not yours yet.” When the difference is not negative, the ticket is yours to use, and the orders still have to be placed in the sequence the formula states.

Remember the single-writer condition on relaxed, because it is easy to generalize one step too far. Relaxed is enough for a counter only one thread writes, and only when no payload depends on it. Head in this listing is that kind of counter if one producer owns it. The sequence is not that kind of variable, because the payload depends on it: seeing the sequence is how the consumer earns the right to read. So the sequence store is the release, and the sequence load is the acquire. Same protocol, two different orders, chosen by whether data depends on the variable. That dependency test is the design rule hiding inside the three words.

You leave the beat able to narrate one publication with integers. Head five, payload written, sequence released as six, consumer with tail five acquires six and only then reads, or acquires something short of six and does not. Head eight shifts the ticket to nine. A sequence of four against head five is full. The compare-and-swap on head stays relaxed and looped because the weak form can fail spuriously, even though one producer could have skipped it to keep the listing shorter. The notes are not the reference. The placement is.

[[BEAT 1]]
Sequentially consistent is not a design. The order that asks for a total order across the atomics will often appear to work, and it will hide which edge you actually needed. You use it to bisect a bug. You do not leave it as the architecture of the ring.

Appearance is the trap, so start with a run that looks fine and say precisely what it does not tell you. Suppose the producer still writes the payload and then stores the sequence as head plus one, and the consumer still loads the sequence and then reads the payload, but both atomic operations are tagged with the strongest order instead of release and acquire. Let head be five again, so the stored ticket is six, and let the consumer’s tail be five, so the consumer is waiting to observe six. If the program is correct for other reasons, the consumer will often observe six and then see the payload. The run looks like publication. You have not learned whether the release-acquire edge was the edge you needed, because a stronger order can paper over a missing edge and also over an edge that was present. A passing run is not a diagram. The lecture’s warning is that the strong order hides the edge. Hiding is not the same as documenting.

Bisect means you change one thing, in one place, for a short experiment. Take a broken publication, the kind where the payload read sometimes shows a stale record, and temporarily strengthen a single operation to the total order while you watch whether the symptom moves. If you strengthen the sequence store and the symptom vanishes, you have a clue that the store’s order was involved. If you strengthen it and the symptom stays, you have a clue that this store was not the missing edge, and you put the operation back. The clue is not a design. The design is the placement you already have: relaxed on the counter that does not publish payload, release on the sequence store after the data write, acquire on the sequence load before the data read. When the bisection is over, those are the words that remain. Leaving the strongest order in place because the symptom went quiet is how a debugging scaffold becomes the architecture.

Change one input in the ticket arithmetic so the bisection is not glued to the number five. Let head be eleven. The release, or the temporarily strengthened store, publishes twelve. The consumer whose tail is eleven needs the sequence to reach twelve. Eleven plus one is twelve. If a strengthened load observes twelve and the payload is intact, you still do not know which order was necessary. You know the symptom, under this ticket, did not appear on this run. If the load observes eleven, the sequence has not reached tail plus one, the difference is negative one, and the consumer must not read. Strengthening the order does not turn a negative difference into permission. The empty test is still a comparison. The order is still about what a successful observation carries. A bug in the comparison will survive any order you attach, which is why the next beat separates index arithmetic from publication. For this beat, the point of changing eleven for five is only that the scaffold looks the same at a different ticket. The scaffold is still temporary.

You might ask whether the strongest order is safer to ship, because a hidden edge that happens to work is better than a missed edge that fails. Safer is not what the lecture claims. It claims the opposite trade. The strong order will often appear to work. That appearance removes the pressure to name the edge, and an unnamed edge is how the next edit breaks the protocol without anyone knowing which line was load-bearing. A release that is actually doing the publication can be pointed at. A total order that happens to include the publication cannot be pointed at, because everything is in it. You cannot review “everything.” You can review “the sequence store is the release, and it comes after the payload.” Review is the reason the design uses the narrow orders. The strong order remains available for the afternoon you are bisecting, and it leaves with the afternoon.

What shipping the strong order does not mean, even for one bug, is that you have explained the protocol to the next reader. The listing would then say that every atomic participates in one total order, and the reader would be unable to tell that the ticket, not the counter, publishes the data. The slide on the compare-and-swap is specific: that operation is relaxed because the ticket publishes and the counter does not. If you promote the compare-and-swap to the strongest order, the listing no longer says that. You have hidden a negative fact, the fact that this variable carries no payload, which is as much a part of the design as the positive fact that the sequence does. What the bisection also does not mean is that a single passing seed is a proof. The notes send you to the language reference for the definition. A quiet run is not that reference. You can use the run to decide which operation to inspect. You cannot use the run as the theory of publication.

Consider a concrete mis-tag and what the strong order would conceal. The producer writes the payload, then stores the sequence with a relaxed store, and the consumer acquire-loads the sequence. The acquire has nothing to synchronize with if the store was not a release. Sometimes, on some machines, the payload will still be visible, because the hardware happened to keep the writes in an order that looks right. The program appears to work. The edge the lecture requires is absent. If you now set both operations to the strongest order, the appearance continues, and the diff against the previous binary is a change of tags rather than the insertion of the missing release. You have made the bug harder to see, which is the concealment the lecture names. The repair is to put the release back on the sequence store and the acquire back on the load, and to return any temporary total order to the narrow tag the design chose. The repair is a placement, and you can read it aloud: write the data, release the sequence as head plus one, acquire the sequence, read the data.

Try the same concealment on the full test, so you do not think orders only matter for the consumer. Head is thirty. The cell sequence is twenty-nine. Twenty-nine is behind thirty, so the producer must not write. That decision is a comparison of integers. No memory order makes twenty-nine catch up to thirty. If you tag the load of the sequence with the strongest order, the load is still obliged to return a value the protocol stored, and twenty-nine still means full. Students sometimes reach for a stronger order when a slot looks mysteriously full, as if the order could complete a ticket that has not been published. It cannot. The order constrains visibility of writes that happened. It does not invent writes. If the sequence is behind head, the cell is full, under the slide’s own sentence, regardless of which legal order you used to load it.

A second changed input, this time on which operation you strengthen, so bisection stays a method rather than a habit of strengthening everything. First experiment: only the sequence store goes to the total order, for one debugging run, head at eleven, ticket twelve. Second experiment: you put that store back, and only the consumer’s sequence load goes to the total order. You do not run a third experiment in which every atomic in the file changes at once, because then you cannot tell which edge moved the symptom. Two experiments, one operation each, is the meaning of bisect here. If neither experiment changes the symptom, you stop blaming the tags and you look at the comparison, the slot, and the question of whether capacity and the mask agree. That look is the next beat. The discipline is the same one you use on any other bug: one change, a prediction, a return to the design when the experiment ends.

What you should be able to say at the end of the beat is short enough to interrupt a review. The strongest order often appears to work. You may use it to bisect, one operation at a time, and then you remove it. The architecture that remains is relaxed where no payload depends on a single writer’s counter, release on the sequence after the payload, and acquire on the sequence before the read. Head five publishes six. Head eleven publishes twelve. A sequence that has not reached tail plus one is empty. A sequence still behind head is full. None of those comparisons become true because the tag got stronger. And the language reference, not the quiet run and not these notes, is the source for what the tags mean.

[[BEAT 2]]
None of the orders repair a wrong slot. Atomics publish a value. They do not check your index arithmetic. If the capacity is not a power of two, the mask lab already showed the bug, and a careful release will publish it faithfully into the wrong place. The address calculation sits under the atomics. You confirm the identity, and then you go back to the publish order, and you keep those two checks in that sequence because neither one implies the other.

The identity you are confirming is the one the mask lab exists to show. When the capacity is a power of two, and the counter you mask is nonnegative, the remainder on division by the capacity equals the counter combined with a mask of capacity minus one, using a bitwise and. The mask is a string of ones in the low bits. The low bits are the slot. The identity is false as soon as the capacity is not a power of two. No memory order appears in that sentence. There is nowhere to put one. The arithmetic either holds or it does not, before you store anything.

Do it with a capacity of eight, which is two to the third. Capacity minus one is seven. Seven in low bits is three ones. Take the counter thirteen. Thirteen divided by eight is one, with remainder five, because eight plus five is thirteen. So thirteen modulo eight is five. The bitwise side: thirteen is written with bits that, masked by seven, keep the low three bits. Those low bits are one, zero, one, which is four plus one, which is five. Five equals five. The slot is five. A release store of a ticket into slot five publishes that ticket at slot five. An acquire that looks at slot five can observe it. An acquire that looks at slot four cannot, and no strengthening of the load will reach across and fetch the other slot for you. The order pairs with a location. You chose the location with the mask.

Change one input. Keep the counter at thirteen and change the capacity from eight to twelve. Twelve is not a power of two. The honest remainder is thirteen modulo twelve, which is one, because twelve plus one is thirteen. The mask a careless port still builds is twelve minus one, which is eleven. Thirteen combined with eleven by a bitwise and is not one. Thirteen’s low bits against eleven leave nine: the bits of thirteen that survive the and are the bits of nine. Nine is not one. The identity failed. Slot nine and slot one are different cells. If you write the payload into slot nine and release the sequence there, the consumer that computes the honest remainder and looks in slot one will acquire nothing that you published, or will acquire an older ticket that really does live in slot one. If the consumer makes the same mistaken mask, both sides agree on slot nine and you have a consistent wrong map until some third piece of code uses division. Consistency of a bug is not correctness. The lab’s point in flipping the capacity off the power of two is that the two calculations diverge. You just watched them diverge, from five-equals-five into one-versus-nine.

A second power of two, so the identity is not a souvenir of the number eight. Let the capacity be sixteen, two to the fourth, so the mask is fifteen, four low ones. Take the counter twenty. Twenty divided by sixteen is one, remainder four, because sixteen plus four is twenty. Twenty masked by fifteen keeps the low four bits, which are four. Four equals four. Slot four is the address calculation. Now change only the capacity, from sixteen to twelve, and keep twenty. Twenty modulo twelve is eight, because twelve plus eight is twenty. Twenty masked by eleven is zero, because the bits that eleven lets through do not include the bit worth sixteen, and twenty is sixteen plus four, and four masked against eleven’s pattern in this case does not survive as eight. Zero is not eight. Same counter, one changed capacity, two slots. A release of head plus one into slot zero does not fill slot eight. The consumer’s acquire is blameless. It synchronized with whatever was actually stored at the address you gave it.

You might ask whether a sequentially consistent store would catch the bad mask, since the previous beat gave that order so much power to hide edges. It would not catch this. The strong order constrains the order of atomic operations. It does not check that a bitwise and equals a remainder. You can bisect publication bugs by strengthening a tag, and you can still compute slot zero when the remainder was eight, and the strengthened store will very reliably publish the wrong cell. A store publishes the value you gave it, at the address you gave it, and the tag does not audit that address. That limit sits on every order you have been given, including the one you are not allowed to leave in as the architecture. If a test fails only when the capacity is twelve and passes when the capacity is eight or sixteen, you are not looking at acquire and release. You are looking at the mask.

What a correct identity does not mean is that the protocol’s full and empty tests have become unnecessary. Slot four under capacity sixteen can be the slot a producer wants and the slot a consumer is finished with, at different laps, because the counter keeps growing while the slot wraps. The lecture does not ask the mask to tell those laps apart. The sequence ticket does that. The producer’s full test is whether the sequence is still behind head. The consumer’s empty test is whether the sequence has reached tail plus one. Both tests can fail while the masked slot number is the same small integer. Seeing slot four empty of permission is not a mask failure. It is a negative difference on the ticket. You fix that by waiting, not by widening the capacity to the next power of two. Widening would change the map. It would not publish a ticket that has not been stored.

Work the ticket and the slot together on the capacity-eight case, so the two mechanisms stay in one story. Head is thirteen. The slot, by the identity, is five. The producer writes the payload at slot five, then release-stores the sequence as fourteen, because head plus one is fourteen. The consumer with tail thirteen acquire-loads the sequence at slot five. If it observes fourteen, it may read the payload at that same slot. If it observes a smaller ticket, the difference against tail plus one is negative, and it does not read. Suppose a bug computes the slot as thirteen masked by something other than seven, and lands on a different index. Then the release of fourteen happens in the wrong cell, and the consumer waiting on slot five does not see it. Strengthening both atomics changes nothing about the index. You confirm the identity first: thirteen modulo eight is five, and thirteen masked by seven is five. Then you confirm the order: payload, then release of fourteen, then acquire, then read. The lab brief for this lesson is exactly that double confirmation. The mask lab is the address. The notes are the publish order, read until you can say them without the slide.

Try the failing capacity on the same head, so one input changes and the publication follows the arithmetic into the ditch. Head is thirteen, capacity is twelve, honest slot is one, masked slot is nine, as computed a moment ago. The producer that trusts the mask writes the payload at nine and release-stores fourteen at nine. The consumer that trusts division waits at slot one for a sequence that reaches fourteen. The acquire at slot one does not observe the release at slot nine, because synchronizes-with is a relation between a store and a load that reads the value that store wrote, at that location. Different locations do not pair. You will see an empty consumer and a producer who believes the ticket went out. Both are behaving correctly relative to the addresses they computed. The bug is that the addresses disagree. No tag on the store repairs a disagreement about where the cell is.

There is a tempting repair you should refuse. You might keep the bad capacity and replace the mask with an actual remainder operation, so thirteen modulo twelve is one on both sides. The slots would agree. You would also have put a division on the path whose cost the mask lab is about, and you would have ignored the lesson that the identity is false as soon as the modulus is not a power of two. The ring this protocol indexes is built for the mask. The static shape of that choice is a capacity that is a power of two, checked where the type is defined, not a remainder you compute because you would rather not think about the failure. If you need a non-power-of-two length, you do not quietly keep the and. You have left the identity the lab asked you to confirm. Say so, and use an operation that really is the remainder, knowing you have changed the cost model. Do not use the and and hope acquire is clever.

What the identity also does not mean is that a power-of-two capacity makes the compare-and-swap mandatory, or makes relaxed inappropriate, or makes the strongest order acceptable as a design. Those choices were settled by who publishes the payload. They survive any capacity that is a power of two, and they remain wrong for the same reasons when the capacity is not. Capacity eight, slot of thirteen equal to five, sequence released as fourteen: the store is still a release, the load is still an acquire, the counter update is still relaxed if one thread owns it and no payload depends on it, and the weak compare-and-exchange still needs its loop if you kept it so the listing stays obvious. Capacity twelve, slots one and nine: the same order tags, now attached to a false address. The tags did not become the bug, and they did not become the fix.

One more pair, small enough to do while someone objects. Capacity thirty-two, which is two to the fifth, mask thirty-one. Counter thirty-two. Thirty-two modulo thirty-two is zero. Thirty-two masked by thirty-one is zero, because thirty-two is a single bit just above the five low ones, and the mask clears it. Slot zero. Change the capacity to thirty, which is not a power of two. Thirty-two modulo thirty is two, because thirty plus two is thirty-two. Thirty-two masked by twenty-nine: thirty-two is bigger than twenty-nine and is a power of two itself, so the and yields zero. Two versus zero. Again the identity fails, and again a release into slot zero is not a release into slot two. You do not need a new theory for thirty. The same sentence covers it. Power of two, nonnegative counter, mask of all low ones, remainder equals the and. Otherwise not.

If you want the publish formula in the same breath as the slot, say it with the numbers attached and without a slide. Capacity eight. Counter thirteen. Slot five, because the identity holds. Write the payload at slot five. Release the sequence as fourteen. The consumer acquires the sequence at slot five. Only if that acquire observes fourteen does the consumer read the payload at slot five. If the capacity is twelve, you do not get to say slot five at all: the remainder says one, the mistaken mask says nine, and you stop and fix the capacity before you argue about tags. Sequentially consistent operations at slot nine still do not fill slot one. The lecture notes are still not the language reference. They are the placement you had to be able to say, and you just said it, under an address the identity actually justifies.

The closing limit is the one the beat opened with, now with the arithmetic behind it. Orders move values that were stored, to loads that observe those stores, at the locations you computed. They do not test whether capacity minus one is a string of low ones. Eight and sixteen and thirty-two pass that test for the counters you tried. Twelve and thirty fail it, and the failing pairs were one against nine, eight against zero, and two against zero. You confirm the identity in the mask lab. You read the publish order until the placement is boring. You do not ask one of those tools to do the other tool’s job.

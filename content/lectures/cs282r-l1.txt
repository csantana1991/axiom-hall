[[BEAT 0]]

Name the idea before you touch a model. Dependence is the object. A supervised model that assumes independent rows is a model of a shuffled market. Returns have dependence, volatility clustering, and a clock that is not the row index. You can still train on rows. You cannot score them as if the shuffle were the exchange.

Say the same thing at the speed of a definition. In the usual supervised table, a row is a bundle of inputs and a label. The fitting procedure averages a loss over rows. That average does not consult the order of the rows. The algebra will run if you shuffle. The market will not become the shuffled market. If your score is the score of the shuffled table, you have measured a different experiment and given it the original name.

Dependence here is not a vague warning. It is the claim that the joint behavior of neighboring times is part of the problem. Volatility clustering is one concrete form of that claim. Large moves tend to sit near other large moves. Quiet stretches tend to sit near other quiet stretches. A clock that is not the row index means that row four hundred is a time, or a place in a sequence of times, and not an independent draw that happens to have been written on line four hundred. Adjacent seconds share a regime. That sentence is the whole reason a random split is dangerous.

You are allowed to train on rows. Packing a decision into a feature vector is a representation, not a confession that time is fake. The optimizer can see those vectors one at a time. The prohibition sits on the evaluation. A random split puts the future in the training set. The score is then a memory test. It asks whether the fit remembers a neighborhood it was allowed to peek into, not whether a decision at that second, using only what was knowable then, would have been any good.

Work the split as a count, not as a mood. Take ten consecutive seconds, numbered zero through nine. Suppose the training set is seconds zero, one, three, four, six, and eight, and the test set is seconds two, five, seven, and nine. Four seconds are being scored. Look at each of them and ask whether any later second was placed in training.

Second two is a test second. Seconds three and four are later, and both are in training. That test second is contaminated. Second five is a test second. Seconds six and eight are later and in training. Contaminated. Second seven is a test second. Second eight is later and in training. Contaminated. Second nine is a test second, and nothing in the sample comes after it. That one is clean, and it is clean only because the tape ended. Three of the four test seconds have a future neighbor inside the training set. You do not need a large theorem. You need to compare integers.

Now take the same ten seconds and refuse the shuffle. Train on zero through six. Test on seven, eight, and nine. Every training timestamp is strictly earlier than every test timestamp. The contamination count is zero. This is not yet the full rolling design you will write down later. It is only the opposite of the random partition. The comparison is the lesson. "I held out some rows" is a statement about a set. "I did not train on the future" is a statement about order. They are not the same statement.

Do the count once more on a pattern that looks fair when you glance down a column. Take seconds zero through five. Put the even seconds in training and the odd seconds in test. Training holds zero, two, and four. Test holds one, three, and five. Test second one has two and four in its future, both training. Test second three has four in its future, and four is training. Test second five has no future inside the sample. Two of the three test points are leaked by a split that alternates. Alternation feels mixed. On a clock it is a systematic gift of the future to the fit. The feeling that a spreadsheet looks shuffled is not a check. The timestamps are the check.

If you want the clean contrast on those same six seconds, train on zero, one, and two, and test on three, four, and five. The contamination count is zero. The sample is tiny, so the estimate will be noisy. Noise is not leakage. You may report a noisy honest split. You may not repair the noise by borrowing a later row and then describing the borrow as a larger sample.

A second numerical picture, so clustering is not only a word. This tape is an illustration of neighboring products, not a recording from the lab and not a market. Let the absolute returns across the ten seconds be one, one, one, eight, nine, seven, one, one, two, and one. The large values occupy seconds three, four, and five, counting from zero. The mean of the ten numbers does not care about that clump. Add them: three ones at the start are three, plus eight is eleven, plus nine is twenty, plus seven is twenty-seven, plus one is twenty-eight, plus one is twenty-nine, plus two is thirty-one, plus one is thirty-two. Thirty-two divided by ten is three and one fifth. Any shuffle of the same ten numbers has the same mean.

Neighboring products do care. On the original order the nine successive products are one times one, one times one, one times eight, eight times nine, nine times seven, seven times one, one times one, one times two, and two times one. That list is one, one, eight, seventy-two, sixty-three, seven, one, two, and two. Add it carefully. One plus one is two. Plus eight is ten. Plus seventy-two is eighty-two. Plus sixty-three is one hundred forty-five. Plus seven is one hundred fifty-two. Plus one is one hundred fifty-three. Plus two is one hundred fifty-five. Plus two is one hundred fifty-seven. One hundred fifty-seven over nine is seventeen and four ninths. The size of that average is the cluster: eight times nine, and nine times seven, dominate the sum.

Separate the cluster and the products collapse. Suppose the eight, the nine, and the seven are moved so that none of them touch, for example the order one, eight, one, nine, one, seven, one, two, one, one. The successive products are eight, eight, nine, nine, seven, seven, two, two, and one. Add: eight plus eight is sixteen, plus nine is twenty-five, plus nine is thirty-four, plus seven is forty-one, plus seven is forty-eight, plus two is fifty, plus two is fifty-two, plus one is fifty-three. Fifty-three over nine is five and eight ninths. Same ten numbers, same mean of three and one fifth, and the average neighboring product has fallen from seventeen and four ninths to five and eight ninths. If your evaluation is about what sits next to what, the shuffle is a different question. Volatility clustering is exactly a claim about what sits next to what.

The question I want on the table is whether dependence forbids a model that reads one row at a time. It does not. A row can hold a short trail of earlier information, written into columns before the fit begins. The model can be a function of that row alone. You have either written the dependence into the features or you have chosen not to, but you have not thereby earned the right to shuffle the score. The question is not whether the architecture looks like a table model. The question is whether the number you are about to quote could have been computed at the decision time.

What the claim does not mean is that dependence is a small correction you mention after an otherwise independent table. It also does not mean that every average is illegitimate. The mean of the absolute returns survived the shuffle, and it should have, because a mean is not a claim about neighbors. The failure is specific. If you needed the path, and you destroyed the path, you do not get to keep the path's conclusions. A day that is quiet, then violent, then quiet is not the same problem as the same rows presented in an order the exchange never used, even when the multiset of rows is identical.

Keep the lab in its place while you hold this idea. The attention lab feeds six signed imbalance numbers. They are a toy sequence, not a market. In this hour you leave the formula alone and read those six numbers as a sequence in time. The highlighted query is now. The weights, when you come to them, say which past ticks this toy kernel considers similar. They are not a license to forget which tick came first. A real book tensor has levels, sides, and a clock. You build that after a weight row is something you can recompute by hand, and you do not build it by shuffling the clock away.

[[BEAT 1]]

The decision in this part of the hour is stationarity. Raw prices wander. Returns are closer to something you can estimate, and they are still not stationary across a regime change. Differencing is not a theory. You have to say what you assume is stable. The candidates named for that sentence are a conditional mean, a covariance, or a book shape. Pick one, and say it out loud. If you cannot say it, you do not have a stationarity assumption. You have a habit.

Start with the wander, on a short price path that is only an illustration. Let the prices be one hundred, one hundred two, one hundred one, one hundred four, one hundred three, and one hundred seven. The mean of the first three prices is one hundred plus one hundred two plus one hundred one, which is three hundred three, divided by three, which is one hundred one. The mean of the last three is one hundred four plus one hundred three plus one hundred seven, which is three hundred fourteen, divided by three, which is one hundred four and two thirds. The level moved. If you had treated the price itself as a draw from one fixed distribution, the first half of this tiny sample and the second half are already disagreeing about the center.

The differences of those prices are plus two, minus one, plus three, minus one, and plus four. Their sum is seven. Their mean is seven fifths, which is one and two fifths. Differencing removed the most obvious wander of the level. That is a calculation, not a theory of the market. Nothing in the subtraction tells you that the next difference will be drawn from the same distribution as these five. It tells you that you changed the object you are holding.

Now use the return definitions you are responsible for, on a different illustration, so price differences and returns do not get blurred. A simple return is a ratio minus one. Start at one hundred, go to one hundred five, stay at one hundred five, and then go to ninety-four and one half. The first simple return is one hundred five divided by one hundred, minus one, which is one twentieth, or five hundredths. The second is one hundred five divided by one hundred five, minus one, which is zero. The third is ninety-four and one half divided by one hundred five, minus one. Ninety-four and one half over one hundred five is nine tenths, because ninety-four and one half divided by one hundred five equals zero point nine. Nine tenths minus one is negative one tenth.

Add those simple returns: five hundredths, plus zero, plus negative one tenth. Five hundredths is one twentieth, and one tenth is two twentieths, so the sum is negative one twentieth, or negative five hundredths. The price itself went from one hundred to ninety-four and one half, which is a simple return of ninety-four and one half over one hundred, minus one, or negative five and one half hundredths. Negative five hundredths is not negative five and one half hundredths. The sum of the simple returns missed the compound move by half a hundredth. Simple returns do not add across time. If your feature, your label, or your story assumes that they do, the story is wrong by arithmetic, not by opinion.

A log return does add across time. The log return on a step is the natural log of the price ratio, which is the same as the natural log of one plus the simple return. On this path the three log returns are the natural log of one point zero five, the natural log of one, and the natural log of nine tenths. The natural log of one is zero. The natural log of one point zero five is about zero point zero four eight eight. The natural log of nine tenths is about negative zero point one zero five four. Their sum is about negative zero point zero five six six. That sum is the natural log of zero point nine four five, because one point zero five times nine tenths is zero point nine four five, and the log of a product is the sum of the logs. Exponentiate and multiply by the starting price of one hundred and you are back at ninety-four and one half. The adding worked. The path was recovered.

Do not let that success smuggle in a second claim. Neither the simple return nor the log return makes the series independent and identically distributed. The adding is an algebraic property of the logarithm. Independence is a statement about information across time. You can add dependent numbers. Accountants add dependent numbers every day. The logarithm did not consult the joint distribution.

Take a second path where the gap between adding and not adding is even less polite. Go from one hundred to eighty, then from eighty to one hundred twenty. The simple returns are eighty over one hundred minus one, which is negative one fifth, and one hundred twenty over eighty minus one, which is one half. Negative one fifth plus one half is negative two tenths plus five tenths, which is three tenths. The direct simple return from one hundred to one hundred twenty is one fifth, or two tenths. Three tenths is not two tenths. If you summed simple returns and called the sum the result of the trade, you invented four tenths of the starting price out of nothing. The log returns are the natural log of eight tenths, about negative zero point two two three one, and the natural log of one and one half, about zero point four zero five five. Their sum is about zero point one eight two three, which is the natural log of one point two. The compound move is one fifth, and the logs recovered it. Again, the recovery is algebra. It is not a stationarity proof.

Here is the regime case, so "still not stationary across a regime change" has a number. This sequence is constructed so you can see two moments disagree. It is not a fitted model and not a lab output. The first six returns are one hundredth, negative one hundredth, two hundredths, negative two hundredths, one hundredth, and zero. Their sum is one hundredth. Their mean is one six-hundredth. Their squares are one, one, four, four, one, and zero, in units of one ten-thousandth. The sum of squares is eleven ten-thousandths. The mean square is eleven over sixty thousand.

The next four returns are five hundredths, negative four hundredths, six hundredths, and negative seven hundredths. Their sum is zero. Their mean is zero. Their squares, in units of one ten-thousandth, are twenty-five, sixteen, thirty-six, and forty-nine. That sum is one hundred twenty-six ten-thousandths. The mean square is one hundred twenty-six ten-thousandths divided by four, which is three hundred fifteen ten-thousandths of a hundredth... do the division directly. One hundred twenty-six over ten thousand, divided by four, is one hundred twenty-six over forty thousand, which is zero point zero zero three one five. Compare that with eleven over sixty thousand. The ratio of the second mean square to the first is one hundred eighty-nine over eleven, which is seventeen and two elevenths. The means were one six-hundredth and zero, close to each other on the scale of these numbers. The mean squares were not close. If you had declared "the mean looks stable, so I differenced and I am done," you would have missed a variance that changed by more than seventeen times inside a ten-step illustration.

That is why the assumption has to name its object. You might assume a conditional mean is stable. You might assume a covariance is stable. You might assume a book shape is stable, meaning the arrangement of sizes and prices you are about to turn into features does not silently change its meaning in the middle of the sample. Those are three different sentences. Differencing the price speaks to none of them until you attach it to one of them. A log return can be the series on which you then state the assumption. It is not a substitute for the assumption.

The question from the room is usually this: if log returns add, and if they look more stable than the price, have I made the series stationary? You have not. You have constructed a series that is closer to something you can estimate. Closeness is not a proof. Across a regime change the new series can still change its mean, its variance, or both, as the ten-step illustration just did for the variance while the means stayed near zero. Stationarity is a decision you state, and then you live with the ways it can fail. It is not a property you earn by choosing the logarithm.

What the claim does not mean is that you should refuse returns and model raw prices out of stubbornness. Raw prices wander, and the first illustration showed a level moving from a mean of one hundred one to a mean of one hundred four and two thirds in six prints. Returns are the more reasonable object. The claim also does not mean that a regime change makes the algebra of the variance formula false. The squares still added. What the regime change limits is what you may say out loud about the next day. You may not say that a covariance estimated on the quiet block is the covariance of the violent block, unless that is the assumption you are willing to defend as an assumption rather than as a fact the difference operator proved.

When you look at the six signed imbalance numbers in the lab, read them as a sequence whose stability you have not been given. They are a toy. They do not settle whether a conditional mean, a covariance, or a book shape is the stable object in a real book. They sit in time so that the weighted average you will compute later has a past and a now. Stationarity is not hiding inside that toy, and it will not start hiding there if you difference the six numbers without saying what the difference was supposed to stabilize.

[[BEAT 2]]

The third idea is features from a book. Imbalance, spread, and a short trail of signed trades are features with units. You feed them to a model only after you have frozen the code that computes them. A feature that uses the next mid is leakage with a polite name. Use only information available at decision time. Write the timestamp of each input. If you cannot, you are not ready to fit.

Units are part of the feature, not a caption you add if the plot has room. An imbalance is not a price. A spread is not a signed trade. A signed trade is not a return. If those three enter a model as naked numbers, the fit will treat a change of units as a change of the world. Freezing the code means the definition, the units, and the timestamp rule are fixed before you search for a model that flatters the label. If the definition is still moving while you tune, you are fitting the definition and the weights at once, and you will not know which one produced the number.

The lecture does not hand you a single official formula for imbalance. What it hands you is the obligation to freeze one. For this worked case, suppose the code you froze says that imbalance is bid size minus ask size, divided by their sum. That sentence is a choice of definition, written down so the arithmetic can be checked. It is not a claim that every desk uses those words. Bid size seventy, ask size thirty. The numerator is forty. The denominator is one hundred. The imbalance is two fifths, or zero point four. Change the book: bid size forty, ask size sixty. The numerator is negative twenty. The denominator is one hundred. The imbalance is negative one fifth, or negative zero point two. Both numbers are dimensionless ratios. They are not prices, and they are not share counts. If you later add this feature to a spread measured in dollars, you have added a pure number to a price, and the sum has no unit at all. The model can still multiply each by its own coefficient. You should still be able to say the unit of each input before you look at the coefficient.

Now the spread, on a book that matches the first imbalance. Suppose the bid price is one hundred and the ask price is one hundred and two hundredths. The spread is two hundredths of a price unit. If your price unit is a dollar, the spread is two cents. If the same quotes are stored as integer ticks of one cent, the spread is two ticks, and the number you feed the model is two, not zero point zero two. Those are the same book and different features. A coefficient fit on the dollar spread is not a coefficient you may paste onto the tick spread. Freezing the code includes freezing that choice. Write it next to the timestamp.

A short trail of signed trades has units too, and it has a length you must state. Suppose the sign is plus one for a trade you have defined as buying and minus one for a trade you have defined as selling, and the trail at the decision time is the last three such signs: plus one, plus one, minus one. You might feed the three signs as three features, or you might feed their sum, which is plus one. The sum is a count of net sign, not a quantity of shares, unless you froze a different code that multiplies sign by size. If size is ten, ten, and four, and you forgot to say whether size enters, then plus one plus one minus one equals plus one, while ten plus ten minus four equals sixteen. Plus one and sixteen are not interchangeable inputs. The trail is short only relative to a length you chose. Three trades is a different object from three seconds of trades. Write which one you froze.

The timestamp is how you audit the trail. Suppose the decision time is second ten. The three trades must carry timestamps at or before second ten. If one of them is stamped second eleven, it is not a feature of the decision at second ten, however natural it looked in the file. Write the timestamp of each input. If a column has no timestamp, and no rule that derives its timestamp from a timestamp you do have, you are not ready to fit. That is a practical test, not a style preference. You cannot check "available at decision time" on a column that does not say what time it is.

Work the leakage that wears a polite name. The decision is at time t. The label you care about is the next return, from the mid at t to the mid at t plus one. A simple return, as before, is a ratio minus one, so the label is the later mid divided by the earlier mid, minus one. Suppose the mid at t is one hundred and the mid at t plus one is one hundred one. The label is one hundredth. Now suppose a feature called a smoothed mid is the average of the mid at t minus one, the mid at t, and the mid at t plus one. Let the mid at t minus one be ninety-nine. The average is ninety-nine plus one hundred plus one hundred one, divided by three, which is three hundred, divided by three, which is one hundred. The number one hundred used the mid at t plus one. That mid is exactly the numerator of the label. You have placed the label's future inside the feature and given the feature a calm name. A feature that uses the next mid is leakage with a polite name.

See the arithmetic of the leak in the label itself. From mid one hundred to mid one hundred one, the simple return is one hundredth. Anyone who is handed the smoothed value one hundred, together with the current mid one hundred, still needs the past mid to back out the future, and in this example the past mid was ninety-nine. Then ninety-nine plus one hundred plus the unknown future, averaged, equals one hundred, so the unknown future is one hundred one. The feature handed you the label. This particular average was constructed to make the recovery obvious. The principle does not require the recovery to be obvious. If the next mid enters the feature by any path, the feature is not knowable at decision time.

Do a second case where the leak is smaller on the page and just as fatal. Decision at t, mid at t is fifty, mid at t plus one is fifty-one, so the next simple return is one fiftieth, or two hundredths. Your feature is not the whole future mid. It is a flag that is one when the next mid is above the current mid and zero otherwise. The flag is one. It does not reveal that the move was two hundredths rather than twenty hundredths. It reveals the sign of the label. If the label you actually fit is that same sign, the feature is the label. If the label you fit is the return itself, the feature is a piece of the label, and a piece is enough to call it leakage. "It was only a bit" is not a defense. Information that is not available at decision time does not become available by being coarse.

The question that usually arrives is whether you may keep editing the imbalance definition as you learn what predicts. You may edit it before the fit is a result you intend to report. You may not edit it on the test window and then talk as if the definition had been frozen. Freezing is a time order, the same kind of order as the split. Code, units, timestamps, and the label horizon are fixed on the past. Then you look forward. If you discover that a different imbalance would have been wiser, that discovery is a new experiment with a new freeze, not a correction you apply quietly to the old score.

What the claim does not mean is that a frozen feature is a good feature, or that a feature with careful units will forecast returns. Freezing stops one way of fooling yourself. It does not produce predictability. Imbalance of two fifths is a description of a book at a time. It is not yet a label, and it is not yet an expected return. The claim also does not mean that every use of a future print is a feature leak. A label is allowed to use the future. That is what a label is for. The crime is letting that future into the inputs, or into a scaler, or into any choice you pretend was fixed at the decision time. You will press on scalers and on the cut itself in the leakage hour. Here the obligation is narrower and already enough to stop a fit: timestamp every column, freeze the code, and refuse the next mid when it tries to enter disguised as a smoother.

When you return to the six signed imbalance numbers, treat them as six numbers already computed by frozen toy code, in time order, with the query at now. Do not invent a market behind them. Do not attach a spread in dollars to them and call the attachment a book. A real book tensor has levels, sides, and a clock. You earn that tensor by being able to recompute a weight row by hand, and by being able to point at the timestamp of every input that entered the row. If you cannot point, you are not ready to fit.

The board in front of you is titled beta is a regression coefficient. Beta of asset i equals the covariance of the asset's return with the market return, divided by the variance of the market return. It is the slope of a linear projection of the asset on the market. It is not a moral category, and it is not stable because you printed it. A printed slope is a number computed from a covariance and a variance you chose to measure. Print it again on a different window and you are allowed to get a different number. The algebra does not apologize for that, and it does not promote the number into a property of the business.

Say the division on a first case before you interpret it. Let the market variance be 0.04, so the market standard deviation is 0.20. Let the covariance of the asset with the market be 0.048. Beta equals 0.048 divided by 0.04, which is 1.2. Read the 1.2 as a slope. In the linear projection, when the market return is one unit above its mean, the asset's projected return is 1.2 units above its own mean. The projection is not a promise that the next outcome will land on the line. It is the line that covariance picks. The residual around that line is still there, and we will refuse to price it on the next board if it can be diversified. The slope itself is only the ratio you just divided.

The same ratio hides inside a correlation, and the hiding is why you should not swap the words. Correlation equals covariance divided by the product of the two standard deviations. Rearrange and beta equals correlation times the asset's standard deviation, divided by the market's standard deviation. Take correlation 0.80, an asset standard deviation of 0.30, and the market standard deviation 0.20. Beta equals 0.80 times 0.30 divided by 0.20, which is 0.80 times 1.5, which is 1.2. The covariance check is 0.80 times 0.30 times 0.20, which is 0.048, and 0.048 divided by 0.04 is the same 1.2. Correlation 0.80 and beta 1.2 are two reports of one covariance. They are not the same report. Correlation has no units. Beta is in units of asset return per unit of market return, which is what a regression slope has to be.

Change the asset's standard deviation and keep the correlation, so the distinction is forced. Correlation still 0.80, market standard deviation still 0.20, asset standard deviation now 0.10. Beta equals 0.80 times 0.10 divided by 0.20, which is 0.40. Same correlation as the previous asset, different slope. A listener who says both assets "move with the market the same way" because both have correlation 0.80 has thrown away the scale. The first asset's projection rises 1.2 when the market rises 1. The second rises 0.40. The pricing line on the next board is drawn against beta, not against correlation, and not against the asset's own standard deviation. If you price these two assets off their shared correlation, you will give them the same expected excess return. The slope says otherwise.

A weighted average of betas, using market weights, equals 1. This is an identity, not a hope about your sample. The market return is the weighted sum of the asset returns, with those market weights. Covariance with the market is linear, so the covariance of the market with itself equals the weighted sum of the covariances of the assets with the market. Divide by the market variance. The left side becomes 1. The right side becomes the weighted sum of the betas. On a two-asset market, let the weights be 0.40 and 0.60, and let the first beta be 1.5. Then 0.40 times 1.5 plus 0.60 times the second beta equals 1. That is 0.60 plus 0.60 times the second beta equals 1, so the second beta equals 0.40 divided by 0.60, which is two thirds. Check: 0.40 times 1.5 is 0.60, and 0.60 times two thirds is 0.40, and 0.60 plus 0.40 is 1. You do not get to assign betas 1.5 and 1.5 to both assets and still claim these are market weights in a market whose beta is 1. The identity will not allow it. One of the betas has to sit on the other side of 1, here at two thirds, or the weights have to change.

Estimation error is not a rounding detail, and the pricing map makes the error easy to see even before you defend the pricing map. Keep market variance at 0.04. If the covariance you measured is 0.044 instead of 0.048, beta is 0.044 divided by 0.04, which is 1.1. If the covariance is 0.052, beta is 1.3. A move of 0.004 in the covariance, which is a small number next to a variance of 0.04, moved beta by a tenth. Later, with a market excess return of 0.06, a tenth of beta is 0.006 of expected return. Two tenths, from 1.1 to 1.3, is 0.012 of expected return. That is not a digit you drop when you round a price to the nearest percent. It is a different point on the line. The slide's warning is this arithmetic. You do not need a sampling theorem to see that the slope is sensitive to the covariance you fed it. You need the division.

You might ask whether beta, being only a regression slope, should be replaced by correlation in ordinary speech. It should not, not on this board. Correlation removed the two standard deviations and then the definition of beta put a ratio of them back. Assets that share a correlation do not share a beta unless they also share a ratio of volatilities to the market. The 0.80 correlation produced beta 1.2 at volatility 0.30 and beta 0.40 at volatility 0.10. Speech that collapses those to "high correlation" has discarded the slope the projection actually uses. You may still report the correlation. You may not pretend it is the coefficient.

Walk one market outcome through the slope so the projection is a number and not only a ratio. Market variance 0.04, beta 1.2. Suppose the market return comes in 0.05 above its own mean. The linear projection puts the asset 1.2 times 0.05, which is 0.06, above the asset's own mean. The projection does not add 0.05, and it does not add the correlation 0.80. It adds the slope times the market's surprise. If the realized residual that period is negative 0.02, the asset finishes only 0.04 above its mean, and the missing 0.02 is the piece the slope did not claim. Beta did its job on the 0.06. It did not promise the residual would be zero, and a single outcome sitting off the line is not a revised beta. A revised beta is a new covariance over the same or a new market variance. One residual is not that division.

Compute the covariance that belongs to the second asset in the market-weight identity, so beta two thirds is not a free-floating label. Market variance is 0.04. Beta two thirds means the covariance is two thirds times 0.04, which is 0.08 divided by 3, about 0.0267. Beta 1.5 means the covariance is 1.5 times 0.04, which is 0.06. Weight those covariances by the market weights 0.40 and 0.60. You get 0.40 times 0.06 plus 0.60 times about 0.0267, which is 0.024 plus 0.016, which is 0.040. That is the market variance again. The identity is the same statement before you divide and after you divide. If a measured pair of covariances does not weight back to 0.04, the pair is not the covariance of these two assets with a market built from these two weights. You fix the inputs. You do not fix the identity.

Here is what the definition does not mean. It does not mean the asset ought to have beta 1.2, or that 1.2 is aggressive in any moral sense. It means this covariance over this variance equals 1.2. It does not mean the number is stable because the formula is simple. Move the covariance from 0.048 to 0.044 and the slope is 1.1. It does not mean a weighted average of betas equals 1 for an arbitrary set of weights. The weights have to be the market weights, the ones inside the market return you covaried against. A portfolio that uses different weights has a beta equal to its own weighted average, and that average equals 1 only if the portfolio is the market, or if you chose betas that happen to average to 1. The identity is about that construction. It is not a compliment you pay every book.

The board in front of you is titled the pricing statement. If the market portfolio is mean-variance efficient, expected excess returns are linear in beta. In words, the expected return of asset i equals r sub f plus beta sub i times the quantity expected market return minus r sub f. The theorem is an if. The tests argue about the if. You can know the line cold and still not know whether the market portfolio you have in mind is the tangency portfolio. Those are different sentences. The notes tell you to keep the algebra and the empirics apart, and this is the place they come apart.

The line is the security market line. It plots expected return against beta, not against standard deviation. Build it on the same market you already used. Let the risk-free rate be 0.02 and the expected market return be 0.08, so the market excess is 0.06. Beta 0, which is a projection with no covariance to the market, has expected return 0.02. You earn the risk-free rate and nothing for a beta that is not there. Beta one half has expected return 0.02 plus one half times 0.06, which is 0.05. Beta two thirds, the second asset in the market-weight identity, has expected return 0.02 plus two thirds times 0.06, which is 0.06. Beta 1 is the market itself, expected return 0.08. Beta 1.2 has expected return 0.02 plus 1.2 times 0.06, which is 0.02 plus 0.072, which is 0.092. Beta 1.5 has expected return 0.02 plus 0.09, which is 0.11. The points lie on a straight line in the beta direction. They do not lie on a straight line in the standard-deviation direction, and the idiosyncratic piece is why.

Idiosyncratic variance is not priced if it can be diversified. That sentence dies if investors cannot diversify. Take two assets that both have beta 1. The first has no idiosyncratic variance. Its total variance is beta squared times market variance, which is 0.04, so its standard deviation is 0.20, and the line says its expected return is 0.08. The second has idiosyncratic variance 0.05. Its total variance is 0.04 plus 0.05, which is 0.09, so its standard deviation is 0.30, and the line still says 0.08. Same expected return, different total risk. If you plot expected return against standard deviation, these two points share a height and not a horizontal position. They do not draw the security market line. A third asset with beta one half and idiosyncratic variance 0.05 has total variance 0.25 times 0.04 plus 0.05, which is 0.06, standard deviation about 0.245, and expected return 0.05. In the standard-deviation plot it sits left of the noisy beta-1 asset and below it. In the beta plot it sits at one half, on the line, and the noisy beta-1 asset sits at 1, on the same line. The horizontal axis is the whole difference between the two drawings.

The route from tangency to this line is a first-order condition, and the notes ask you to walk it. Take the tangency portfolio as the market M. The first-order condition says expected excess returns are proportional to covariance with M. There is a single constant of proportionality, call it lambda, that does not depend on the asset. Apply the condition to the market itself. The market's excess expected return equals lambda times the market variance. So lambda equals the market excess divided by the market variance. In the numbers, lambda equals 0.06 divided by 0.04, which is 1.5. For the asset with covariance 0.048, the excess expected return is 1.5 times 0.048, which is 0.072. Add the risk-free rate and the expected return is 0.092, which is the same point you got from beta. Divide the proportionality by the market variance instead of multiplying lambda by the covariance, and the covariance over the variance appears by itself. That ratio is beta. Excess expected return equals beta times the market excess. Nothing in that paragraph estimated a regression from a tape of prices. It rewrote the tangency condition in the units of beta. The frontier geometry and this pricing corollary are the same if, said in two vocabularies. Do not fit a stock in the frontier lab and call the fit a test of the corollary.

Change one input, the risk-free rate, and watch the line tilt without any new covariance. Keep the expected market return at 0.08 and move r sub f from 0.02 to 0.03. The market excess falls from 0.06 to 0.05. Lambda falls from 1.5 to 0.05 divided by 0.04, which is 1.25. The asset with beta 1.2 now has expected return 0.03 plus 1.2 times 0.05, which is 0.03 plus 0.06, which is 0.09. It was 0.092. The covariance did not change, and the priced mean did, because the hurdle and the market excess changed. Beta is not a complete description of the expected return. It is the coefficient on an excess you still have to supply. A conversation that quotes 1.2 and does not quote r sub f or the market excess has not stated the pricing sentence.

A second, smaller case keeps the algebra from being a single memorized 1.2. Let the market variance be 0.0225, so the market standard deviation is 0.15. Let the covariance be 0.018. Beta equals 0.018 divided by 0.0225, which is 0.80. Let the risk-free rate be 0.01 and the expected market return be 0.07, so the excess is 0.06. The expected asset return is 0.01 plus 0.80 times 0.06, which is 0.058. Lambda here is 0.06 divided by 0.0225, which is about 2.667, and lambda times the covariance is about 2.667 times 0.018, which is 0.048. Add 0.01 and you get 0.058 again. Move only the risk-free rate to 0.02 and keep the expected market return at 0.07. The excess is now 0.05. The expected asset return is 0.02 plus 0.80 times 0.05, which is 0.060. Same beta, different line, different priced mean. If your notes treat beta as if it were already an expected return, this is the counterexample.

You might ask where a separate forecast of the asset's mean goes. Under the if, it does not go anywhere. The expected return is pinned by the risk-free rate, the market excess, and beta. A forecast that disagrees with 0.092, in the first case, is a disagreement with the if, or a disagreement with the inputs you used to compute beta. It is not an extra term you add because the forecast feels informed. You may reject the if. The tests argue about that. You may not keep the if and also keep an unrelated mean. The sentence is an equality, not a suggestion.

Put the two beta-1 assets on the security market line by their betas and then again by their standard deviations, so the axes cannot be confused in memory. Both have beta 1, so both sit at horizontal position 1 and vertical position 0.08 when the risk-free rate is 0.02 and the market excess is 0.06. One of them has standard deviation 0.20. The other has standard deviation 0.30 because of idiosyncratic variance 0.05. On a standard-deviation chart those are two different x-coordinates, 0.20 and 0.30, with the same expected return 0.08, a flat pair, not a line with slope 0.06. Add the beta one half asset, expected return 0.05, standard deviation about 0.245. In beta space it is halfway from 0 to 1 and its expected return is halfway from 0.02 to 0.08, because halfway in beta is halfway in the priced mean. In standard-deviation space it sits at about 0.245, between 0.20 and 0.30, with a lower mean than both. Halfway in risk measured by sigma is not where it lives, and its mean is not halfway between 0.08 and 0.08. The security market line is the beta chart. If your drawing slopes against sigma, you have changed the theorem.

One more substitution, beta equal to 0, keeps the intercept honest. Covariance 0, market variance 0.04, beta 0. Expected return equals 0.02 plus 0 times 0.06, which is 0.02. Lambda times the covariance is 1.5 times 0, which is 0. There is no excess. If someone adds a "diversification bonus" on top of 0.02 because the asset is uncorrelated, they have left the pricing statement. Uncorrelated, in this model, means unpriced, and unpriced means the risk-free rate, not the risk-free rate plus a compliment. The idiosyncratic variance of that asset can be large. It still does not enter the expected return, and that conclusion still dies if the holder cannot diversify. The formula does not contain a slot for their inability. You have to say the inability in a separate sentence and stop using the line.

Here is what the pricing statement does not mean. It does not mean the market portfolio is efficient. It means if it is, then expected excess returns are linear in beta. CAPM holds when the market portfolio is the tangency portfolio. If your market proxy is not that portfolio, the equality is not a claim about the proxy. The slide names the empirical warning in one line: Roll's critique is that you test a proxy, not the market. Keep that in a different sentence from the algebra. The algebra did not look at a proxy. It looked at M, defined to be the tangency portfolio. It does not mean idiosyncratic risk is irrelevant to an investor who cannot diversify. The sentence that leaves idiosyncratic variance unpriced dies exactly there. An undiversified holder of the noisy beta-1 asset, standard deviation 0.30, does not experience the same risk as the holder of the clean beta-1 asset, standard deviation 0.20, even though the line quotes both at expected return 0.08. The line is a statement about priced expected return in the model where the idiosyncratic piece can be diversified away. It is not a statement that the two holders sleep the same.

The board in front of you is titled what you may not conclude. A low-beta stock is not automatically a buy. CAPM restricts means. It does not rank purchases. Mispricing, if you want that word, is a residual against a model you are willing to defend. Alpha from an in-sample regression is a fitting error until it survives a clock it did not see. The lab does not estimate a beta from prices. It only shows the mean-variance object the model assumes is the market. Do not fit a stock in the lab and call it a CAPM test.

Start with a low beta that the line has priced exactly, so the temptation has nothing to grab. Beta one half, risk-free rate 0.02, market excess 0.06. The line says the expected return is 0.05. Suppose the mean you computed in the same sample is also 0.05. The residual is zero. There is no alpha. A person who calls this a buy because the beta is low is not pointing at a cheap residual. The residual is gone. What they are pointing at is a lower mean, 0.05 against the market's 0.08, which is the model being consistent. Low beta paid less, in expectation, because the slope says it should. Treating that lower mean as a discount sticker reverses the equality. The equality already assigned the mean. It did not leave a bargain underneath.

Now take a residual that is not zero, and refuse to promote it. The beta 1.2 asset is priced at 0.092 on the first line. Suppose the sample mean on the same window is 0.11. The gap is 0.11 minus 0.092, which is 0.018. That 0.018 is the in-sample alpha against this line. It is the fitting error of a comparison that used the covariance, the market variance, the risk-free rate, the market mean, and the asset mean from one clock. It has not survived a clock it did not see. You may write the number down. You may ask whether the model is the one you are willing to defend, because if you will not defend the if, the residual is not mispricing. It is the distance to a line you do not believe. If you will defend the line, the residual is still a same-sample gap until a later window, one that did not choose these inputs, still shows it. The board does not give you that later window. It tells you not to pretend the current gap is already the test.

A second residual, built from the smaller case, keeps the moral from depending on 0.018. Beta 0.80, risk-free rate 0.01, market excess 0.06, priced mean 0.058. Suppose the sample mean is 0.07. The gap is 0.012. Suppose instead the sample mean is 0.04. The gap is negative 0.018. The positive gap and the negative gap are the same kind of object. One of them flatters a purchase and the other flatters a sale, and neither has left the sample that produced the beta. Changing the risk-free rate to 0.02 moved the priced mean from 0.058 to 0.060 and would move both gaps by 0.002. A residual that changes when you retouch an input you already had is not a property of the asset. It is a property of the line you drew.

The market weights identity puts a lid on what an in-sample alpha can mean even before a new clock arrives. Betas weighted by market weights equal 1, so the market's own alpha against itself is zero. In the two-asset picture, weights 0.40 and 0.60, betas 1.5 and two thirds, the priced means are 0.11 and 0.06 if the risk-free rate is 0.02 and the market excess is 0.06. The market mean is 0.40 times 0.11 plus 0.60 times 0.06, which is 0.044 plus 0.036, which is 0.080, exactly the market mean you started with. If your sample means differ from 0.11 and 0.06, the value-weighted gap is a gap against a market mean that those same sample means also define, once the market is the weighted sum. You can move alpha from one asset to the other. You cannot give every asset a positive in-sample alpha against the portfolio that is built from them and then act surprised when the weights refuse to add up. That is part of why a proxy test is not the theorem. If the portfolio in the regression is not the market, Roll's critique is the right sentence: you tested the proxy. If the portfolio is exactly the weighted bundle of the assets you are scoring, a cloud of positive alphas is an arithmetic mistake, not a basket of bargains.

The lab will not save you from that mistake, and it is not trying to. The frontier lab shows the mean-variance object the pricing statement assumes is the market. It does not estimate a beta from prices. A tangency marker on a two-asset bullet is the geometry behind the if. It is not a security market line fitted to a ticker. If you read a weight off that marker and call it a beta, you have renamed a portfolio weight. Beta is a covariance over a variance. A weight becomes a beta only after you have done the projection, or after you have used the identity that market-weighted betas equal 1, which tells you the bundle's beta and not a single stock's bargain. Leave the frontier up while you read the security market line, which is the lab's instruction. Use it as the picture of efficiency. Do not use it as a substitute for the regression the definition named.

You might ask why a positive in-sample alpha is not already a completed decision to buy. Because the alpha was measured on the clock that built the line. The covariance that made beta 1.2, the sample mean 0.11, and the market mean 0.08 are not three independent witnesses. They are numbers from one window. The gap 0.018 is how far that window sits from the equality. A decision to buy, in the sense of a claim that the mean will exceed the line out of sample, needs a clock the fit did not see. This board does not contain that clock. It contains the restriction on means, and the instruction not to promote a fitting error. You can still want the stock for a reason outside the model. You cannot claim that CAPM handed you the buy. CAPM, if you accept the if, handed you 0.092.

Work the low-beta case with a sample mean that sits above the line and one that sits below it, so neither direction gets to be the automatic trade. Beta one half, priced mean 0.05. If the sample mean is 0.07, the in-sample gap is 0.02. If the sample mean is 0.03, the in-sample gap is negative 0.02. The positive gap invites a story about a cheap defensive stock. The negative gap invites a story about a stock that does not even earn its low-beta keep. Both stories use one window. Neither is a buy or a sell on the strength of this board. Change the market excess from 0.06 to 0.04, one input, and keep the risk-free rate at 0.02. The priced mean for beta one half falls from 0.05 to 0.02 plus 0.02, which is 0.04. The old sample mean 0.07 now sits 0.03 above the line, and the old sample mean 0.03 sits 0.01 below it. The gaps moved because the line moved, not because the asset announced a new fact. An order that appears or disappears when you retouch the market excess was never the asset's decision. It was the line's.

Set the in-sample gap next to a change in beta that is only estimation, so a fitting error is not confused with a new purchase. Start from covariance 0.048, beta 1.2, priced mean 0.092, sample mean 0.11, gap 0.018. Now suppose the covariance you trust is 0.052 instead, beta 1.3, because estimation moved it. The priced mean becomes 0.02 plus 1.3 times 0.06, which is 0.098. The gap against the same sample mean 0.11 shrinks from 0.018 to 0.012. Nothing about the sample mean changed. A tenth of beta, which the first board already priced at 0.006 of expected return, ate a third of the alpha. If you would not trade the difference between covariance 0.048 and 0.052 as a fact, you should not trade the 0.018 as a fact either. Both numbers move when the covariance moves, and the covariance is an estimate. The slide said estimation error in beta is not a rounding detail. This is that sentence in the alpha column.

Here is what the refusal does not mean. It does not mean the security market line is an empirical fact. The line is the equality under an if, and the tests argue about the if, including the critique that a test sees a proxy rather than the market. It does not mean every residual is noise in some permanent sense. A residual against a model you will defend, on a clock the fit did not see, is a different object from the 0.018 you computed in sample. The board does not compute that object. It tells you not to counterfeit it. It does not mean low beta is a sell any more than it means low beta is a buy. Beta one half priced at 0.05 is a mean. A mean is not an order. It does not mean the frontier picture is useless once you refuse to fit a stock on it. The picture is the mean-variance object the theorem assumes is the market. Looking at it while you say the line is how you remember that the algebra and the empirics are different sentences. The algebra says: if that object is the market, expected excess returns are linear in beta. The empirics, which this lecture does not pretend to settle, would have to show you that object, not a proxy, and a residual that survives a clock it did not see. Until then you have a restriction on means, a slope called beta, and a list of conclusions you do not get to draw.

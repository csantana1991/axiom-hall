A median is a rank. An approximate quantile structure keeps a compressed picture of ranks. The structures people name GK and t-digest are different compressions of that picture. This studio does not pretend a count-min grid is a quantile sketch. Frequency is not rank. If you remember only that refusal, you will not point the frequency lab at a latency question and call the result a median.

Rank means position in an ordered list. Take nine latency observations, already small enough to sort by hand: four, four, five, five, five, seven, eight, eight, and thirty. Sorted, they occupy ranks one through nine. The middle rank is five. The value sitting at rank five is five. That is the median of this list. A ninety percent quantile, if you take it as the smallest value whose rank reaches ninety percent of nine, needs rank at least eight point one, so you look to rank nine under a ceiling reading, or you declare the convention before you argue. The value at the top of this list is thirty. The median did not see thirty as a special object. The upper quantile did. Either way you are asking a question about order statistics, not about how many times the symbol “five” appeared.

Frequency answers a different question on the same list. The value five appears three times. The value four appears twice. The value thirty appears once. A count-min sketch, given those values as symbols, estimates point frequencies. Queried for five, it aims at three, or at an overestimate of three. Three is not the median. The median is the value at a rank, which happened to be five, and the coincidence that the value and some other count are both the number five is a trap in the example, so look again. The median’s value is five. The frequency of the value five is three. The frequency of the value thirty is one. None of those frequencies is a rank. Rank five means the fifth position. It does not mean the symbol whose name is five, and it does not mean a symbol that occurred five times. The two fives in the previous sentence are doing different jobs, which is why the lecture separates the words.

Change one input. Replace the thirty with a six, and keep the other eight numbers. The sorted list is four, four, five, five, five, six, seven, eight, eight. The middle rank is still five, because you still have nine observations. The value at rank five is still five. The upper tail changed. The median did not. A frequency sketch of the symbols would notice that thirty disappeared and six appeared once. That is a point-frequency change. It is not how you detect whether a quantile moved. In this particular replacement the median’s value stayed put, which tells you the middle rank is insensitive to a single extreme, and it tells you nothing about count-min. Count-min was never looking at the middle rank.

You might ask whether you can recover a median by querying a count-min sketch for every symbol and walking cumulative frequencies until you pass half the stream. On a toy alphabet you can sometimes assemble a histogram that way, and then you are using the audit, not the sketch’s guarantee. Cumulative sums of overestimates are not ranks. If the sketch says five occurred four times when it occurred three, every later cumulative step is shifted, and the rank you think is the middle can land on the wrong value. The one-sided error that is virtuous for a point query is a bias in a cumulative walk. The lecture does not authorize that walk. A count is still not a position in the order. A count-min grid is not a quantile sketch. GK and t-digest exist as names of different rank compressions so you have somewhere else to point, not so you can describe them as count-min with a new label. This studio does not run them. Do not invent their internals to fill the silence.

What the rank claim does not mean is that frequency sketches are useless on a tape of trades, or that quantiles are the only honest summary. Point frequency is the question count-min answers, and the previous lessons sized it. A median is the question you ask when the ordered position matters, as it does for a latency. Using the wrong one is the mistake, not building either one. What it also does not mean is that nine sorted numbers are a streaming algorithm. You stored the list. An unbounded vector of samples is a stream you failed to sketch. Nine is tiny, like the eight roots were tiny, and tiny is allowed when you admit you stored the population. The moment latencies keep arriving, storing every one breaks the contract you already accepted. You then want a compressed picture of ranks, with an epsilon you are willing to tolerate on the rank, or you want a histogram whose edges you chose and whose name is histogram.

Say the epsilon on the rank as a different Greek letter job from the frequency epsilon, even though the alphabet is the same. An epsilon of one tenth on a rank, for a stream of one thousand latencies, is a slack of one hundred positions, because one tenth of one thousand is one hundred. The approximate median is allowed to land within one hundred ranks of the true middle, under whatever definition the structure you picked actually proves. That is not “within one tenth of the latency value.” A rank slack of one hundred can be a small value gap in a dense part of the distribution and a huge value gap in a sparse tail. The guarantee you must hear is about rank until the structure’s theorem says otherwise. Count-min’s epsilon was about an additive frequency error scaled by the length of a count stream. Reusing the word epsilon does not reuse the theorem. Name the question, then the structure, then the epsilon that structure knows how to carry.

On the nine-element list, one tenth of nine is nine tenths of a rank, less than one position. An approximate median with that rank epsilon would have to land very close to rank five, whose value is five. You still would not compute it with count-min. You would either sort, because nine is tiny, or you would use a rank compression whose error is stated on ranks. Sorting nine numbers is an honest exact answer. It does not scale, and it does not become GK by wishing. The lab you have is still the frequency sketch. The brief tells you to use it as a frequency instrument only, and then to write one sentence about what you would store instead if the question were a latency quantile. The sentence is: a compressed rank structure, or a histogram with edges you chose, and not the count-min grid.

The thirty at the end of the original list is the number that makes the value-versus-rank gap obvious. The median value is five. The maximum is thirty. A person who reports “about five” has said something about the middle rank and nothing about the tail. A person who reports the frequency of five has said something about repetitions and nothing about either. A person who wants the tail states a high rank, or states histogram edges that isolate large latencies, and shows those edges. You do not get the tail by taking the count-min estimate of the symbol thirty, which is about one, the frequency, and calling that a quantile. One arrival is a count. Rank nine is a position. Both can be true, and they are not interchangeable.

Space is the design variable. You say the epsilon you can tolerate on the rank, then you pick the structure. A latency histogram with fixed buckets is allowed. You call it a histogram. If the buckets are linear in the tails you care about, the tail buckets are the whole instrument. An unbounded vector of every latency is a stream you failed to sketch. The capstone reports a JavaScript timing, not a quantile sketch of nanoseconds. You do not launder a timing number through a sketch you did not build.

Start by naming the tolerance, because the structure is a consequence. Suppose you will accept a rank error of one tenth on a latency stream, and you expect about one thousand observations. One tenth of one thousand is one hundred ranks. You need a structure whose stated guarantee is on the order of one hundred ranks, or tighter if you want margin. A count-min grid does not become that structure by allocating more columns. Columns buy frequency error. Rank error is a different purchase. If you cannot name a rank epsilon, you are not ready to pick GK or t-digest or anything else, and those two names remain different compressions you have not been shown the internals of. You pick after the tolerance, not before.

A histogram is the honest cheaper object when you are willing to freeze edges. Suppose you choose edges at zero, ten, twenty, forty, eighty, and one hundred sixty, in the same time unit as the latencies you measure. That is five buckets: from zero up to ten, from ten up to twenty, from twenty up to forty, from forty up to eighty, and from eighty up to one hundred sixty. The widths are ten, ten, twenty, forty, and eighty. They widen. You store five counters plus the definition of the edges. Space is five numbers and a short list, independent of how many latencies arrive. A latency of fifteen increments the second bucket. A latency of one hundred increments the last bucket. A latency of two hundred falls outside every edge you chose, and you need a policy for that, stated in advance, because an unnamed overflow is a missing bucket. Fixed bins are honest if you show the bin edges. These are the edges. Anyone can see that the last bucket is eighty units wide and the first is ten.

Now make the buckets linear in the region you claimed to care about, and watch the instrument collapse. Edges every ten units from zero to one hundred: zero, ten, twenty, and so on up to one hundred. That is ten buckets, each of width ten. Space is still small, ten counters. Suppose the tail you care about is everything at or above ninety. That entire tail is one bucket, from ninety to one hundred, and anything beyond one hundred is outside the edges. One counter is then “the whole instrument” for the region you said mattered. You cannot tell ninety-one from ninety-nine. You cannot tell a pile at the edge from a pile at the top. The lecture’s sentence is literal here. Linear buckets in the tail you care about make the tail bucket the instrument. Widening was the earlier design, not a decoration. Equal widths are allowed. They are a choice to spend your resolution uniformly, including in the middle, and to be nearly blind inside the last bin.

Change one input. Keep the equal width of ten, and extend the last edge from one hundred to two hundred, adding ten more buckets. You now have twenty counters covering zero to two hundred. The region at or above ninety is no longer a single bucket. It is eleven buckets if you count from ninety to two hundred in steps of ten. You spent space, ten extra counters, to stop the tail from being one cell. That spend is the design variable moving. If instead you keep ten counters and change the edges to the widening set, you spend the same ten counters differently: coarse in the tail, fine at the left, or whatever your edges actually do. Five widening buckets and twenty equal buckets are different instruments. Neither is a count-min sketch. Both are histograms if you show the edges and call them histograms. The unbounded alternative, a vector that grows with every latency, has space equal to the stream. That is the failure mode the contract already forbids. You chose edges so you would not keep the vector.

You might ask whether the capstone’s timing output is already a quantile sketch, because a timing is a latency. It is not. A timing printed from that JavaScript harness is the run you measured, not a sketch of ranks and not a statement in nanoseconds. A single timing, or a handful of timings printed by a harness in that language, is a report of those runs. It does not compress ranks. It does not carry a rank epsilon. It does not become one by being stored in a growing list. If you keep every timing in a vector, you have the unbounded sample the notes warn you about. If you keep one number, you have one number. Either way, say what you stored. The frequency lab does not fill the gap. Streaming symbols through count-min estimates how often a symbol appeared. A timing is not a symbol in that lab unless you deliberately bin it, and once you bin it you have built the histogram, and you should say so.

What a histogram does not mean is that you have approximated a quantile to a stated rank epsilon. You have counted fixed bins. The bin counts are exact for those edges, assuming you increment the right bin, and they are silent inside a bin. Exact bin counts are not an approximate median. You can define a bin-based summary, for example the smallest edge whose cumulative count reaches half the observations, and that summary’s error is bounded by the bin width, which is a statement about your edges, not about GK. Call it a histogram summary. Do not call it a t-digest. What the widening edges also do not mean is that the tail is solved. An eighty-unit last bucket still conflates every latency inside it. You sized it on purpose. Purpose is not the same as fine resolution. If the tail is the instrument, you may need still more edges out there, and the space cost is the extra counters. You pay it or you admit the bin is coarse.

Take one thousand latencies and drop them into the ten equal buckets of width ten between zero and one hundred, and suppose nine hundred land below ninety and one hundred land in the last bucket. The last bucket’s count is one hundred. Its share of the stream is one hundred divided by one thousand, which is one tenth. You know that one tenth of the mass sits somewhere from ninety to one hundred. You do not know the median of that tenth. The overall median, rank five hundred in a list of one thousand, sits somewhere in the lower buckets, and you can find which bucket by a cumulative walk over exact bin counts. Suppose the cumulative count first exceeds five hundred inside the bucket from forty to fifty. Then you know the median’s bin, and you know the value is between forty and fifty. The uncertainty is the width, ten. That is a clear, modest claim. It is not a rank-epsilon theorem, and it is not a frequency-sketch theorem. It is the histogram doing the job you built it for. Change the last bucket’s count from one hundred to four hundred, one changed input, and the lower nine hundred becomes six hundred. The median rank is still five hundred. Whether it still falls in the forty-to-fifty bucket depends on the lower bins, which you must recompute. The tail got heavier. The median may or may not move. The last bucket still does not tell you the upper quantile’s value inside its range. More mass in a coarse bin is still a coarse bin.

The space comparison is the beat’s point, so say it with the counts. One thousand raw latencies stored as samples: space follows one thousand. Ten histogram counters plus eleven edges: space follows the design, and it stays put when the next thousand arrive, provided you keep the same edges. A rank sketch sized to a rank epsilon of one tenth would have its own space, which you look up when you pick that structure, and which you do not copy from the count-min formula of e over epsilon and a natural log of one over delta. That formula was for point frequency. Reusing it for ranks is how frequency and rank get blurred again. Five widening buckets are even smaller. They are also coarser. Small space is not automatically the right space. It is the right space when the epsilon, or the edges, match the question.

You leave with a choice you can write in one sentence, which is what the lab brief asked for. If the question is a latency quantile, you store a histogram with the edges you are willing to show, or a bounded rank sketch whose epsilon is a rank epsilon, and you say which. You do not store an unbounded vector, and you do not store a count-min grid and pronounce a median. Equal bins of width ten from zero to one hundred make the top bin the entire tail instrument. Widening bins spend the same kind of counter on a different resolution. The capstone’s JavaScript timing is a timing. It is not that sketch, and it is not that histogram, until you build one and show its edges or its rank bound.

Submodular, in one paragraph of mathematics and then a refusal. A set function is submodular when adding an element helps less as the set grows. Greedy gives a one-minus-one-over-e approximation for monotone submodular maximization under a cardinality constraint, under the conditions in the theorem. It is not a synonym for any diminishing-returns story on a trading desk. You do not get to hear “the second feature helped less than the first” and then quote the factor as if a theorem had fired.

The factor is a number you can compute from e, the same base of the natural logarithm you already used to size a width, now used for a different theorem. One divided by e is a little more than thirty-six hundredths and a little less than thirty-seven hundredths. One minus that value is a little more than sixty-three hundredths. Call it sixty-three hundredths when you need a short name, and remember the exact object is one minus one over e, not a rounded trading threshold. If an optimal set of size k has value ten, the greedy set’s value is at least that factor times ten, under the hypotheses. Sixty-three hundredths of ten is a little more than six and three tenths. The guarantee is a floor relative to the optimum. It is not a prediction that greedy will score exactly six and three tenths, and it is not a statement that the optimum is ten. Ten was an illustration of the multiplication.

Change one input. Let the optimal value be twenty instead of ten, and keep the same factor, because the factor does not depend on k or on the optimum. Sixty-three hundredths of twenty is a little more than twelve and six tenths. The floor moved because the optimum moved. The approximation ratio did not. If someone tells you the ratio improves when the cardinality budget grows, they are not stating this theorem. Cardinality k limits how many elements greedy may take, and the stated guarantee against the best set of that same size stays one minus one over e, under the hypotheses. A larger k can raise both the greedy value and the optimal value. It does not, by itself, replace the factor with a tighter one.

Greedy, in the sense the slide uses, keeps the best element each time. Start from the empty set. At each step, among the elements you have not taken, add the one that increases the function the most, and stop when you have k elements. “Best” means the largest immediate increase, not a story about importance. If two elements tie, the theorem’s statement does not care which you break the tie toward, as long as you really are taking a maximizer of the marginal gain. A heuristic that takes the element you like for another reason is not this greedy, and it does not inherit the factor.

A small coverage example makes the marginal gains decrease in public. Let the ground set be four points, labeled one through four. Let the available sets be A containing one and two, B containing one and three, C containing only four, and D containing two, three, and four. The function is the number of points covered by the union. Coverage of this kind is monotone: adding a set never uncovers a point. It is submodular: a new set helps less, or at least not more, once you already cover some of its points. Cardinality k equals two. From the empty collection, D covers three points, A covers two, B covers two, and C covers one. Greedy takes D first. The value is three. Next, A adds the point one, because two is already covered, so the marginal gain is one. B adds the point one as well, marginal gain one. C adds nothing, because four is already covered, marginal gain zero. Greedy takes A or B. The value becomes four. The best set of size two is also four, for example D together with A. Greedy matched the optimum on this instance. The guarantee only promised about sixty-three hundredths of four, which is a little more than two and a half. Matching the optimum is better than the floor. The floor is not a claim that you will do poorly. It is a claim that, when the hypotheses hold, you will not do worse than that factor.

You might ask whether a desk story with diminishing returns is close enough, because the second contract helps less than the first. Closeness is not the hypothesis. The theorem needs a set function, monotone, submodular, maximized under a cardinality constraint, and the other conditions the theorem names. A profit curve that bends is not automatically a set function. A set function that is not monotone can fall when you add an element, and the stated factor does not apply. A constraint that is not a cardinality, a budget on weight rather than on count, is a different theorem or no theorem. The lecture’s refusal is specific. One minus one over e is not a synonym for any diminishing-returns story on a trading desk. If you want the factor, you name the set function, you check monotonicity and submodularity, you name k, and you run the greedy that takes the best marginal element. If you cannot do those steps, you tell the story without the factor.

What the guarantee does not mean is that greedy found the optimum, or that sixty-three hundredths is your score. On the coverage instance the score was four out of four. On another instance the score can sit near the floor without violating the theorem. What it also does not mean is that every greedy heuristic in the building has this proof. The word greedy is cheap. The proof is not. And the factor does not mean a count-min width, even though both formulas contain e. The width formula used e divided by a frequency epsilon. This factor uses one minus one over e, with no epsilon in it. Mixing them produces a nonsense constant. Leave e over epsilon with the frequency sketch. Leave one minus one over e with monotone submodular maximization under cardinality, under the hypotheses.

Check the diminishing-returns definition on the same sets, so submodular is not only a label. Adding C to the empty collection covers one new point. Adding C after D covers zero new points, because D already holds four. One down to zero is helping less as the set grows. Adding A to the empty collection covers two. Adding A after D covers one. Two down to one is the same pattern. A function that helped more after the set grew would violate the definition you were given, and the factor would not be yours to quote. You do not need a second example to remember the direction. Helps less, or at any rate not more. The coverage counts go the right way. A story that goes the wrong way is not rescued by a citation you do not have.

The paragraph stays one paragraph in spirit even after the arithmetic, which is the lecture’s request. You are not building a trading system out of coverage sets in this studio. You are preventing a particular kind of inflation, the kind that hears a diminishing return and emits a constant. The constant is real. The hypotheses are real. The lab you are sitting in front of is still a frequency sketch. Use it as a frequency instrument only. If the question on the table is a latency quantile, the previous beat already gave you the sentence: histogram with edges you show, or a bounded rank sketch, and the name of which one. If the question on the table is which k sets to pick under a monotone submodular coverage function, greedy and the factor are the relevant theorem, and you still have to be inside the hypotheses. Most questions are neither, and then neither slogan applies.

One last numeric refusal, so the desk story dies in arithmetic rather than in tone. Someone says a second signal improved a book by half as much as the first, so the book is submodular and greedy is within sixty-three hundredths of optimal. You have one sentence of marginal gains, no set function, no proof of monotonicity, no cardinality k, and no greedy that selected a best element. Half as much is a ratio of two increments. It is compatible with submodularity and also compatible with a function that later increases its marginal gains. Two increments cannot certify the whole function. They cannot license the factor. If the optimum were ten, the floor would be a little more than six and three tenths only after the hypotheses are met. They are not met by the sentence. You say so, and you go back to the instrument you actually have, which estimates a point frequency from nonnegative updates, or to the histogram whose edges you are willing to write down.

The three beats of this lesson are three refusals to mix tools. A median is a rank, and a count-min grid does not compute it. Space for a latency question is either edges you chose or a rank sketch you named, not an unbounded vector and not a JavaScript timing dressed up as nanosecond quantiles. A submodular guarantee is one minus one over e under a cardinality constraint and the theorem’s hypotheses, not a mood of diminishing returns. Keep the tools in those lanes and the numbers stay checkable. Cross the lanes and the same numbers become costumes.

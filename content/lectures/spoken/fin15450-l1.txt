The idea in this opening block is a process with four properties, and then a stop. A standard Wiener process starts at 0. It has independent increments. It has Gaussian increments whose variance equals the length of the interval. It has continuous paths. Those four sentences are the definition you are allowed to use. Continuity is not differentiability. The path is too rough for ordinary calculus. Adding folklore on top of the four properties is how a later calculation picks up a derivative that is not there.

Start at time 0, with the value 0. That is the first property, and it is an equality, not a distribution. W at 0 is 0. There is no coin flip at the origin of the clock. If a picture starts somewhere else, it is not a standard Wiener process, though it may be a Wiener process plus a constant, which is a different object and should be named as one.

The second and third properties travel together. Take two times s and t with t greater than s. The increment W at t minus W at s is independent of the past up to time s, and it is normal with mean 0 and variance t minus s. The length of the interval is the variance, not the standard deviation. That slip is the one that ruins a simulation quietly. On an interval of length 1, the variance is 1 and the standard deviation is 1. On an interval of length 0.25, the variance is 0.25 and the standard deviation is 0.5. On an interval of length 4, the variance is 4 and the standard deviation is 2. The standard deviation scales with the square root of time. The variance scales with time. When you say "the increment is about the size of the interval," you have said the wrong scaling. The typical size is the square root of the interval.

Put the Gaussian law on a concrete interval. From time 0.25 to time 1, the length is 0.75, so the increment is normal with mean 0 and variance 0.75. The standard deviation is the square root of 0.75, about 0.866. An increment of 0.3 on that interval is a modest draw. An increment of 3 is several standard deviations out, not a draw you should treat as typical when you sketch. From time 2 to time 5 the length is 3, so the variance of the increment is 3 and the standard deviation is the square root of 3, about 1.732. Independence says that this increment does not remember the value of W at time 2, except that it is added to it. The past can set the level. It does not set the distribution of the change.

The variance of the level itself is the increment law with s equal to 0. Because W at 0 is 0, W at t is an increment over an interval of length t, so W at t is normal with mean 0 and variance t. At time 1, W is a standard normal. At time 0.25, W is normal with variance 0.25, standard deviation 0.5. At time 4, W is normal with variance 4, standard deviation 2. The distribution is spreading like the square root of the clock. A path that has the same visual width at time 4 as it had at time 1 is not a sketch of this process.

The covariance follows from the increment law, and you should derive it once so the formula min of s and t is not a charm. Assume s is less than or equal to t. Write W at t as W at s plus the increment from s to t. The increment is independent of the past, hence uncorrelated with W at s, and it has mean 0. The covariance of W at s and W at t is the covariance of W at s with itself, plus a zero, which is the variance of W at s, which is s. If instead t is the smaller time, the same argument gives t. So the covariance of W at s and W at t is the minimum of s and t. Check the arithmetic on numbers. Covariance of W at 0.25 and W at 1 is 0.25. Covariance of W at 2 and W at 5 is 2. Covariance of W at 0.5 with itself is 0.5, which matches the variance. The correlation is not the covariance. Correlation divides by the two standard deviations. At times 0.25 and 1, the standard deviations are 0.5 and 1, so the correlation is 0.25 divided by 0.5, which is 0.5. At times 2 and 5 the standard deviations are the square root of 2 and the square root of 5, about 1.414 and 2.236, and the covariance is 2, so the correlation is 2 divided by about 3.162, about 0.632. The covariance formula is the one on the board. The correlation is a rescaling you can compute when you need it, and it is not a substitute for the minimum.

Independence of increments has a second numerical face. The covariance between the increment from s to t and the level W at s should be zero when s is the earlier time. That covariance is the covariance of W at t and W at s, minus the variance of W at s. With s equal to 0.25 and t equal to 1, that is 0.25 minus 0.25, which is 0. With s equal to 2 and t equal to 5, that is 2 minus 2, which is 0. The increment really does not correlate with where you were. If a simulation produces increments that systematically continue the previous step, the independence property is the one that failed.

The fourth property is continuous paths. A continuous path can be drawn without lifting the pen, in the sense that it does not jump. It does not follow that the pen has a slope. Differentiability would ask for a slope. This process does not have one, as a pathwise fact you should treat as part of the roughness, not as a rare accident on a few unlucky draws. Ordinary calculus is the calculus of functions that are smooth enough for a first-order Taylor expansion to be the whole story. The Wiener path is continuous and still too rough for that story. The next block says what the roughness is, in the only units that survive: squared increments. Until then, the prohibition is enough. Do not differentiate W with respect to t and then feed that derivative to an ordinary chain rule.

You might ask whether the four properties overdetermine the process, or whether continuity is already implied by the Gaussian increments. Keep the list as the lesson states it. The increment law describes the process at a finite set of times. Continuity is a statement about the whole path, including times you did not list. A finite collection of normal random variables with the right covariances matches the process at those times and says nothing, by itself, about whether the path jumps between them. The continuous version is the process in the definition. When you plot, you will be tempted to connect the dots with straight segments. That temptation belongs to the last block. For now, notice that a finite list of increment laws plus a refusal to jump is the whole object. You do not need a velocity to finish the definition. The definition stopped.

What the claim does not mean should be said while the four properties are still the whole lecture. It does not mean the path is a smooth random wave with a well-defined speed. Variance equal to the length of the interval is a statement about increments, not about derivatives. It does not mean every normal process with variance t is a Wiener process. You also need the covariance, or equivalently the independent increments, and you need continuity, and you need the start at 0. A single normal random variable at one time is only a slice. It does not mean the standard deviation equals the time. At time 4 the variance is 4 and the standard deviation is 2. At time 0.25 the variance is 0.25 and the standard deviation is 0.5. It does not mean independent increments are the same sentence as uncorrelated levels. The levels W at s and W at t are correlated, with covariance equal to the minimum. The increments over disjoint intervals are the independent pieces. It does not mean a negative value of W is a violation. The Gaussian is symmetric around 0. At time 1, a value of negative 0.7 is an ordinary draw from a standard normal, one you should be able to accept without inventing a reflecting barrier. This lesson does not add a barrier. Four properties, then stop.

Change one time and recompute the increment, so the variance rule is in your hands and not only in the examples I chose. Take the interval from time 1 to time 1.04. The length is 0.04. The increment is normal with mean 0 and variance 0.04, so the standard deviation is 0.2. A draw of size 0.2 is one standard deviation, not a small deterministic drift. The squared increment of a one-standard-deviation draw is 0.04, which equals the length of the interval. That coincidence, for a single typical squared increment, is the hint the next block turns into a limit. One squared increment is about the length of its own small interval. Add them up, and the sum of the squares wants to be about the length of the whole interval. The path can move by a large total amount, in the sense of adding absolute increments, and still keep a sober sum of squares. Ordinary calculus assumes the sum of squares vanishes when the mesh does. Here it does not. That is the surprise, and it gets its own block.

The idea in this block is quadratic variation, and it is the whole surprise. Take a partition of the time interval. Sum the squares of the Wiener increments over that partition. As the mesh of the partition goes to zero, that sum tends to the length of the interval, not to zero. The convergence is in probability. It is not a claim that every path, on every coarse grid, prints the length exactly. It is the claim that a fine grid stops being a place where the squared increments can be neglected. That is why a second-order Taylor term survives in the limit. Itô’s formula is that surviving term, written later as a calculation rule. This block earns the survival. It does not yet expand a general function.

Write the object in words. On a grid, each increment is delta W. You square it, you add the squares, and you look at the sum. The bracket of W with itself, up to time t, is t. That is the limit statement, compressed into a symbol. The ordinary chain rule drops the term that does not vanish here. If you remember only one contrast from this block, remember that contrast. A smooth path has squared increments that vanish too fast to matter. This path does not.

The expectation of one piece is already the whole mechanism, before any limit. On an interval of length delta t, the increment is normal with mean 0 and variance delta t, so the expected square of the increment is delta t. If you cover time 0 to time t with n increments of length delta t, and n times delta t equals t, the expected sum of squares is n times delta t, which is t. The expectation does not shrink when you refine the grid. Refinement adds more terms, and each term gets smaller, and the product stays at t. That is why the limit is the length and not zero. A student who expects the sum of squares to vanish is importing the estimate from a differentiable path, where an increment is on the order of delta t rather than on the order of the square root of delta t. Square a quantity of order delta t and you get order delta t squared, and n of those is order delta t, which does go to zero. Square a quantity of order square root of delta t and you get order delta t, and n of those is order t, which stays. The Gaussian increment variance is what puts you in the second case.

A single skeleton makes the sum something you can add on a page. Cover time 0 to time 1 with four steps of width 0.25. The square root of 0.25 is 0.5, so each increment is 0.5 times a standard normal. Suppose one draw of those four standard normals is 0.5, negative 1.5, 1, and 0. I am choosing a draw so the arithmetic is visible. I am not claiming a theorem that these four numbers are typical. The increments are 0.25, negative 0.75, 0.5, and 0. The squares are 0.0625, 0.5625, 0.25, and 0. The sum of the squares is 0.875. The length of the interval is 1. One coarse skeleton gave 0.875, which is near 1 and not equal to 1. Near is what a coarse grid owes you. Equality is the limit. The path of partial sums of the increments themselves is 0.25, then negative 0.5, then 0, then 0. You ended at 0, and the quadratic variation was 0.875. Ending near the start does not mean the path sat still, and it does not mean the sum of squares is small. The absolute increments add to 0.25 plus 0.75 plus 0.5 plus 0, which is 1.5, already larger than the net change, which is 0. The path spent variation that the net change refunded.

Change the mesh and recompute one step, so the square’s scale is obvious. Keep a standard normal draw of 1.3, and change only the width. On a width of 0.25 the increment is 0.5 times 1.3, which is 0.65, and the square is 0.4225. On a width of 0.01 the increment is 0.1 times 1.3, which is 0.13, and the square is 0.0169. The square fell almost in proportion to the width: 0.01 over 0.25 is 0.04, and 0.0169 over 0.4225 is 0.04. Each term shrinks like delta t. The number of terms grows like 1 over delta t. The sum is trying to hold still. On the coarse grid a single square, 0.4225, was already a large fraction of the whole interval length 1. On the fine grid a single square, 0.0169, is a small fraction, and you need about a hundred of them to cover one unit of time. A hundred times an expected square of 0.01 is 1 again. The disappearance of each term is not the disappearance of the sum.

The absolute sum does the opposite, and that is infinite variation. The increment is on the order of the square root of delta t, not on the order of delta t. The number of increments on the unit interval is 1 over delta t. The product is on the order of 1 over the square root of delta t, which grows as the mesh shrinks. You do not need a new random draw to see the scaling. If each absolute standard normal factor were about 1, then on a grid of width 0.25 you would add four contributions of size 0.5, total 2. On a grid of width 0.01 you would add one hundred contributions of size 0.1, total 10. On a grid of width 0.0001 you would add ten thousand contributions of size 0.01, total 100. The sum of absolute increments blows up as the mesh goes to zero. The path has infinite variation. It is not of bounded variation on any interval you would care about. Ordinary Stieltjes calculus, the kind that wants finite variation, does not apply. That is the precise sense in which the path is too rough.

Connect the explosion to the derivative that people still try to plot. The ratio of an increment to delta t is an increment of size about square root of delta t, divided by delta t, which is size about 1 over square root of delta t. On a width of 0.01, a one-standard-deviation increment is 0.1, and the ratio is 0.1 over 0.01, which is 10. On a width of 0.0001 the same one-standard-deviation ratio is 0.01 over 0.0001, which is 100. Refining the grid makes the difference quotients larger, not more stable. A derivative would require those quotients to settle. They do not. Estimating a slope from a picture is estimating a quantity that the refinement makes worse. The next block will say this again at the level of the lab plot. The quadratic variation statement is the reason, and it belongs here: the squares add up to the clock, and the slopes do not settle.

You might ask whether convergence in probability allows you to treat the sum of squares as exactly t on the grid you actually have. It does not. The four-step skeleton summed to 0.875, not to 1. A different draw of the same four normals would have summed to something else. The expectation of the sum is 1 on every grid that covers the unit interval, and the expectation being 1 is not the same sentence as the realized sum being 1. As the mesh goes to zero the realized sum concentrates around t. On a desk-sized grid it is a random number with mean t. When a later calculation replaces the sum of squares by t, it is taking that limit, not inspecting your four squares. If you are still on a coarse grid, keep the remainder in mind. The rule you will be taught is a limit rule.

What the claim does not mean is the difference between a rough path and a mysterious one. It does not mean the path is discontinuous. Continuity was one of the four properties, and infinite variation does not cancel it. A path can fail to jump and still fail to have finite length. It does not mean the variance of W at t has been replaced by something else. The variance of the level is still t. Quadratic variation is a path property of the accumulated squares, and for this process its limit coincides with the number that also happens to be the variance. The coincidence is real and the roles are different. Variance is an expectation of a square of the level. Quadratic variation is a sum of squares of increments, in the limit, path by path, in probability. It does not mean every process with variance t has quadratic variation t. You used independent Gaussian increments of variance delta t to get the expected square equal to delta t and to get the concentration. A different process can spread like t and move in a smoother way. It does not mean the ordinary chain rule is slightly inaccurate. On this path the second-order term is of the same order as the first-order term in time. Dropping it does not leave a small error. It leaves the wrong drift. You will see that when the logarithm is expanded. For this block it is enough to refuse the drop. The sum of squared increments tends to t. The sum of absolute increments blows up. The first-order picture of a smooth curve is the wrong picture, and the second-order picture is the one the limit keeps.

One more skeleton, with a different width, so the expectation and the realization stay distinct in a second arithmetic. Cover the unit interval with steps of width 0.04, so there are 25 steps, and suppose you do not list all 25 normals. You can still say the expected sum of squares is 25 times 0.04, which is 1. Suppose two of the standard normals are 2 and negative 0.7 and you only want the contribution of those two, as a fragment. The width is 0.04, whose square root is 0.2. The increment from the 2 is 0.4, and its square is 0.16. The increment from the negative 0.7, if it had been on a width of 1 instead, would have been negative 0.7, square 0.49, but on the width 0.04 it is 0.2 times negative 0.7, which is negative 0.14, square 0.0196. I changed the width and the contribution collapsed. The fragment 0.16 plus 0.0196 is 0.1796, a piece of a sum whose expectation for the full 25 steps is 1. Two large-looking normals did not fill the quadratic variation. The grid did, in expectation, by having 25 terms. When you move to the lab, the fan of paths is a fan of skeletons like these. The roughness is the sum of squares along each skeleton, and that sum is aiming at the clock, not at zero, even on the paths that wander back to where they started.

The idea in this block is that a simulation is not the definition. The lab draws discrete skeletons. A fine time step makes a picture. It does not make the path differentiable. Do not estimate a derivative of W by dividing an increment by the time step, and do not act surprised when that ratio refuses to settle. The samples can be exact. The polyline you draw between them is a convention. Linear interpolation is not a Wiener path.

The exact step on a grid is short enough to memorize. If the grid width is delta t, you add the square root of delta t times a standard normal. In words, delta W equals the square root of delta t, times Z, with Z normal of mean 0 and variance 1. That matches the increment law exactly. The increment you just built is normal with mean 0 and variance delta t, because the variance of a constant times Z is the square of the constant, and the square of the square root of delta t is delta t. Independent draws of Z, one per grid step, match independent increments. Starting the sum at 0 matches the start at 0. What the grid does not deliver is the continuous path between the nodes, and it does not deliver a derivative at the nodes. You have sampled a continuous object exactly at a finite set of times. You have not smoothed it.

Euler’s method, in the narrow sense of adding that Gaussian increment, is not an approximation for the Wiener process itself. It is exact at the grid times. The approximation begins when the drift and the diffusion depend on the state. Then the increment’s law depends on where you are, and a frozen coefficient over a finite step is a genuine discretization error. If the thing you are simulating is W alone, do not apologize for the Gaussian step and do not “improve” it by using a smaller variance. A smaller variance would be the wrong process. If the thing you are simulating is a diffusion with state-dependent coefficients, the same Gaussian step becomes the noise input to an approximation, and shrinking the step is then a real numerical question. The lab’s skeletons of geometric Brownian motion are built from exact log-increments, which is the state-dependent case handled by an exact transformation rather than by a crude Euler step on the level. You will derive that transformation when the logarithm is expanded. For this block, the Wiener step itself is the exact one.

Build one step numerically and then refuse the slope. Let delta t equal 0.01. The square root is 0.1. Suppose Z equals 1.2. Then delta W equals 0.12. The squared increment is 0.0144, close to the width 0.01 and not a puzzle. The illegal slope is 0.12 divided by 0.01, which is 12. Suppose the next Z is negative 0.8. The next increment is negative 0.08. The next illegal slope is negative 8. The two quotients, 12 and negative 8, do not look like a derivative sampled from a continuous slope. They look like noise divided by a small width, which is what they are. Change only the width, to 0.0001, and keep a one-standard-deviation Z. The increment is 0.01 and the quotient is 100. The picture got finer and the estimated derivative got wilder. That is the scaling from the previous block, now as a plotting habit you have to unlearn. A fine dt makes the skeleton look more like a continuous path, because the missing gaps are smaller. It does not make the difference quotients converge. If the lab invites you to zoom in, zoom in on the continuity, not on a slope.

The polyline is the other plotting habit. Between two samples you often draw a straight segment, because a graphics library needs a segment. The segment is a drawing convention. Along that segment the path is linear in time, hence differentiable on the open segment, hence of finite variation, hence of quadratic variation zero along the interior of the segment. The only quadratic variation in the drawing sits at the sample times, in the jumps of slope, and even that is an artifact of the polyline rather than a feature of the Wiener path. Linear interpolation is not a Wiener path. It agrees with one at the nodes you sampled, if your nodes used the right variance, and it is a different function between them. If you feed the polyline to an ordinary calculus routine, you are differentiating the convention. The routine will return an answer. The answer belongs to the polyline.

Say the same point with the four-step skeleton from the previous block, so the picture has coordinates. Width 0.25, increments 0.25, negative 0.75, 0.5, and 0, partial levels 0.25, negative 0.5, 0, and 0. A polyline connects (0, 0) to (0.25, 0.25) to (0.5, negative 0.5) to (0.75, 0) to (1, 0). On the first segment the slope is 0.25 over 0.25, which is 1. On the second segment the slope is negative 0.75 over 0.25, which is negative 3. On the third, 0.5 over 0.25, which is 2. On the fourth, 0. Those four slopes are properties of the drawing. The Wiener increment on each quarter is a single normal random variable, not a constant velocity. If you refined each quarter into a hundred exact Gaussian steps, the polyline inside that quarter would stop being one straight segment, the sum of absolute increments inside the quarter would jump up, and the sum of squares inside the quarter would concentrate around 0.25. The coarse polyline hid the roughness inside each segment. Refinement reveals it. It does not create it. The definition already had it.

The lab instruction about the fan is the practical version of this block. You set the drift and the volatility aside for a moment and look at the fan of paths. They are discrete geometric Brownian motion skeletons, built from exact log-increments. You resample. The paths change, because they are skeletons of a random process. The call price on the right must not flicker. A flicker means the number on the right was tied to the particular random draw, which is the wrong dependence. This lesson does not derive that price. It tells you how to read the picture while the price sits still. The fan is a sample of paths. The price is not the average of a handful of polylines, and it is not a functional of the slopes you think you see. If resampling the fan moves the price, the bug is in the wiring, not in the Wiener process. If resampling the fan moves the fan, the generator is doing what the increment law asked.

You might ask how fine the grid must be before the skeleton can stand in for the path in a demonstration. For the Wiener process itself, at the grid times, any grid is exact, including a coarse one. Fineness is about the picture and about any quantity that depends on the path between the nodes or on a state-dependent coefficient you froze. Quadratic variation on a coarse grid is a random sum with the right expectation and a noticeable spread. On the four-step draw it was 0.875 against a mean of 1. A finer grid pulls a typical realization toward the mean. If the demonstration is “the sum of squares is the clock,” use a fine grid or show several coarse grids and their average. If the demonstration is “the increment over a stated interval is exactly normal with variance equal to the length,” a single step of that length is already a complete demonstration, and refining it would only be theater. Match the grid to the claim. Do not refine out of nervousness, and do not coarsen a state-dependent scheme and call it exact.

What the claim does not mean is a license to ignore discretization everywhere, or a ban on plotting. It does not mean numerical schemes never have error. It means the Wiener increment, by itself, is not the source of that error. The error starts when coefficients that should vary inside the step are held fixed, or when a quantity you compute from the polyline uses the segments as if they were the path. It does not mean you should add a random slope in order to be honest. There is no slope to add. The honest plot is a set of exact samples, possibly connected by a convention you do not then differentiate. It does not mean a continuous path is a path you can zoom into until it looks straight. Zooming multiplies the apparent slope by the reciprocal of the square root of the width. Straightness never arrives. It does not mean two different grids that share an endpoint have the same joint behavior for the wrong reason. If you want W at time 1, one step of variance 1 and one hundred steps of variance 0.01 give the same marginal distribution at time 1, because the sum of independent mean-zero normals is normal with variance equal to the sum of the variances, and one hundred times 0.01 is 1. They do not give the same polyline, and they do not give the same sum of absolute increments. Marginal exactness at one time is a weaker statement than pathwise agreement.

Change one input, the standard normal draw, and recompute a single exact step so the square-root rule cannot hide. Keep delta t equal to 0.01, and replace Z equals 1.2 by Z equals negative 0.3. The increment is 0.1 times negative 0.3, which is negative 0.03. The squared increment is 0.0009, much smaller than the width 0.01, which is allowed on a single step. The expected square is 0.01. A small draw produces a small square. The illegal slope is negative 0.03 over 0.01, which is negative 3, still an order of magnitude larger than a derivative you might have hoped would be near 0, and still a different number from the previous draw’s slope of 12. Two draws, two quotients, no settled derivative. The exactness sits in the distribution: over many independent steps of width 0.01, the average of the squared increments should sit near 0.01, and the average of the increments themselves should sit near 0. If your generator matches those two averages and each increment is constructed as the square root of delta t times an independent standard normal, you have implemented the increment law. You have not implemented a derivative. Leave the derivative out of the sentence.

The four properties, the quadratic variation, and the exact grid step are one object seen three ways. The process starts at 0, adds independent normal increments with variance equal to elapsed time, and does so continuously and without a slope. The sum of squared increments tracks the clock. The sum of absolute increments does not stay bounded as you refine. The way you simulate the clock on a grid is the square root of the width times a standard normal, exact for W, and only an input to an approximation when the coefficients begin to depend on the state. The picture is allowed. The slope read off the picture is not.

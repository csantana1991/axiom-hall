The unit of movement is the line. When you ask the machine for a small field, the hardware does not fetch that field by itself. It moves a cache line. On the machines you will benchmark, that line is commonly sixty-four bytes. A load of one int pulls the neighbors that happen to sit in those sixty-four bytes. If your struct is twelve bytes, you did not save a trip. You invited the neighbors along.

Hear that as a statement about the transfer, not about the type you wrote. The type records the width of the field you named. The memory system records the width of the line. Those widths disagree, and the line is the width that moves.

Start with the twelve-byte struct, because that number is the one this lesson gives you. Ask how many such structs fit in one line if you pack them with no extra padding. Five times twelve is sixty. Sixty-four minus sixty is four. So five structs occupy sixty bytes, and four bytes of the line are left over. Those four bytes are not a sixth struct. They are remainder. The remainder is not the lesson. The lesson is that a request for one field inside one of those structs still moves the whole line, including pieces of the neighboring structs that share it. You did not pay twelve bytes of traffic. You paid a line of sixty-four.

Change one input. Let the struct be sixteen bytes instead of twelve, and keep the line at sixty-four. Sixty-four divided by sixteen is four, exactly, because four times sixteen is sixty-four. Four records fill the line with nothing left over. A load that names the first record still moves four records. The arithmetic got cleaner, and the claim did not change. Shrinking a record from sixteen bytes to twelve does not turn one line into a smaller transfer. It only changes how the neighbors sit inside the line you were going to move.

You might ask whether a careful load of a single byte can be arranged so the machine fetches only that byte. On this account of the hardware, no. What moves is still the whole line, not the single byte you thought you requested. The address you named selects which line. It does not set the size of the transfer. A smaller field can still be worth writing, because more useful fields may then share the line you were going to fetch anyway. It is not a way to purchase a smaller trip. If you needed a reason to stop tuning field widths in isolation, that is the reason.

What the sixty-four-byte claim does not mean is that every machine you will touch uses a line of that width. Sixty-four is common. It is not a law of nature. Before you publish a result that depends on the width, confirm it in the architecture manual for the chip you actually ran. What it also does not mean is that two cores are already in conflict merely because a line has a width. The conflict called false sharing is two cores writing the same line. A single core reading a line is a different event. Two cores that only read are a different event again. Do not promote a width into a race.

The lab turns the same distinction into a charge you can watch, and you should hear the charge as a model. The cache lab is not a processor. It charges one when two writers sit on different lines, and eight when they share one line. The eight is a teaching penalty so the toggle does something visible. Quote it as a model. Do not quote it as cycles from a chip.

Take the two charges and divide. Eight divided by one is eight, so the shared case costs eight times the split case. Suppose you apply that charge once for each paired update, and you run one thousand paired updates. The split case costs one thousand model units. The shared case costs eight thousand. The difference is seven thousand, and none of that difference is extra work in your algorithm. It is the model saying that the writers sat on one line. Change the teaching penalty from eight to four, one changed input, and leave the one thousand updates alone. The shared case now costs four thousand, the split case still costs one thousand, and the gap is three thousand instead of seven thousand. The mechanism is the same. Only the constant moved. If you change the update count instead, from one thousand to two hundred, the split case costs two hundred and the shared case, still at eight, costs one thousand six hundred. Again the ratio is eight. The lab brief says the same thing in one sentence you can check: a shared line costs eight times two private lines. The notes say the charge is one when the writers sit apart and eight when they share. Those two sentences are the same model.

On metal, the experiment that matches the picture is two threads incrementing adjacent counters, with padding and without it, timed with a proper harness. The course numbered 6.172 assigns versions of that experiment. That is a pointer to an assignment, not a number you may paste into a report as if you had measured it. You still time your own counters.

Put the twelve-byte packing next to the charge. Five twelve-byte structs fill sixty bytes of a sixty-four-byte line. If two cores each write a different one of those five, your source shows two objects, and the model charges eight, because the line is one. Distinct types are not distinct lines. Lines are addresses. Two addresses share a line when they fall in the same sixty-four-byte span, not when their field names differ.

Consider the leftover four bytes as their own small case, so the remainder does not stay abstract. Five times twelve is sixty, and sixty-four minus sixty is four. Those four bytes might be the start of an object that continues on the next line, or padding the compiler inserted, or unused space. You cannot tell which from the number twelve alone. You can tell that the trip was still sixty-four bytes. If you need the neighbors, read the addresses. Guessing them from the struct size is how the leftover four bytes become a surprise later.

Nothing in the width says you should pad every struct out to sixty-four bytes. Padding is a later decision, and it spends capacity. A line that could have carried several useful records then carries fewer of them, and you have moved padding you may not have needed. The claim in this opening is only about what moves. Which layout deserves to move is the next question, and it depends on the inner loop, not on a picture of a struct.

Say the transfer rule in the language of a single load, without adding a width for the int that the lesson does not give you. You load one int. The line that contains it moves. Whatever else occupies the rest of those sixty-four bytes moves with it, including the rest of a twelve-byte struct and whatever of the next struct still fits. The int selects a place. The line is the parcel. Students sometimes reverse that and treat the line as a suggestion. Do not. The parcel is the fact. The named field is how you asked.

One further arithmetic check, so the sixteen-byte variant and the twelve-byte case stay distinct in your notes. Under the twelve-byte packing, the fifth struct ends at byte sixty, counting from zero as a start. Under the sixteen-byte packing, the fourth struct ends at byte sixty-four, which is exactly the end of the line. In the first packing, a sixth struct cannot start on this line. In the second, a fifth struct cannot start on this line either. Both packings still cost one line for a touch anywhere inside them. If your loop touches only the first object and then jumps far away, you still paid the line, and the other objects in it were passengers.

The model’s eight is not a prediction of how many times slower the metal experiment will be. It is a teaching penalty, chosen so that sharing and not sharing look different on the board. A real pair of threads can land anywhere relative to that eight, including close to it and including nowhere near it, once the harness, the counters, and the chip are real. You quote one and eight only with the sentence that they are the lab’s model. The architecture manual remains the place you confirm the line width. The harness remains the place you time the counters.

So the idea you should be able to say without a slide is short. Hardware moves lines. A common width is sixty-four bytes, and it is not a law. A twelve-byte struct does not shrink the move. Changing that struct to sixteen bytes still does not shrink the move. The lab charges one or eight according to whether two writers share a line, and that pair of charges is a model. False sharing, when you meet it, means two cores writing the same line, not merely two cores existing.

Walk one more packing so the remainder arithmetic is something you can redo under a third size, with only one input changed from the twelve-byte case. Keep the line at sixty-four and let the struct be twenty-four bytes. Two times twenty-four is forty-eight. Sixty-four minus forty-eight is sixteen. Two structs fit, and sixteen bytes remain. Three structs would be seventy-two, and seventy-two is larger than sixty-four, so the third struct cannot start on this line without crossing into the next one. A load of either of the first two still moves the whole line. The passengers are the other twenty-four-byte struct, or part of it, plus the sixteen leftover bytes. Compare that with the twelve-byte packing, where five structs fit and four bytes remain, and with the sixteen-byte packing, where four structs fill the line exactly. Three different object sizes, one transfer size. That is the whole point of naming the line as the unit.

You might also ask how the teaching charge should be quoted if someone in the room treats eight as a measured slowdown. You quote it with the lab’s own limit attached. The notes tell you the cache lab is not a processor, that one is the charge for writers on different lines, and that eight is the charge when they share. Take the one thousand updates once more, but split them into the two writers so the model is not only a single combined charge. The model does not give you a separate invoice per writer. It gives you one when the two writers sit on different lines, and eight when they share one. You do not add a private one for each writer and then multiply. The notes are explicit: the charge is one in the split case and eight in the shared case. A last boundary on the claim. Moving a line of sixty-four bytes does not tell you the latency of that move, and it does not tell you whether the line was already resident. The lesson’s number is a width. The lab’s numbers are comparative charges inside a toy. Residency, miss penalty, and the coherence traffic that follows a write are later material. You can leave this beat able to compute how many twelve-byte structs meet inside sixty-four bytes, able to recompute that count after you change twelve to sixteen or to twenty-four, and able to refuse both a law-of-nature reading of sixty-four and a cycle-accurate reading of eight.

The layout question is array of structs against struct of arrays. An array of structs is the layout you write first. A struct of arrays is the layout a tight loop often wants, because the next useful field is the next address. The right layout is the one the inner loop streams. You measure that loop. You do not measure the picture.

Say the two pictures in the order the slide gives them. Array of structs reads x, y, z, then x, y, z, then x, y, z. The fields of one record sit together, and the next record follows. Struct of arrays reads x, x, x, then a run of y, then a run of z. One field occupies a contiguous stretch, and the other fields live elsewhere. Neither picture is a moral preference. Each matches a different touch pattern. When you touch a whole record, the grouped layout matches the touch. When you stream one field, the separated layout matches the touch. A market event that you use as a whole record can stay in the grouped layout. You do not owe anyone a conversion.

Take eight records, each a twelve-byte struct, and ask what a scan costs in lines if you walk them as an array of structs. Eight times twelve is ninety-six. Ninety-six divided by sixty-four is one full line, with thirty-two bytes left, because sixty-four plus thirty-two is ninety-six. Those eight records occupy two lines: the first line holds as many as fit, and the rest spills. From the earlier packing, five times twelve is sixty, so the first line holds five records and four leftover bytes, and the remaining three records are three times twelve, which is thirty-six bytes, on the next line. A loop that reads every field of every record streams both lines, and the passengers are fields you intended to read. That is the grouped layout doing the job it is good at.

Now suppose the inner loop wants only one field out of the three that make the twelve-byte record, and you left the records grouped. The next copy of that field is not the next address. The other fields of the same record, and then the other fields of the next record, sit between the two copies you care about. To visit eight copies of the one field, you still walk the same ninety-six bytes, because those copies are scattered through the array of structs. You move two lines to collect eight fields. Whether that is waste depends on whether the other bytes were useful. If they were not, the grouped layout made the useful field a sparse subset of a dense trip.

Change one input. Keep the twelve-byte record and the sixty-four-byte line, and change the record count from eight to four. Four times twelve is forty-eight. Forty-eight is less than sixty-four, and sixty-four minus forty-eight is sixteen, so four grouped records fit on a single line with sixteen bytes spare. A loop that touches every field of those four records moves one line, and the line is mostly payload. A loop that wanted only one field still moves that same line under the grouped layout, because the field does not have its own run of addresses. The trip got smaller because the working set got smaller, not because the layout learned which field you meant. If you then switch those four records to a struct of arrays, the useful field occupies its own contiguous run. The lesson does not give you a separate width for that one field, so you do not invent a byte count for the run. You do say what the layout changed: successive useful values became successive addresses, which is the reason a tight loop often wants the separated form.

You might ask whether every array should be rewritten as a struct of arrays before you measure. No. The layout you write first is allowed to be the grouped one, and for a market event consumed as a whole record it can stay that way. Rewriting first, and measuring never, inverts the instruction. The instruction is to name the inner loop, see whether it streams one field or a whole record, and measure that loop rather than the picture of x, y, z against x, x, x. A picture can tell you which layout is a candidate. It cannot tell you the time.

What the preference for struct of arrays does not mean is that the separated layout is faster on every loop, or that a grouped market event is a mistake. It means the separated layout puts the next useful field at the next address when you stream one field. If your loop touches the whole record, the grouped layout already does that for the record as a unit. What it also does not mean is that a drawing of the two layouts is a profile. Padding you cannot see in a profiler is still padding, and a layout you have not timed is still a hypothesis. The slide’s advice is to measure the loop. Treat a confident sentence about speed, spoken from the picture alone, as a sentence you have not earned.

Work the spill again with the record count chosen so the line boundary falls in a place you can point at. Five twelve-byte records fill sixty bytes. A sixth begins at offset sixty and needs twelve bytes, but only four bytes remain in the line, so the sixth record crosses. An array of structs does not magically align your records to lines. It only groups fields. If a record crosses a line, a touch of that record can move two lines even though you named one object. That crossing is ordinary address arithmetic, not a failure of the grouped idea. You notice it by computing sizes and offsets, and you confirm it by reading the addresses, the same way you refuse to assume a width you have not checked.

Consider a loop that streams one field across a long run, and keep the claim qualitative where the lesson gives you no per-field width. In the separated layout the next value of that field is adjacent. Hardware that moves a line then brings several upcoming values of the same field, which is what you wanted. In the grouped layout the same line brings a mixture, which is what you wanted only when the mixture is the record. The phrase to keep is the one from the notes of the lecture: the right layout is the one the inner loop streams. “Often wants” is doing real work in the sentence about struct of arrays. Often is not always. A market event used as a whole record is the lecture’s own example of a grouped layout that can stay.

A second numeric change, distinct from cutting eight records down to four. Keep eight records and change the record size from twelve bytes to sixteen, the same one-input change you used when you were only counting passengers. Eight times sixteen is one hundred twenty-eight. One hundred twenty-eight divided by sixty-four is two, exactly. The grouped array fills two lines with no remainder. Five would not be the packing count anymore, because sixteen does not go into sixty-four five times. Four times sixteen is sixty-four, so each line holds four records, and two lines hold eight. If the loop reads every field, both lines are intended traffic. If the loop reads one field only, both lines are still the traffic of the grouped layout, and the separated layout would have given that field a private run whose length you would then measure instead of guessing. Same eight records, different record size, different bytes, same decision rule.

You might also ask whether a struct of arrays removes false sharing by itself. It does not. False sharing is two cores writing the same line. Separating fields into different arrays can place two hot counters far apart, or it can place them adjacent at the starts of two arrays that the linker lays next to each other. The layout changes addresses. It does not pronounce a verdict until you know whether those addresses share a line. That verdict is the next beat’s subject, under alignment, and it is why a picture of x, x, x is not yet a promise about cores.

Hold the lab’s model beside the layout choice without pretending the model knows which layout you picked. The model charges one or eight according to whether two writers share a line. A grouped layout makes sharing more likely when two cores write different fields of nearby records, because those fields are interleaved inside the same lines. A separated layout makes sharing less likely when the two hot fields live in runs that fall on different lines, and it does nothing for you when the runs still share. The charge is still a teaching penalty, not a chip. You use it to see the mechanism. You use a harness when you need a time.

What you should not conclude is that the twelve-byte market event must be split because ninety-six bytes became two lines. Two lines for eight whole records is a small working set, and if you consume the record whole, those two lines are the stream you asked for. The lecture allows that event to stay grouped. Split it when the inner loop streams one field and a measurement says the mixture hurts. Leave it when the loop wants the record. The algebra of ninety-six and sixty-four tells you how many lines the grouped walk touches. It does not tell you to rewrite the type tonight.

Say the decision in one breath you could give a classmate. You write an array of structs first. You look at the inner loop. If it touches the whole record, you may keep the grouped form, and a market event is the example already on the slide. If it streams one field, a struct of arrays puts the next useful value at the next address, which is why a tight loop often wants it. Time that loop before you trust the layout. You do not argue from the picture. And you do not treat either layout as a cure for two cores writing one line until you have looked at the addresses.

Alignment is a promise about addresses. When you write alignas of sixty-four, you are asking that the address of the object be a multiple of sixty-four. That promise does not, by itself, separate two fields that still live in the same object. For the next element of an array to start on a fresh line, sizeof must also be a multiple of the alignment. For an aligned struct, the language makes sizeof a multiple of the alignment. You still read sizeof. You do not assume.

Check the promise on two addresses before you talk about fields. One hundred twenty-eight divided by sixty-four is two, exactly, so one hundred twenty-eight is a multiple of sixty-four and could be the address of an object aligned that way. One hundred is not. Sixty-four times one is sixty-four, and sixty-four times two is one hundred twenty-eight, and one hundred sits strictly between them. An object at one hundred fails the promise even if every field inside it is a type you like. Alignment is a fact about the starting address. It is not a fact about the spelling of the struct.

Now put two fields inside one object and watch the promise fail to split them. Suppose the object itself begins at a multiple of sixty-four, and the two fields sit at offsets zero and eight within it. Both offsets are inside the first sixty-four bytes, because eight is less than sixty-four. Aligning the object moved both fields together. It did not give them different lines. Head and tail need their own lines or they false-share, and they do not get those lines from a single alignas painted on a parent that still holds both. The slide states the requirement directly: alignment of the object, and sizeof a multiple of that alignment, are what push the next element onto a fresh line. Two members of one small object are not two elements in that sense.

Use the language rule as a numeric contract you can audit. If the alignment is sixty-four, sizeof of the aligned struct is a multiple of sixty-four. The smallest such positive multiple you should expect to see, when the payload is smaller than the alignment, is sixty-four itself. Then an array of two such objects places the second at a start address sixty-four bytes after the first. If the first begins at one hundred twenty-eight, the second begins at one hundred ninety-two, because one hundred twenty-eight plus sixty-four is one hundred ninety-two. Both starts are multiples of sixty-four. Each object opens a line. That is the separation head and tail need when each of them is its own aligned object, not when they are adjacent members.

Change one input. Ask for alignment to thirty-two instead of sixty-four, and keep the line width at sixty-four, which is still the width you were told is common and not a law. A start address of zero is a multiple of thirty-two. A start address of thirty-two is also a multiple of thirty-two, because thirty-two times one is thirty-two. Those two objects satisfy the weaker alignment and still share a sixty-four-byte line, because both sit inside the span from zero to sixty-three. The next line would begin at sixty-four. Thirty-two is too small a stride to guarantee a fresh line of sixty-four. This is why the lecture names sixty-four, matching the line, and not a looser number that merely sounds aligned. If you change the stride back, an object at zero and an object at sixty-four do not share that line. The difference between thirty-two and sixty-four is the whole difference between a promise the line respects and a promise it can ignore.

You might ask whether, once the language guarantees that sizeof is a multiple of the alignment, you may skip reading sizeof in the build. You may not. The notes and the slide agree on the habit: read sizeof, do not assume. The guarantee is why a correct compiler will give you a multiple. The read is how you notice that you aligned the wrong object, or that a field you thought was its own object is still a member, or that a change in the type dropped the alignment while a comment stayed behind. A comment is not the promise. The address and the size are the promise. Check them in the build anyway, even though the language rule is on your side for a truly aligned struct.

What alignas of sixty-four does not mean is that two hot fields have been separated. It means the object you attached it to starts at a multiple of sixty-four. Fields inside that object still share its storage. What it also does not mean is that the padding required to make sizeof a multiple will show up as a hot line in a profile. Padding you cannot see in a profiler is still padding. It still occupies bytes, and it still moves when the line moves. Invisibility in one tool is not absence. What it also does not mean is that aligning every object to sixty-four is free. Each padded object consumes a whole multiple of the line even when the payload was a small counter. You pay that cost because head and tail otherwise false-share, not because alignment is a decoration you sprinkle on types you have not measured.

Compute the waste so the cost of the promise is a number. A counter that lives in an aligned object of sizeof sixty-four occupies a line by itself. Two such counters, head and tail, occupy two lines. Without the separation they might have shared one line, and the lab’s model would charge eight instead of one for the pair of writers. With the separation the model charges one. The difference in the model is seven model units per paired update, using the same one-versus-eight tariff as before. Over one thousand paired updates the model says one thousand versus eight thousand. You are buying that difference, in the model, by spending a second line on the second counter. On a chip the ratio may not be eight. The layout fact remains: two lines, two starting multiples of sixty-four, two writers who no longer write the same line.

Try the sizeof audit on a size that is wrong, so you know what failure looks like before the compiler saves you. Suppose someone claims the struct is aligned to sixty-four and then reports sizeof forty. Forty is not a multiple of sixty-four. An array stride of forty would place the second element forty bytes after the first. If the first is at zero, the second is at forty, which is inside the first line. The language is not supposed to do this for an aligned struct, which is why a reported forty means you are looking at the wrong type, or the alignment did not apply, or the number was copied from memory. That is the practical content of “do not assume.” You read the size the build actually produced.

The same audit tells you when the next element is safe. Sizeof sixty-four, one hundred twenty-eight, or one hundred ninety-two are all multiples of sixty-four, since they are one, two, and three times the alignment. A stride of one hundred twenty-eight bytes means the next object starts two lines later, with a whole line of padding between them. That still separates the lines. It also spends an extra line you may not need. The lecture does not ask you to pad more than the rule requires. It asks you to meet the rule, which the language already enforces for the aligned struct, and to confirm the size rather than remember it.

You might ask a second question, about whether alignment repairs a bad inner-loop layout from the previous beat. It does not. Alignment moves and pads objects. It does not decide whether an array of structs or a struct of arrays matches the stream. You can align a grouped market event and still be right to keep it grouped, because you consume the whole record. You can separate fields into arrays and still false-share if the two arrays’ hot regions land on one line. The beats compose. The line is the unit that moves. The loop chooses which bytes ought to be neighbors. The alignment promise chooses starting addresses and, through sizeof, the stride to the next element. None of the three replaces the other.

Name the idea before you name a speed. This is a handoff, not a marketplace. The capstone moves a market event from a producer to a consumer without a lock and without allocating. It is not an exchange, a matching engine, or a broker. Scope is how this stays finishable. If you add a venue, you have left the practicum and started a different project, and you will not finish either one by describing them as the same object.

Say what crosses the handoff. One producer thread produces events. One consumer thread takes them. The capacity is fixed before the first event, and it stays fixed. The event is trivially copyable, which means the language can copy the bytes of the value without running a custom constructor in the middle of the transfer. You do not allocate after startup. The slots exist. The producer writes into a slot it is allowed to write. The consumer reads a slot it is allowed to read. Nothing in that sentence discovers a price, matches two orders, or routes anything to a venue. The event may describe a market. The ring does not become the market.

The finish line follows from that narrowness. You can draw one cell, one ticket, and one mask, and a classmate can push back on the drawing. You can run a checksum on a known sequence of order identifiers and get an equality you can recompute by hand. You can say, without a new subsystem, whether the producer was permitted to write. A matching engine has none of those properties at the scale of a semester. It has order types, priority, partial fills, and a notion of a trade. None of those are in the handoff. Leaving them out is not a missing feature you apologize for. It is the decision that makes the defense a defense of an engine rather than a tour of a venue you did not build.

Work the checksum identity on a sequence short enough to add in your head, so the later hundred-thousand case is not a magic formula. The default build pushes order identifiers zero through N minus one, and the consumer sums them. The sum of the integers from zero through N minus one is N times the quantity N minus one, divided by two. For N equal to four, the identifiers are zero, one, two, and three. The sum is six. The formula gives four times three, divided by two, which is twelve divided by two, which is six. For N equal to five, the identifiers add to ten, and five times four divided by two is ten. The equality is the test. If the consumer's sum is six when N is four, every identifier arrived once. If the sum is five, something was dropped or torn or double-counted, and you stop. You do not time a ring that has already failed this arithmetic.

Scale the same identity to the default run, which is a threaded checksum of one hundred thousand events. N is one hundred thousand. The sum is one hundred thousand times ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine, divided by two. Divide the hundred thousand by two first, which gives fifty thousand, then multiply by ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine. Fifty thousand times one hundred thousand is five billion. Fifty thousand times one is fifty thousand, so fifty thousand times ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine is five billion minus fifty thousand, which is four billion nine hundred ninety-nine million nine hundred fifty thousand. That is the number the consumer must produce. It is not an approximation and it is not a tolerance. A torn or dropped event fails the equality. If the checksum mismatches, you stop. You do not interpret a timing of a broken ring.

There are two builds, and they answer two different questions. The default build is that threaded checksum of one hundred thousand events. You compile with g plus plus, optimization level three, the C plus plus twenty standard, and pthread, producing an executable named spsc test from the file spsc ring buffer dot cpp, and then you run it. The command is a check of the sum, not a latency distribution. The second build adds the define AXIOM WITH BENCHMARK and links the benchmark library. It runs two threads, one pushing and one popping. A push-only loop is a bug, not a benchmark. You will spend a later hour on why that loop times a livelock. In this hour it is enough to refuse the phrase "the benchmark" for a loop that never consumes.

Compute one intermediate N so the hundred-thousand figure is not the only value your hand remembers. For N equal to one thousand, the identifiers are zero through nine hundred ninety-nine. The sum is one thousand times nine hundred ninety-nine, divided by two. One thousand divided by two is five hundred, and five hundred times nine hundred ninety-nine is five hundred times one thousand minus five hundred, which is five hundred thousand minus five hundred, which is four hundred ninety-nine thousand five hundred. You can check a short prefix of that run by hand. Identifiers zero through nine sum to forty-five, and ten times nine divided by two is forty-five. If a consumer reports forty-four on that prefix, the equality has already failed and the remaining nine hundred ninety identifiers cannot repair the story you tell about the program. You stop. The same rule scales without a change in meaning from ten events, to one thousand, to one hundred thousand. Only the integer gets longer.

The event those identifiers ride in has a shape you should be able to say, because "trivially copyable" is not a mood. A reasonable event for this handoff carries an integer order identifier, a side, a size, and a price in integer ticks. You freeze that layout before the timed run, and you do not allocate a string for the symbol on every push. The identifier is what the checksum adds. The other fields are along for the copy. If the layout is twelve bytes or sixty-four bytes or something larger, the protocol does not change, and the time, if you ever measure it, does. Event size belongs on the same line as the flags when a clock exists. Until a clock exists, the layout still belongs in the talk, because a handoff of an unspecified blob is not yet a handoff of a market event. It is a handoff of a slot. Say what the bytes mean, and say that the ring does not interpret them. Interpretation would be a strategy, or a matcher, and both of those are outside the producer-consumer contract.

The question from the room is whether a clean handoff is secretly a matching engine you can grow later by adding a book. It is not. A book remembers resting interest and decides who trades with whom. This ring forgets an event once the consumer has taken the bytes, except insofar as the consumer, which is your code on the other side, chooses to remember them. The ring's job ends at the handoff. If your consumer is a strategy, the strategy is not the ring. If your consumer is a logger, the log is not a venue. Scope is how the work stays finishable. Growing a matcher inside the slot turns the trivially copyable event into a protocol of cancels and replaces, and you will no longer be able to say, in one whiteboard drawing, what the ticket means. You can build a matcher some other time. You cannot smuggle it in and still claim you are testing this handoff.

What the claim does not mean is that the event is forbidden to look like a market event. An order identifier, a side, a price in integer ticks, a size: those can live in a trivially copyable struct, and the checksum can still add the identifiers. The marketplace prohibition is about behavior, not about vocabulary. The ring does not match, does not advertise, and does not credit an account. It copies bytes from one thread to another under a protocol, with no lock and with no allocation after startup. The claim also does not mean that one producer and one consumer is a temporary simplification you may violate with a comment. The listing is specialized to one writer by convention. A second producer is a different protocol. You will see that again when the compare-exchange is on the table. Here, the point of saying it early is that the scope is a contract. One producer, one consumer, fixed capacity, trivially copyable event, no allocation after startup. If a sentence in your defense requires a second writer, the sentence is about a system you did not build.

The second idea is that the targets are hypotheses. More than one million events a second, and a handoff under two hundred fifty nanoseconds, are reasonable ambitions on pinned metal with a warm cache. They are not properties of this browser. The lab measures the protocol in JavaScript and says so. The C plus plus file is what you time. Browser timings are JavaScript timings. Nanoseconds come from a counter on the machine, or not at all. When you do have a machine number, you state the CPU, the compiler flags, and the core pin. If you do not have them, you do not have the number.

Turn the ambitions into arithmetic so they cannot hide inside a prefix. One second contains one billion nanoseconds. If you hand off one million events in that second, and you pretend the handoffs share the second evenly, each handoff occupies one thousand nanoseconds. One thousand is four times two hundred fifty. So a handoff of two hundred fifty nanoseconds is a stricter pace than one million events a second, if the handoff were the only work in the second. At two hundred fifty nanoseconds each, one billion divided by two hundred fifty is four million events in a second. The two ambitions are not the same sentence. "More than one million events a second" allows a thousand nanoseconds per event. "Under two hundred fifty nanoseconds" asks for something at least four times tighter, and it asks for it as a handoff latency rather than as a throughput slogan. Both are described as reasonable ambitions on pinned metal with a warm cache. Neither is a measurement sitting in the repository waiting for you to copy it onto a slide.

Apply the same arithmetic to the checksum's one hundred thousand events, still as arithmetic and not as a timing you ran. At one thousand nanoseconds each, one hundred thousand events occupy one hundred thousand times one thousand nanoseconds, which is one hundred million nanoseconds, which is one tenth of a second. At two hundred fifty nanoseconds each, they occupy one quarter of that, twenty-five million nanoseconds, which is twenty-five milliseconds. Those products are what the ambitions would imply if the ring were already at the target and nothing else were on the path. They are not what the JavaScript lab measured, and they are not what you may write under a chart unless a counter on a stated CPU produced them. The usefulness of the product is comparative. A tenth of a second and twenty-five milliseconds are long enough to notice and short enough to drown inside a slow strategy, which is the next idea. They are not a result.

The lab is a different instrument, and the lecture wants you to say the difference in the same breath as any number you show. The lab runs the protocol in JavaScript. A rate it prints is a rate of that JavaScript loop. It can teach you that a full ring drops, and it can teach you the order of the tickets. It cannot teach you that the handoff was two hundred fifty nanoseconds, because it is not the C plus plus binary on pinned metal, and it is not a warm cache on a core you named. If you show the browser number, you label it as a browser number. Nanoseconds come from a counter on the machine, or not at all. "Or not at all" is the option you will actually use until you have the counter. Leaving the number off the slide is a completed part of the assignment, not a gap you paper over with the ambition.

When you do time the C plus plus file, the report has three companions or it does not have a latency. The CPU, because the ambition was about pinned metal and a warm cache, and a different chip is a different hypothesis. The compiler flags, because optimization level three and the C plus plus twenty standard and pthread are part of the default build, and a debug build is a different program. The core pin, because a thread that wanders is not the pinned case the ambition named. If any of the three is missing, you can still say you ran the checksum. You cannot say you tested the hypothesis. The benchmark binary, when you link it, adds the define and the benchmark library. It does not add a CPU. You still write the CPU down. And if the checksum mismatches, you do not get to skip ahead to that binary and hope the timer averages the bug into something smooth.

Put the two ambitions next to a capacity so throughput and latency stop sharing a sentence. Suppose the capacity is sixteen, which is the ring you will step in the protocol hour, and suppose you could fill it at the loose ambition of one thousand nanoseconds per event. Sixteen events would take sixteen thousand nanoseconds, which is sixteen microseconds, if every push were a successful handoff at that pace and nothing stalled. At two hundred fifty nanoseconds, the same sixteen successful handoffs would take four thousand nanoseconds, four microseconds. A full ring of sixteen is a small object. The ambition is not "sixteen is fast." The ambition is a sustained pace after the consumer has been keeping up for millions of events, on metal, with a warm cache, pinned. Sixteen times two hundred fifty is only the arithmetic of one fill. One million divided by sixteen is sixty-two thousand five hundred fills. At four microseconds a fill, sixty-two thousand five hundred fills take sixty-two thousand five hundred times four microseconds. Sixty-two thousand five hundred times four is two hundred fifty thousand microseconds, which is a quarter of a second. That quarter-second is what one million events would occupy at a steady two hundred fifty nanoseconds, and it matches the earlier result that two hundred fifty nanoseconds is four million events per second, because a quarter of four million is one million. The arithmetic is consistent. It is still not a measurement. It tells you that a sixteen-slot ring is not "too small to be a million events." The consumer has to recycle each slot tens of thousands of times. The ticket publish of tail plus capacity is what makes that recycling legal. A ring that works once, for sixteen events, and then sticks, does not have a throughput hypothesis at all.

A browser cannot host this arithmetic by renaming units. If the JavaScript lab reports a rate, you may divide one billion nanoseconds by that rate and obtain a quotient. The quotient answers "how long did this loop take per operation in this browser," and only if the rate was operations per second of that loop. It does not answer "how long does the C plus plus handoff take on pinned metal." Write the question next to the quotient if you compute it, so the unit does not outrun the instrument.

The question to answer is whether a fast-looking number in the browser can be translated into nanoseconds by a unit conversion. It cannot. A conversion multiplies a measurement by a factor. It does not change which instrument took the measurement. If the lab says the JavaScript loop performed some number of operations in a second, that is a JavaScript sentence. Dividing one billion nanoseconds by that count produces a quotient in nanoseconds, and the quotient inherits the instrument. You may compute it as a way to feel the size of the JavaScript rate. You may not relabel the quotient as the handoff on metal. The C plus plus file is what you time. Until you time it, the honest sentence is that you have a hypothesis of more than one million events a second and of a sub-two-hundred-fifty-nanosecond handoff on pinned metal with a warm cache, and that you have not run that hypothesis.

What the claim does not mean is that the hypotheses are silly, or that they are guarantees. They are called reasonable ambitions under conditions you can state: pinned metal, a warm cache, a handoff that is actually the path you timed. They are not properties of the source code in the abstract. A correct ring can be slow on a busy machine, in a cold cache, with a huge event, under a compiler you did not name. A fast number can come from a push-only loop that is livelocked and therefore not a handoff at all. Reasonable does not mean observed. Observed does not mean portable to a laptop browser. You keep the two ambitions as targets you might earn, and you keep the checksum as the thing you can earn this week without inventing a clock.

The third idea is the budget you owe to the separation between the ring and the strategy, the budget associated with AC two fifteen. If a model sits on the path, the ring's nanoseconds are a rounding error. Either the path is parse, decide, and send, with a tiny decide, or you report the ring in isolation. Write that sentence on the first slide of the defense. A huge strategy mean means the nanosecond target is about a different path. The lab's budget figures are teaching numbers. Make the strategy stage honest before you open the ring.

Put the rounding error in the same units as the hypothesis, so "rounding" is a ratio and not a gesture. The handoff ambition under discussion is two hundred fifty nanoseconds. Suppose, as a supposition for the ratio and not as a measured strategy, that the decide stage takes fifty microseconds. One microsecond is one thousand nanoseconds, so fifty microseconds is fifty thousand nanoseconds. Fifty thousand divided by two hundred fifty is two hundred. The decide stage, in this supposition, is two hundred times the entire handoff ambition. Add them and the sum is fifty thousand two hundred fifty nanoseconds. The handoff is two hundred fifty parts in that sum, which is under half of one percent. If your slide quotes two hundred fifty nanoseconds for the whole path, you have hidden two hundred parts of the path inside a number that describes one part. That is the rounding error. It does not require the supposition to be your real strategy. It requires you to notice that a decide stage measured in tens of microseconds makes a nanosecond ring the wrong headline.

Now suppose the decide stage is actually tiny, which is the other legal sentence. Let the decide be fifty nanoseconds, again a supposition so the addition is visible. Two hundred fifty nanoseconds of handoff next to fifty nanoseconds of decide is five times the decide. The handoff dominates that particular neighbor. In that world, a ring number is a large piece of the mechanical story, and you still have to measure parse and send rather than guess them. The lecture's instruction is to choose. Either you are in the world where decide is tiny, and the path is parse, decide, and send, or you report the ring in isolation and you do not let the audience add it to a model and call the sum a trading latency. You do not get a third option where a large model is on the path and the slide still says two hundred fifty nanoseconds.

The first slide of the defense is where that choice has to be visible, because everything after it will be heard as a latency claim whether you intend that or not. Write one of the two sentences in plain language. One version: the path under test is parse, decide, and send, and the decide is this small measured thing, or you do not yet have the measurement and you say so. The other version: the number, if you have one, is the ring alone, and the strategy is not inside it. A chart of the ring with a caption that says "engine latency" will be heard as the first version even if you meant the second. The caption is part of the claim. Teaching numbers in the lab are there so you can see a strategy mean that is large relative to a nanosecond and recognize that the target has changed paths. They are not a measurement of your model, and they are not a number you copy into the defense as if the lab were the machine.

Tie the budget back to the checksum before you talk about any timer. The default main pushes identifiers zero through N minus one and the consumer sums them. For one hundred thousand events the sum is four billion nine hundred ninety-nine million nine hundred fifty thousand. If that equality fails, the budget discussion is theater. You would be explaining the latency of a handoff that does not hand off. Run the checksum first. Then ask whether a model sits on the path. Then, and only if you still want a clock, time the C plus plus file under flags, a CPU, and a pin you are willing to write down. The order is the discipline. A beautiful budget slide above a broken sum is a broken defense.

Do the ratio at a second supposition so fifty microseconds is not the only scale you can see. Let the decide stage take one millisecond. One millisecond is one million nanoseconds. One million divided by two hundred fifty is four thousand. The decide, in this supposition, is four thousand times the handoff ambition. The sum of the two is one million plus two hundred fifty nanoseconds, and the handoff is two hundred fifty parts of that sum, about one part in four thousand. Calling the path a two-hundred-fifty-nanosecond path would be wrong by a factor of roughly four thousand, not by a rounding you could fix with a footnote. A millisecond model is a different path. You report it as a millisecond model, or you take it off the path and report the ring alone. The fifty-microsecond supposition was two hundred times, and it was already enough to demote the ring. The millisecond supposition is the same sentence with a harsher ratio, so you do not attach the conclusion to one convenient constant.

A tiny decide has a ratio you should also be able to flip. Fifty nanoseconds of decide against two hundred fifty nanoseconds of handoff means the handoff is five times the decide. If parse and send are unknown, you still do not add invented values for them. You say the decide is a small neighbor of the handoff only if you measured the decide, and otherwise you say you have not measured it. Five times is the arithmetic of the supposition. It becomes a slide only when the fifty is real. Until then the first slide carries the choice of sentence, not the number five.

The question to answer is where a model is allowed to sit if the ring is the capstone. It is allowed to sit off the path you are timing, or it is allowed to sit on the path if the decide is tiny and you say so. It is not allowed to sit on the path, take milliseconds, and then vanish from the sentence that quotes the ring. The ring moves an event from a producer to a consumer. If the consumer is a model, the consumer's runtime is the consumer's runtime. You can report it. You cannot report it as nanoseconds you did not measure, and you cannot report the ring's hypothetical nanoseconds as if they included it. Isolation means the model is a different line on the slide, possibly a line that says you have not measured it. A missing line is more honest than a line that hides the model inside the handoff.

What the claim does not mean is that a slow strategy is a failed capstone. The capstone is the handoff, the checksum, and a budget that tells the truth about whether the strategy is on the path. A huge strategy mean means the nanosecond target is about a different path. It does not mean you have failed the ring. It means you stop advertising the ring's ambition as the latency of the strategy. The claim also does not mean that parse, decide, and send is a slogan you can satisfy by typing those three words above a network diagram. Tiny is a comparison. Fifty nanoseconds beside two hundred fifty is a different story from fifty thousand beside two hundred fifty. If you have not measured the decide, you do not get to call it tiny. You write the sentence in the form that admits the missing measurement, and you leave the nanosecond box for a later hour, when a machine has earned it.

When you open the lab for this hour, you are looking at the engine's budget, with teaching numbers, not at a leaderboard. Make the strategy stage honest before you open the ring. If the strategy mean is huge, say that the two-hundred-fifty-nanosecond ambition is about a different path, and say whether you are reporting the ring in isolation. That is the first slide. The rest of the practicum is a handoff you can still explain after the slide has told the truth.

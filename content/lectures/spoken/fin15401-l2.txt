The board in front of you is titled a risk-free asset collapses the frontier to a ray. Mixing a risky portfolio with cash, or with borrowing at the same rate, traces a straight line from the point zero risk and mean equal to the risk-free rate, through that risky portfolio. Every mix on the line shares one risky recipe. Only the scale changes. The best risky portfolio is the one where this line is steepest. That slope is the Sharpe ratio.

Speak the ratio the way the slide writes it. S of w equals the quantity w transpose mu minus r sub f, divided by the square root of w transpose capital sigma w. The numerator is expected excess return over the risk-free rate. The denominator is standard deviation, not variance. The line is drawn in the plane whose horizontal axis is standard deviation. Slope is rise in expected return over run in standard deviation. If you divided by variance you would be scoring a different picture, and it would not be the slope of the ray you just drew.

Take a worked pair and score three risky portfolios before you optimize anything, so the slope is a number and not a compliment. Let the means be 0.08 and 0.14. Let the standard deviations be 0.20 and 0.25. Let the correlation be 0.20, so the covariance is 0.20 times 0.20 times 0.25, which is 0.01. The variances are 0.04 and 0.0625. Let the risk-free rate be 0.03. Asset 1 alone has excess return 0.08 minus 0.03, which is 0.05, and standard deviation 0.20, so its Sharpe ratio is 0.05 divided by 0.20, which is 0.25. Asset 2 alone has excess return 0.11 and standard deviation 0.25, so its Sharpe ratio is 0.11 divided by 0.25, which is 0.44. An even split has mean 0.11, excess 0.08, and variance one half squared times 0.04 plus one half squared times 0.0625 plus 2 times one half times one half times 0.01, which is 0.01 plus 0.015625 plus 0.005, which is 0.030625. The standard deviation is 0.175. The Sharpe ratio is 0.08 divided by 0.175, which is about 0.457. The even split already beats both single assets on this slope. Beating them does not make it the tangency portfolio. It makes it a candidate the ray can pass through, and a better candidate may sit nearby.

The capital market line, once you have the best risky portfolio, starts at the risk-free point and passes through tangency. Points on the segment between cash and tangency are long the fund and long cash. Points beyond tangency, above the risky bullet in the region of higher mean and higher standard deviation, require borrowing at r sub f. Say if that rate is a fiction. If you cannot borrow at the same 0.03 you used in the formula, the part of the ray beyond the tangency portfolio is a drawing of a market you do not have. The segment between cash and tangency can still describe lending at 0.03. The two sides of the point are not the same institutional fact, and the formula does not notice the difference unless you say it.

Leverage moves you along the ray. It does not change the risky mix. Suppose the tangency portfolio, which we will derive on the next board and which I will use here as a completed object, puts weight 27 over 79 on the first asset and 52 over 79 on the second. To four decimal places those weights are 0.3418 and 0.6582. Its mean is about 0.1195. Its standard deviation is about 0.1904. Its excess over 0.03 is about 0.0895, and its Sharpe ratio is about 0.470. Now put half the capital in that mix and half in the risk-free asset. The mean becomes one half of 0.1195 plus one half of 0.03, about 0.0747. The standard deviation becomes one half of 0.1904, about 0.0952. The excess return halves, the standard deviation halves, and the ratio stays about 0.470. Borrow instead. Put 1.5 times capital in the tangency mix and finance the extra half at 0.03, which is a weight of negative 0.5 on the risk-free asset. The mean is 1.5 times about 0.1195 minus 0.5 times 0.03, about 0.1642. The standard deviation is 1.5 times about 0.1904, about 0.2856. The Sharpe ratio is again about 0.470. You did not find a better risky recipe by borrowing. You slid up the same ray. If the borrowing rate is not really 0.03, this upper point is the fiction the slide told you to name.

Compare the ray with a worse fund at the same standard deviation, so steepness is not abstract. Take standard deviation 0.10 on the tangency ray. The scale y that hits that risk is 0.10 divided by about 0.1904, about 0.525. The mean is 0.03 plus 0.525 times about 0.0895, about 0.077. The same standard deviation built from asset 1 and cash uses scale 0.10 divided by 0.20, which is one half. The mean is 0.03 plus one half times 0.05, which is 0.055. Same risk, mean 0.077 against mean 0.055, because the slope 0.470 beats the slope 0.25. That gap is the whole content of choosing the tangency fund before you choose leverage.

You might ask why the denominator is standard deviation rather than variance, since the frontier you already know was built out of a variance objective. Because the picture changed axes when the risk-free asset arrived. Variance is the quadratic you minimize to trace the risky bullet. The ray is a straight line in mean and standard deviation. Its slope divides by the horizontal unit you actually plotted. A ratio that used variance would rank portfolios differently and would not equal the geometric slope of the line from the risk-free point. On the numbers, asset 2 has variance 0.0625 and excess 0.11, so excess over variance is 1.76, while its Sharpe ratio is 0.44. Asset 1 has excess over variance equal to 0.05 divided by 0.04, which is 1.25, and Sharpe ratio 0.25. The order happens to agree here. It does not have to be the definition. The definition on this board is excess over standard deviation.

Write four points on the tangency ray so the collapse to a line is a table you can recompute. Use the fund with mean about 0.1195, standard deviation about 0.1904, and risk-free rate 0.03. At scale y equal to 0 you are in cash: mean 0.03, standard deviation 0, and there is no Sharpe ratio at that single point because the denominator is zero and the excess is zero. The ray begins there. It does not have a slope at a lonely origin until a second point exists. At y equal to one half, mean about 0.0747, standard deviation about 0.0952, slope about 0.470. At y equal to 1 you are fully in the fund: mean about 0.1195, standard deviation about 0.1904, slope about 0.470. At y equal to 1.5, mean about 0.1642, standard deviation about 0.2856, slope still about 0.470. Plot mean against standard deviation and the four points sit on one line. The risky bullet, the curve of mixes that do not use cash, bends underneath that line except at the tangency point, where the line touches. That touch is why the fund is called tangency. A line through the even split would be less steep, slope about 0.457, and it would not be the supporting line. It would cut into the region the steeper line already reaches at lower risk.

Here is what the ray does not mean. It does not mean every point on it is available in the market you trade. Borrowing at r sub f is an assumption you have to say out loud, and if the rate is a fiction the points beyond tangency are a fiction. It does not mean a high slope is a statement about drawdowns, gaps, or a client who cannot survive a path. The denominator is standard deviation in the two-moment model, nothing else. It does not mean the risky mix changes when you add cash. Half cash and half tangency kept the weights 27 over 79 and 52 over 79 inside the risky half. Cash sat beside them. It does not mean the even split's Sharpe ratio of about 0.457 was a failure. It was a lower slope than about 0.470. On a flat board those can look close. The geometry still picks the steeper line, and the next board shows where that line touches.

The board in front of you is titled one fund, if you believe the inputs. Every mean-variance investor who agrees on mu, capital sigma, and r sub f holds the same tangency portfolio and differs only in leverage. That is the two-fund separation theorem. One fund is the tangency portfolio of risky assets. The other fund is the risk-free asset. Disagreement about mu is the whole industry. If your mean vector is not mine, separation across the two of us is already gone, even when we share a covariance matrix and a risk-free rate.

The closed form is the proportionality you should derive once and then check. The risky weights are proportional to capital sigma inverse times the quantity mu minus r sub f times the vector of ones. You then normalize so the risky weights sum to 1. For two assets the lab can also evaluate the Sharpe ratio on a fine grid of w, including modest shorts. For two assets the grid is the method, not an approximation you need to apologize for. The closed form is the check against that grid when capital sigma is well conditioned.

Do the inverse in the worked pair, with correlation 0.20, so the matrix is comfortable. Capital sigma is the matrix with 0.04 and 0.01 on the first row and 0.01 and 0.0625 on the second. The determinant is 0.04 times 0.0625 minus 0.01 squared, which is 0.0025 minus 0.0001, which is 0.0024. The inverse has diagonal entries 0.0625 divided by 0.0024 and 0.04 divided by 0.0024, which are about 26.0417 and 16.6667, and off-diagonal entries negative 0.01 divided by 0.0024, about negative 4.1667. With r sub f equal to 0.03 the excess means are 0.05 and 0.11. Multiply the inverse by that excess vector. The first raw weight is about 26.0417 times 0.05 plus negative 4.1667 times 0.11, which is 1.30208 minus 0.45833, which is 0.84375. The second raw weight is about negative 4.1667 times 0.05 plus 16.6667 times 0.11, which is negative 0.20833 plus 1.83333, which is 1.625. The raw weights sum to 2.46875. Divide. The normalized weight on the first asset is 0.84375 divided by 2.46875, which is 27 over 79, about 0.3418. The weight on the second is 1.625 divided by 2.46875, which is 52 over 79, about 0.6582. Those are the holdings inside the risky fund. They are not yet a statement about how much of your wealth sits in the fund.

Score it. The mean is 27 over 79 times 0.08 plus 52 over 79 times 0.14, which to four decimals is 0.1195. The variance is about 0.03625, and the standard deviation is about 0.1904. Excess over 0.03 is about 0.0895. Sharpe ratio about 0.4700. Set that next to the three candidates from the previous board. Asset 1 scored 0.25. Asset 2 scored 0.44. The even split scored about 0.457. The tangency fund scores about 0.470. A grid with steps of one hundredth in the weight, including a modest range of shorts, finds its best slope at a weight near 0.34, with a Sharpe ratio that matches 0.470 to the digits you can hear. The grid and the inverse are the same answer on a well conditioned two-asset matrix. You do not apologize for the grid. You use it to see the closed form land.

Separation is the scale, and risk aversion chooses the scale, including a short of the fund if someone wants the ray on the other side of cash. Take the tangency excess of about 0.0895 and the tangency variance of about 0.03625. An investor who maximizes mean minus a times one half variance, with all risk coming from a scale y on this single fund, chooses y equal to the excess divided by a times the variance. At a equal to 4, y is about 0.0895 divided by 0.145, about 0.617. That investor holds about 62 percent in the tangency fund and the rest in cash. The risky weights inside the fund stay 27 over 79 and 52 over 79. At a equal to 2, y is about 1.234. That investor borrows. About 123 percent of capital goes into the same fund, and the extra is financed at 0.03, if that rate is real. At a equal to 8, y is about 0.309. More cash, same fund. Three aversions, one risky recipe. That is the theorem, and the hypothesis is sitting in the first sentence: they agree on mu, on capital sigma, and on r sub f.

Change mu and the fund moves. Keep everything else, and lower the second mean from 0.14 to 0.10. The excess vector becomes 0.05 and 0.07. The same inverse produces raw weights about 1.010 and 0.958, which normalize to about 0.513 and 0.487. The tangency fund is now nearly an even split, and its Sharpe ratio falls to about 0.343. Raise the second mean instead, from 0.14 to 0.20. The normalized weights become about 0.184 and 0.816, and the Sharpe ratio rises to about 0.690. One input, the second mean, moved the risky mix from about 66 percent in asset 2, to about 49 percent, to about 82 percent. Investors who disagree about that mean do not hold one fund. They hold three different funds in this calculation. Disagreement about mu is not a footnote to separation. It is the reason two desks do not share the recipe. The theorem never promised they would, once the means differ.

Change the risk-free rate instead of the mean, so you can see a second input with a cleaner story. At r sub f equal to 0.05, with the original means 0.08 and 0.14, the excesses are 0.03 and 0.09. Raw weights come out 0.40625 and 1.375, which normalize to 13 over 57 and 44 over 57, about 0.228 and 0.772. The fund tilts harder toward the higher-mean asset because the hurdle rose and the first asset's excess shrank more, in relative terms, against that hurdle. The Sharpe ratio, now measured over 0.05, is about 0.369. Same covariance, different cash rate, different tangency. Separation still holds inside a room that agrees on the new rate. It does not hold between a room using 0.03 and a room using 0.05.

Constraints break separation. A long-only rule, a leverage cap, a ban on borrowing at r sub f, or a ban on shorting the fund, cuts the ray. Two investors with different risk aversion can then be pressed against different constraints, and the risky mix itself can change. You are back to a quadratic program. The closed form proportionality assumed the only restriction on risky weights was that they sum to 1 after scaling, with cash or borrowing absorbing the rest. Take the a equal to 2 investor who wanted y about 1.23. If borrowing is forbidden, that investor cannot sit at 1.23. The optimum under the cap is not "the same fund, smaller scale" in every constrained problem. Sometimes the boundary wants a different blend of the two risky assets than the unconstrained tangency. Do not quote separation in a mandate that has already broken it.

Recompute the tangency mean from the weights without rounding the weights first, so the four-decimal mean is not a rumor. Weight 27 over 79 times 0.08 is 2.16 divided by 79. Weight 52 over 79 times 0.14 is 7.28 divided by 79. The sum is 9.44 divided by 79, which is 0.11949367 before you round. Subtract 0.03 and the excess is 0.08949367. The variance that pairs with these weights is about 0.0362506, whose square root is about 0.1903959. Divide the excess by that standard deviation and you get about 0.47004. When I say about 0.470, I am rounding that quotient, not substituting a different portfolio. The grid point at weight 0.34, with the other weight 0.66, scores a slope that matches to three decimals because 0.34 is already within a hundredth of 27 over 79. Move the grid point on purpose to weight 0.50 and you fall back to about 0.457, which is the even split you already computed from the quadratic. The surface of the Sharpe ratio near the maximum is flat enough that a hundredth of weight does not embarrass the method, and it is not so flat that an even split is the answer.

You might ask why your risk aversion does not show up inside the stock mix, since you feel more cautious than the person who borrows. Under identical beliefs it shows up in the scale and only in the scale. Caution chooses y. It does not choose a new tangency. The moment your mu differs, or your r sub f differs, or your constraint set differs, the stock mix can differ, and the calculation with the second mean lowered to 0.10 already showed a mix that moved from 52 over 79 to about one half. Aversion was not the input that moved it. The mean was.

Translate the scale into balance-sheet weights, because "the same fund" is easy to say and easy to misread on a blotter. Take y about 0.617, the a equal to 4 investor, and the fund weights about 0.3418 and 0.6582. Asset 1 receives 0.617 times about 0.3418, about 0.211 of capital. Asset 2 receives 0.617 times about 0.6582, about 0.406 of capital. Cash receives 1 minus 0.617, about 0.383. The risky slice, 0.211 and 0.406, is still in the ratio 27 to 52. Now take y about 1.234. Asset 1 receives about 1.234 times 0.3418, about 0.422. Asset 2 receives about 1.234 times 0.6582, about 0.812. Cash receives 1 minus 1.234, about negative 0.234, which is the borrowing. The risky slice 0.422 and 0.812 is the same ratio, scaled past 1. If a report lists only asset 1 and asset 2 and renormalizes them, both investors look identical. That renormalization throws away separation. The theorem's prediction is visible only when cash, including a negative cash balance, is still on the page.

Here is what one fund does not mean. It does not mean every investor holds the market, and it does not mean the tangency portfolio is unique in the world. It is unique for a shared mu, a shared capital sigma, and a shared r sub f, up to the normalization, and only when the matrix is well enough behaved to invert. It does not mean leverage is a detail you can ignore when you report holdings. The person with y about 0.617 and the person with y about 1.234 hold the same risky weights and very different balances of cash and borrowing. Reporting only 27 over 79 and 52 over 79 hides the scale separation was invented to isolate. It does not mean a grid is a sloppy substitute for the inverse. On two assets the grid is a legitimate evaluation of the Sharpe ratio, and here it agreed. It does not survive a constraint. If the mandate forbids the short or the borrowing the formula used, you have a different problem, and the sentence to say is that you are back to a quadratic program.

The board in front of you is titled do not divide by a tiny sigma. Sharpe ratios explode when a mix has almost no variance. Near rho equal to negative 1 the scanner can crown a numerical ghost. The lab refuses non-finite slopes and shows the weights it actually scored. Your job on this board is to recognize the ghost before a large slope becomes a story.

Keep a pair that can actually cancel. Standard deviations 0.10 and 0.30, means 0.06 and 0.12, risk-free rate 0.03. You already know that at rho equal to negative 1 the variance hits zero at weight 0.75 on the first asset, and the mean there is 0.75 times 0.06 plus 0.25 times 0.12, which is 0.075. The excess over 0.03 is 0.045. A zero denominator and a positive excess is not a large Sharpe ratio. It is not a number. The lab's refusal of non-finite slopes is this case. If you let a scanner print infinity, or print a huge stand-in, you have crowned the ghost.

Watch the explosion approach, with the weights sitting still, so you cannot pretend the portfolio is wandering into a clever new hedge. At rho equal to negative 0.50 the covariance is negative 0.015. Solve capital sigma times the raw weight equal to the excess vector 0.03 and 0.09. One solution is raw weights 6 and 2, which normalize to 0.75 and 0.25. The mean is 0.075. The variance is 0.005625, so the standard deviation is 0.075. The Sharpe ratio is 0.045 divided by 0.075, which is 0.60. A finite, ordinary slope. At rho equal to negative 0.90 the same normalized weights, 0.75 and 0.25, still solve the proportionality, the standard deviation falls to about 0.0335, and the Sharpe ratio rises to about 1.34. At rho equal to negative 0.99 the standard deviation is about 0.0106 and the Sharpe ratio is about 4.24. At rho equal to negative 0.999 the standard deviation is about 0.00335 and the Sharpe ratio is about 13.4. The holdings did not become wiser as the ratio climbed. The denominator shrank. At rho equal to negative 1 the denominator is gone and the excess is still 0.045. That is the ghost, and it wore the same weights the whole way down.

The lab's other duty is to show the weights it actually scored. A slope of 13 without the weights is a rumor. The weights 0.75 and 0.25, next to a standard deviation of a few thousandths, tell you the mix is a near cancellation of 0.10 against 0.30. You can see the ghost because the position is on the page. A scanner that reports only the winning slope has hidden the only diagnostic you have. Refusing the non-finite value is half the behavior. Printing the weights is the other half.

The well conditioned pair from the previous board is the control, not a contradiction. Correlation 0.20, standard deviations 0.20 and 0.25, determinant 0.0024, tangency weights 27 over 79 and 52 over 79, standard deviation about 0.1904, Sharpe ratio about 0.470. Nothing in that denominator is a rounding error. The inverse was tame. The grid agreed. You are allowed to trust a slope when the standard deviation is a real risk of the mix and the matrix is safely definite. The rule is not that large slopes are false. The rule is that a slope produced by dividing by almost nothing is not evidence. About 0.470 came from dividing about 0.0895 by about 0.1904. About 13 came from dividing 0.045 by about 0.00335. The arithmetic operation is the same. The situation is not.

You might ask whether a scanner that prints a Sharpe ratio of 13, or of 50, has found something you should scale up with leverage. No. Scaling rides the ray. It multiplies a standard deviation you already have. If the standard deviation is a near cancellation, leverage multiplies a number that existed because two risky legs were pretending to offset. The notes refuse to call that offset a tradable hedge until the borrow, the basis, and the gap risk have names, and that refusal was already in force when the variance merely got small. Leverage does not name them. Check the denominator and the weights. If the mix is 0.75 and 0.25 on volatilities 0.10 and 0.30 with correlation near negative 1, you are looking at the cancellation, not at a fund that earned a slope of 13 by a property you can borrow against. The lab refuses the non-finite endpoint and shows the weights so this question can be answered from the page.

Change the risk-free rate on the ghost, one input, and the infinity changes character without becoming a portfolio. Keep rho equal to negative 1, weights 0.75 and 0.25, mean 0.075, standard deviation 0. If you set r sub f equal to 0.075, the excess is 0 and the standard deviation is 0. You do not have a slope of zero, and you do not have a slope of infinity. You have a single riskless point that earns exactly the cash rate, which is what a zero-variance claim is worth when its mean matches r sub f, and you still have not named the borrow, the basis, or the gap risk that would make the zero real. If you set r sub f equal to 0.08, above 0.075, the excess is negative 0.005 while the standard deviation is 0. The ratio runs off to a non-finite negative value. The lab should refuse that too. A ghost with a minus sign is still a ghost. The original case, r sub f equal to 0.03 and excess 0.045, is the flattering version, the one a scanner is tempted to crown. The unflattering version is the same weights and the same zero. Only the hurdle moved.

Put the approach on one more correlation so the climb is not four memorized lines. At rho equal to negative 0.50 you had standard deviation 0.075 and Sharpe ratio 0.60 exactly, because 0.045 divided by 0.075 is 0.60, and the variance 0.005625 is 0.075 squared. Halfway in correlation from that point toward negative 1 is not halfway in the ratio. By rho equal to negative 0.99 you were already near 4.24, and the last thousandth of correlation, from negative 0.999 to negative 1, is where the ratio stops being a usable number at all. A lab that samples rho on a coarse grid can step over that last thousandth and still print a shocking finite slope from a standard deviation of a few thousandths. Showing the weights 0.75 and 0.25 is how you notice. The slope alone will look like a discovery.

You can write the ray as a formula and check each scale against it. With slope about 0.470 and intercept 0.03, the mean on the ray is 0.03 plus 0.470 times the standard deviation you chose. At standard deviation 0 the mean is 0.03. At standard deviation about 0.0952 the formula returns 0.03 plus 0.470 times 0.0952, about 0.0747, which matches the half-cash point. At standard deviation about 0.1904 it returns about 0.1195, the fund itself. At standard deviation about 0.2856 it returns about 0.1642, the borrowed point. The formula is the line. It does not know whether a given standard deviation required borrowing. You know that only by comparing the standard deviation with the fund's own standard deviation, about 0.1904. Below that, you are lending, if cash earns 0.03. Above that, you are borrowing at 0.03, and if that rate is a fiction you do not get to keep the formula past the fund.

The raw weights, before you normalize, are the numerical tell. At rho equal to negative 0.50 the solver returned raw weights 6 and 2, and dividing by their sum 8 produced 0.75 and 0.25. At rho equal to negative 0.90 the raw weights are 30 and 10, ten times larger, and they still normalize to the same 0.75 and 0.25. At rho equal to negative 0.99 the raw weights are 300 and 100. At rho equal to negative 0.999 they are 3000 and 1000. Each time you push the correlation one digit closer to negative 1, the inverse inflates the un-normalized solution by another factor of ten, and the portfolio you would actually hold does not change. That inflation is the ill-conditioned cousin of the ghost. If a program prints the raw vector and someone forgets to normalize, the position looks enormous. If a program normalizes and prints only the Sharpe ratio, the position looks brilliant. You need both the normalized weights and the standard deviation on the page. The raw vector going from 6 and 2 to 3000 and 1000 is not a bigger trade. It is a matrix getting closer to singular while the holdings stay 0.75 and 0.25.

Here is what the warning does not mean. It does not mean low correlation is useless. At rho equal to negative 0.50 the same weights produced a finite Sharpe ratio of 0.60 and a standard deviation of 0.075, which is a real point on a risky frontier, quieter than either asset. You may study that point. You may not slide the correlation to negative 1 and keep the interpretation. It does not mean the closed form is always the thing to invert. When the matrix is singular, at exact cancellation, there is nothing to invert and the slope is not finite. When the matrix is merely ill conditioned, the raw weights can become huge before normalization, which is a numerical cousin of the same ghost. It does not mean a Sharpe ratio near 0.470 on the well conditioned example is a forecast. It is the slope implied by the mu, the capital sigma, and the r sub f you typed. If those inputs are absurd, the ray is absurd, which is what the lab brief says in the other direction. If it looks absurd, the inputs are absurd. A calm slope can also be absurd inputs. The specific disease on this board is the tiny denominator. Do not divide by it, do not leverage it, and do not report it without the weights that produced it.

Name the idea. The protocol is a ticket on a cell. Each cell starts with a sequence equal to its index. The producer may write cell i when its sequence equals the monotonic head. After the write, it publishes head plus one. The consumer may read when the sequence equals tail plus one, then publishes tail plus capacity, so the producer can reuse the slot on the next lap. Those two publishes are the whole conversation. Everything else is how you find the cell and how you fail closed when the ticket is not yours.

Fix a capacity of four for the paper walk. Four is the walk in the notes. The cells begin as four tickets: slot zero holds sequence zero, slot one holds sequence one, slot two holds sequence two, and slot three holds sequence three. The monotonic head is zero. The monotonic tail is zero. The mask folds a counter onto a slot. For this capacity, counters zero and four and eight all land on slot zero, counters one and five land on slot one, and so on. The counter itself does not wrap. The slot index does. You will see the counter reach four and five while the slots stay inside zero through three.

Push the first event, and name every load. Load the head with a relaxed load. The head is zero. The mask sends you to slot zero. Acquire the cell sequence. It is zero. It equals the head, so the difference is zero and the producer is allowed to claim. Claim the head, which moves the monotonic head from zero to one, and then write the payload into the cell. After the write, release the sequence head plus one, using the head you claimed from, which is zero, so you publish sequence one. The tickets are now one, one, two, and three. The head is one. The tail is still zero. Notice that slot one still holds its original sequence one. That one was the starting ticket, not a publish. Slot zero's one is a publish. They look alike and they are not the same event. You tell them apart by knowing which lap you are on, which is what the rest of the walk forces you to do.

Push the second event. Relaxed load of head gives one. Slot one. Acquire sequence one. It equals the head. Claim the head, now two. Write. Release sequence two. Tickets: one, two, two, three. Head is two.

Push the third. Head two, slot two, sequence two, equal. Claim head three. Release sequence three. Tickets: one, two, three, three. Head is three.

Push the fourth. Head three, slot three, sequence three, equal. Claim head four. Release sequence four. Tickets: one, two, three, four. Head is four. Tail is zero. You have used every slot once. The ring is full, but you should earn that word from the next push rather than from a feeling that four events is a lot.

The fifth push must fail. Relaxed load of head gives four. The mask folds four onto slot zero. Acquire the sequence. Slot zero holds one, the publish from the first push. The signed difference is the sequence minus the head you are holding, one minus four, which is negative three. The difference is less than zero. That is the full test. You do not write. You do not claim. The head stays four. The tickets stay one, two, three, and four. The producer returns false. A false return is a refusal, not a spin you are required to hide inside the push. The event did not enter.

Read that refusal against the sentence you started with. The producer may write when the sequence equals the monotonic head. Sequence one does not equal head four. The consumer has not yet published a later ticket on slot zero, so the producer is not allowed to lap. Forget the capacity publish, and you will see in a moment that the ring works once and then refuses forever. The fifth push is the first time that fact is visible.

Pop once, and switch roles without getting casual about the indexes. Load the tail relaxed. It is zero. Slot zero. Acquire the sequence. It is one. The consumer may read when the sequence equals tail plus one. Tail plus one is one. The sequence matches. Claim the tail, which moves the monotonic tail from zero to one. Read the payload. Then release tail plus capacity. The tail you claimed from is zero, the capacity is four, and zero plus four is four. You publish sequence four on slot zero. Tickets: four, two, three, four. Head is still four. Tail is one. The payload you read is the first event, and slot zero's ticket is no longer one. It jumped by the capacity, from the consumer's point of view: the publish is tail plus capacity, not zero, and not the old sequence plus one.

Push one more event, the one the lab asks you to look at. Head is four. Slot zero. Sequence is four. Four equals the head. The difference is zero. Claim the head, now five. Write the new payload. Release head plus one, which is five. Tickets: five, two, three, and four. Read the ticket on slot zero. It is five. It is not zero. Five is the first producer publish on that slot, which was one, plus the capacity, which is four. The ticket advanced by the capacity. It did not reset. If your paper says zero, you reset, and the lab will disagree. Believe the lab and find the off-by-one.

That single slot's history is the lecture in miniature. Slot zero began at zero, which was its index. The producer published one. The consumer later published four. The producer published five. The monotonic head at those moments was zero, then four. The head never went backward. The slot index did go backward, from the later slots to slot zero, because the mask folded it. People who store the head modulo the capacity as the only counter will eventually be unable to tell a lap from a start. The protocol does not do that. The mask is an address calculation. The ticket is the memory.

Two wrong publishes are worth walking, because an off-by-one is the usual way a paper disagrees with the lab, and the notes tell you to believe the lab. First wrong publish: the producer writes the payload and stores the old head, not the old head plus one. On the first push the old head is zero, so slot zero's sequence stays zero, while the monotonic head advances to one. The consumer's tail is zero. It needs sequence equal to one. It acquires zero. Zero minus one is negative. The consumer believes the ring is empty. The payload is sitting in the cell, and the protocol will not read it. Every later push advances the head further from a ticket that never moved to the value the consumer is waiting for. The checksum never sees identifier zero. The sum fails. The repair is one word: publish head plus one, the head you claimed from, not the head you claimed from with the plus omitted.

Second wrong publish: the producer stores old head plus two. The first push publishes two on slot zero, and the head counter is one. The consumer wants one. It sees two. The difference against tail plus one is positive rather than zero. In the general loop a positive difference means you are not the owner of this observation, so you reload and try again. You can spin there, watching a ticket that jumped over the value you needed. The lab's ticket on that cell will not match your paper, and the instruction is to believe the lab. The legal sequence of tickets on slot zero in the successful walk was zero, then one, then four, then five. Anything that skips one, or that returns to zero, is the off-by-one you were sent to find. After you find it, read the C plus plus and match the release store to the step that published one, not to the step that incremented the head counter. Those are different stores. The head counter becomes one when you claim. The cell becomes one when you release. Same integer, different location, and the consumer is watching the cell.

The question that belongs to this walk is why the consumer waits for tail plus one, rather than for the sequence to equal the tail, or for the sequence to equal the head. The producer publishes head plus one, not head. After the first push, the head counter has already moved to one, and the cell holds one, so a casual glance says everything is one. The consumer's tail is still zero. If the consumer waited for the sequence to equal the tail, it would wait for zero, and the cell no longer holds zero. It holds the publish, which is the old head plus one, and the old head was the tail at the time of the empty ring, so the publish equals tail plus one. Use a different comparison and you will pop an empty ring or skip a full one. On the first pop, tail plus one is one, and the sequence is one. That match is the permission. After the pop, the consumer does not publish tail plus one. It publishes tail plus capacity, which is a ticket for a later lap, not a second copy of the same permission.

What the claim does not mean is that the monotonic counters wrap when the slot wraps, or that a published ticket of five means five live events. After this push, the head is five and the tail is one. The number of events sitting in the ring is four: the three that were never popped, plus the new one. Five minus one is four, which happens to be the capacity, and the ring is full again. You do not need that subtraction stored in a shared size field. You need the tickets. The claim also does not mean the fifth push was a partial write. The difference was negative, so the producer returned before the write. The payload from the first event stayed in the cell until the pop read it, and only then did a later payload replace it, after the ticket had moved to four and the producer had matched it. Order is the protocol. A write that races the read is a broken listing, not a variant.

The idea that makes the tickets worth the trouble is full and empty. Both can show the same masked index. Empty means the consumer's ticket has not been published. Full means the producer's ticket has not come back around. A difference less than zero is the test. A separate size field would be another shared counter. You do not need it.

Go back to the start of the capacity-four ring, before any push, and look at the indexes with a cold eye. Head is zero, tail is zero, and both mask to slot zero. The same index is showing. Acquire slot zero's sequence, which is zero. For the producer, the sequence equals the head, the difference is zero, and the ring is not full. For the consumer, the sequence would need to equal tail plus one, which is one, and the sequence is zero. Zero minus one is negative. The difference is less than zero, so the consumer's ticket has not been published. The ring is empty. Same slot, opposite permissions. If you had inferred the state from the masked index alone, you would have called one situation by one name, and you would have been wrong about one of the two roles.

Now stand at the moment after four successful pushes and before any pop. Head is four, tail is zero. Four masks to slot zero. Zero masks to slot zero. The same index is showing again. Slot zero's sequence is one. For the producer, one minus four is negative, so the producer's ticket has not come back around. The ring is full. The fifth push returns false. For the consumer, tail plus one is one, and the sequence is one. The consumer may read. The ring is not empty. Again one index, and again the two roles disagree. Full and empty are not two names for the same bitmask. They are two failures of two different comparisons, and in this picture only one of the failures is present.

Finish the lap so you can see the empty case return at a nonzero counter. Continue from the tickets you had after the extra push, which were five, two, three, and four, with head five and tail one. Pop the remaining events one by one, and publish tail plus capacity each time.

The tail is one, the slot is one, the sequence is two, and tail plus one is two. Match. Claim the tail, now two. Publish one plus four, which is five. Tickets: five, five, three, four.

The tail is two, the slot is two, the sequence is three, tail plus one is three. Match. Claim the tail, now three. Publish two plus four, which is six. Tickets: five, five, six, four.

The tail is three, the slot is three, the sequence is four, tail plus one is four. Match. Claim the tail, now four. Publish three plus four, which is seven. Tickets: five, five, six, seven.

The tail is four, the slot is zero, the sequence is five, tail plus one is five. Match. This is the event you pushed on the second lap. Claim the tail, now five. Publish four plus four, which is eight. Tickets: eight, five, six, seven. Head is five. Tail is five.

Try one more pop. Tail five masks to slot one. The sequence there is five. Tail plus one is six. Five minus six is negative. The consumer's ticket has not been published. Empty. Stop. You popped five events: the original four, and the one extra push. The notes tell you to pop until empty. This is empty.

Look at the indexes at the end. Head five and tail five both mask to slot one. Same index, and the ring is empty, which you know from the negative consumer difference, not from the mask. The producer, if you asked, would see sequence five against head five, difference zero, and would be allowed to write slot one. Empty for the consumer and writable for the producer, at the same masked index. That is the dual of the full picture, where the producer was refused and the consumer was allowed, also at slot zero.

A size field would seem to make this easier, and it would be a mistake. Suppose you kept a shared counter of how many events are inside, incremented on push and decremented on pop. That counter is touched by both threads on every operation. You have created exactly the kind of shared hot location the tickets were designed to avoid, and you have to define its atomic update, its order relative to the payload, and its behavior when push fails. The protocol you are walking does not need it. The producer learns fullness because the cell sequence is still behind the head. The consumer learns emptiness because the cell sequence is still behind tail plus one. The information is on the cell, published with the release that already had to exist for the payload. A second counter would be another protocol, not a convenience.

Check the arithmetic of "how many are inside" without storing it, on two of the states you already trust. After the four initial pushes, head is four and tail is zero. Four minus zero is four, the capacity, and the fifth push failed. After the full drain, head is five and tail is five. Five minus five is zero, and the extra pop failed. After the first pop and before the extra push, head was four and tail was one. Four minus one is three. You had pushed four and popped one, and three were left. The subtraction of the two monotonic counters agrees with the history. It is a check you can do on paper. It is not a field you must place on the ring and share. If your paper tickets disagree with the lab, believe the lab and find the off-by-one, then come back and see whether head minus tail still matches the number of successful pushes minus the number of successful pops.

Do the capacity-sixteen case at the joints only, because the outcome you are preparing is a sixteen-slot ring, and the notes' capacity of four should not be the only scale your hand can manage. Cells zero through fifteen start with sequences equal to their indexes. Head and tail start at zero. Sixteen successful pushes bring the head to sixteen. Each push i, for i from zero through fifteen, writes slot i and publishes sequence i plus one. The seventeenth push loads head sixteen, masks to slot zero, and acquires sequence one. One minus sixteen is negative. Difference less than zero. Full. The push returns false. Pop once from tail zero. Slot zero's sequence is one, which equals tail plus one. Claim the tail to one. Publish zero plus sixteen, which is sixteen. Push again. Head sixteen matches sequence sixteen. Claim the head to seventeen. Publish seventeen. The ticket on slot zero is seventeen, not zero. Seventeen is one plus sixteen, the first publish plus the capacity. The same sentence as the capacity-four walk, where the ticket became five, which was one plus four. If you reset slot zero to zero on the pop, the producer waiting for sixteen would see a negative difference and the ring would refuse the next lap. Forget the capacity publish and the ring works once.

Lay the occupancy beside the tickets at five moments so the subtraction is a habit rather than a trick you used once. Moment one, the start. Head zero, tail zero, difference zero. Empty, and the consumer's ticket test agrees. Moment two, after one push. Head one, tail zero, difference one. One event lives in slot zero, whose ticket is one. Moment three, after four pushes. Head four, tail zero, difference four. Full. The fifth push returns false, which agrees with a difference equal to the capacity. Moment four, after one pop. Head four, tail one, difference three. Moment five, after the extra push and the full drain. Head five, tail five, difference zero. Empty again, and a further pop returns false. Five moments, one subtraction, and at no moment did you need a size field to know the count. If you insert a size field and it ever disagrees with head minus tail, the size field is the bug. The tickets and the monotonic counters are the protocol. The size field would be a second source of truth, which is a polite name for a source of lies.

The capacity-sixteen joints obey the same subtraction. After sixteen pushes, head sixteen minus tail zero is sixteen, and the seventeenth push fails. After one pop, head sixteen minus tail one is fifteen. After the extra push, head seventeen minus tail one is sixteen, and the ring is full again, with slot zero holding ticket seventeen. After a complete drain of those seventeen events, if you popped once before the extra push and sixteen more times to catch up, tail would meet head at seventeen, and the difference would be zero. You do not have to recite all sixteen intermediate tickets to see the endpoints. You do have to be able to compute one of them if asked. Slot three, for example, starts at sequence three. The producer publishes four when head is three. Much later the consumer, when tail is three, publishes three plus sixteen, which is nineteen. The next producer publish on that slot is the head that lands there, which is nineteen, plus one, which is twenty. Twenty is four plus sixteen. Again the ticket advanced by the capacity between producer publishes, and it did not reset to three or to zero. If your drawing of slot three shows a zero on the second lap, the drawing is the reset bug, moved from slot zero to a slot whose starting index is not zero. The bug is easier to see on slot three precisely because a reset to zero is obviously not the starting ticket and obviously not the next lap.

The question here is whether you may keep a size field anyway, off to the side, just for logging, as long as the protocol does not branch on it. A log that is not on the ring, written by one thread from values it already owns, is a log. A size field inside the ring object, updated by both sides, is another shared counter even if you promise not to branch on it. The promise does not change the cache line. You do not need it for correctness, because head and tail and the cell tickets already tell you full, empty, and, on paper, the occupancy. If a debugger wants a count, compute it from a snapshot you understand to be racy, or have the consumer count pops locally. Do not add a shared size to make the whiteboard feel more like a queue from an introductory course. This queue tells the truth with tickets.

What the claim does not mean is that the same masked index is a bug, or that full and empty are indistinguishable. They are distinguishable, and the whole point of the ticket is that they remain distinguishable when the indexes coincide. At the start, slot zero meant empty. After four pushes, slot zero meant full. After the drain, slot one meant empty, while a producer would still be allowed to write it. If your test treats "indexes equal" as "empty," you will call a full ring empty and then overflow it. If your test treats "indexes equal" as "full," you will refuse to push into an empty ring at startup, because the indexes are equal on the first line of the program. The difference less than zero is the test, and it is a different difference for each role. The claim also does not mean that returning false is a drop you should retry inside the protocol until it becomes true. False means not now. A caller may retry. The ring may not spin inside the definition of a successful push and call the spin a transfer.

The idea that people try to delete, once they have followed a single producer around the ring, is the compare-exchange. Compare-exchange weak can fail even when the value matched, so the loop is mandatory. With one producer the compare-exchange is logically redundant. It stays in the listing because this is the general cell protocol, specialized to one writer by convention. Do not add a second producer and hope the comment saves you.

Say what the operation claims to do, in words, before you talk about deleting it. You load a counter, call it the expected value. You attempt to replace it with a new value only if it still holds the expected value. On success, you were the claimer, and the counter now holds the new value. On failure, you were not, and you typically receive the value that was actually there. Weak means a failure is allowed even when the value matched. That failure is spurious. It is not evidence that another thread won. It is not evidence that the ring is full or empty. It is a property of the weak form. Because a match can still fail, one attempt is not a decision. The loop is mandatory. You retry. On the retry you load again, you recompute the slot, you acquire the sequence again, and you try the claim again. If you treat a spurious failure as a logical false return, you will drop an event on a ring that was willing to take it, or you will report empty when the consumer's ticket was sitting there. The checksum will catch the drop eventually. The listing's loop is how you avoid needing the checksum for this particular cause.

Walk a spurious failure on the first push of the capacity-four ring, so it has a definite shape. Head is zero. Slot zero. Sequence zero. The difference is zero, so you are allowed to attempt the claim. Compare-exchange weak tries to move the head from zero to one, and it fails even though the head really was zero. If the code returns false immediately, the caller hears that the push did not happen, and the ring is still empty, tickets still zero, one, two, three. The event is lost if the caller gives up, or it is delayed if the caller retries at a higher level. Neither of those is the protocol's intended meaning of false. False was reserved for a negative difference, the full test. A spurious failure has a difference of zero and an unsuccessful claim. The loop goes around. The second attempt loads head zero again, sees sequence zero again, and claims. The publish of sequence one happens once, after a successful claim, not once per failed attempt. If you wrote the payload on the failed attempt, you would write a cell you had not claimed. The order in the push is claim, then write, then release the ticket. A failed claim writes nothing.

The redundancy sentence is narrower than it sounds. With one producer, no other producer is racing the head. A plain store of the new head, by that one producer, would not collide with a second writer's store. In that limited sense the compare-exchange is logically redundant. It is not redundant with the consumer, because the consumer does not claim the head. It claims the tail. And it is not a license to delete the memory orders around the cell. The producer still acquires the sequence before deciding, and still releases the new sequence after the payload is written. The consumer still acquires before reading the payload, and still releases tail plus capacity after the read. Those orders are how the ticket and the bytes stay in one story. Removing a redundant-looking claim does not remove them, and removing them does not become correct just because there is only one producer.

Why the listing keeps the compare-exchange anyway is the other half of the sentence. This is the general cell protocol, specialized to one writer by convention. The general protocol uses the claim because more than one producer would otherwise race the head. The practicum uses one producer. The code you are reading was not rewritten into a different algorithm that only makes sense for one producer. It is the general algorithm, used in a setup that happens to have one writer. If you delete the compare-exchange and substitute a plain store, you may still be correct under the one-writer convention, and you are no longer holding the listing the notes tell you to match line by line. Match the listing. Then, if you want to argue that a plain store would also serve one producer, make that argument as a comment on a convention, not as a quiet edit you forget to mention. The loop stays, in any case, for as long as the weak compare-exchange stays, because the spurious failure does not disappear when the second producer is absent.

The dangerous reading is the reverse edit. Students notice that the compare-exchange looks like it was built for several producers, and they add a second producer because the instruction "seems ready." It is not ready. The specialization to one writer is a convention the rest of the capstone relies on, including the way you think about who is allowed to write a given cell. Do not add a second producer and hope the comment saves you. A comment that says "single producer" does not push a second writer's stores into a safe order. If two producers run the general claim correctly, you are in a different design, with a different test plan, and this defense is no longer the defense of the ring you built. If two producers run a listing you edited until the claim is a plain store, they will both write. The checksum is the minimum place that failure shows up. For a small picture, suppose two producers both load head zero on the capacity-four ring, both pass a torn-out compare-exchange, both store head one, and both write slot zero. One payload survives. One identifier never enters the sum. For N equal to four, the honest sum is six. A missing identifier moves the sum, and the equality fails. You do not then time the result. You also do not "fix" it by putting the comment back.

The acquire and the release deserve one pass of their own, because the compare-exchange discussion tempts people to think the only atomic operation is the claim. On the push, the head load is relaxed. The cell sequence load is an acquire. The claim on the head, in the general protocol, is the compare-exchange. The payload write happens after the claim succeeds. The sequence store that publishes head plus one is a release. On the pop, the tail load is relaxed. The sequence load is an acquire. The claim on the tail succeeds or the pop returns false. The payload read happens after that success. The sequence store that publishes tail plus capacity is a release. Relaxed, acquire, and release are three different promises. Relaxed does not keep the ticket and the payload in order. Acquire and release are what make the consumer see the write that happened before the producer's publish, and what make the producer see the slot as free only after the consumer has finished reading. If you leave the compare-exchange in and drop the release, you have kept the impressive instruction and lost the protocol.

The consumer's claim can fail spuriously too, and the pop loop is mandatory for the same reason. After the first successful push, slot zero holds sequence one, tail is zero, and tail plus one matches. Compare-exchange weak tries to move the tail from zero to one and fails even though the tail was zero. If the pop returns false on that failure, the caller thinks the ring is empty. It is not. The ticket is published and the payload is waiting. A checksum consumer that gives up will report a sum short by identifier zero, and for a four-identifier run the sum will not be six. The loop retries, reloads the tail, reacquires the sequence, sees the match again, and claims. Only then does it read, and only then does it release zero plus capacity, which is four. A spurious failure writes nothing and publishes nothing. Treating it as emptiness is how a correct producer and a skittish consumer fail the sum with no full ring anywhere in the story.

Count the attempts so the loop is visible. Suppose the weak compare-exchange fails twice and succeeds on the third try, on that first pop. The payload is read once. The sequence four is stored once. The two failures are not two pops, and they must not be two additions into the checksum. If your consumer added the payload on every attempt, including the failed claims, you would add identifier zero three times, and the sum would be inflated by twice the identifier. On this particular identifier the inflation is zero, which is a terrible example because zero plus zero is zero. So use the second event. Identifier one sits in slot one after the second push. Three attempts that each added the payload would contribute one, three times, and the sum would be high by two. The legal sum of zero through three is six. High by two is eight, and eight is not six. The equality catches a consumer that acts before the claim succeeds. The order is the same shape as the push. Claim, then touch the payload, then release the next ticket. The loop exists so that a weak failure can happen without becoming that early touch.

The question to answer is whether you should delete the compare-exchange to make the single-producer ring simpler for the defense. You should not. The defense is of the listing you can walk, and the listing includes a weak compare-exchange inside a loop because a weak compare-exchange can fail even when the value matched. The logical redundancy is a remark you are expected to understand, not a patch you are expected to apply in silence. You can say, out loud, that with one producer the claim is not resolving a fight between producers. You should then say that the loop remains mandatory while the weak form remains, and that the cell still needs its acquire and its release. A simpler drawing that omits the spurious failure will not match the code the moment the claim fails for no logical reason. The whiteboard has to have a loop on it, or the whiteboard is a different program.

What the claim does not mean is that a spurious failure is a full ring, or that keeping the general protocol invites you to widen the convention. A negative difference means full, on the producer side, or empty, on the consumer side. A failed weak compare-exchange means try again. Conflating them will make a correct ring look lossy under contention that does not exist. And the survival of the compare-exchange in the file is not an invitation. Specialized to one writer by convention means one writer. The general form is there so you are reading the real cell protocol rather than a fairy-tale queue. It is not there so the capstone can grow a second producer over the weekend. If the checksum ever fails after an edit of this kind, stop. Do not interpret a timing of a broken ring. Match each line of the C plus plus to a step you have already taken on paper, on the capacity-four walk, before you touch the sixteen-slot ring and before you touch a clock.

The idea in this opening block is the hole between a bid and an ask. A limit order rests at a price. A marketable order trades against the opposite side. The spread is the best ask minus the best bid. The mid is the average of those two prices. Neither the spread nor the mid is a trade until someone crosses.

Say what rests, before you say what trades. A bid is a limit order to buy. It sits in the book at the price you named, with a size, until something trades against it or it is taken away. An ask is a limit order to sell, and it rests the same way. The best bid is the highest resting buy. The best ask is the lowest resting sell. Everything behind those two prices is depth. The touch is only the best bid and the best ask, together with the sizes posted there. The lecture starts at the touch because the spread and the mid are summaries of the touch, not of the whole book.

A marketable order is an order that can trade now against the opposite side. If you are buying, marketable means you are willing to pay the ask, or to pay worse than the ask if the ask is not deep enough. If you are selling, marketable means you are willing to hit the bid. The order that rests does not cross. The order that trades does. Keep those as different verbs. Posting is not printing. A price can sit in the book all day and never be a trade.

The spread is the hole. In words, spread equals best ask minus best bid. When the ask is strictly above the bid, the spread is positive, and there is a set of prices between them where nothing is resting at the touch. The mid is the average. In words, the mid equals the best bid plus the best ask, divided by two. You should check that the mid sits halfway across the hole, because that is what an average of two numbers does. The distance from the ask down to the mid equals the distance from the mid down to the bid, and each of those distances is half the spread. That identity is arithmetic. It is not a claim that anyone will trade at the mid.

The lab stores prices as integer cents and displays them as dollars. A one-tick improve, in that representation, moves a price by one cent. Use that unit for the arithmetic, and label the pictures as checks of the definitions, not as a tape. Let the best bid be some integer number of cents, call it B. Let the book be one tick wide, so the best ask is B plus one. The spread is one cent. The mid, in cents, is B plus one half. It is not an integer cent. There is no resting price at the mid, because the only integer prices at the touch are B and B plus one, and the average fell between them. Display the spread as dollars by dividing by one hundred. One cent displays as one hundredth of a dollar. The mid displays as B cents, converted to dollars, plus half a cent. Half a cent is not a tick in this lab. The summary is allowed to fall between ticks. A resting order is not.

Change one input. Keep the same bid B and make the book two ticks wide, so the ask is B plus two. The spread is two cents, which displays as two hundredths of a dollar. The mid is B plus one, an integer cent, one tick above the bid and one tick below the ask. Half the spread is one cent, and it lands on a tick only because the spread was an even number of cents. The one-tick book and the two-tick book use the same definitions. One of them has a mid that is not a legal resting price. The other has a mid that is. Do not round the odd case into a tick and then call the rounded number the mid. The mid is the average. If the average is not a cent, say so.

The notes tell you what a take does, and the lab brief tells you the order of the clicks you will try: lift the offer, then hit the bid, then improve. Price priority comes first. The take consumes the best price first, then the next. It does not pro-rate across prices. Time priority inside a level does not matter here, because a level is one aggregated size. If you lift a size that is strictly smaller than the ask size, you print at the best ask, and the remaining size at that same price is the old size minus what you took. The best ask price does not change. The spread does not change. The mid does not change. You changed a size, which is real, and you did not change the hole. If you lift exactly the aggregated size, the level is empty and it is deleted. The next ask, if there is one, becomes the best ask, and the spread widens by whatever distance that next price sits above the old ask. This lesson does not give you a numeric depth behind the touch, so you do not invent the next price. You only keep the rule: best price first, then the next, no pro-rata.

Improve is a different edit from a take, and join is a third. Join adds size at the current best. The best price does not move. The spread does not move. Only the size at a price you already had gets larger. Improve inserts a new best, one tick tighter, and only if it does not cross. On the two-tick book, the bid is B and the ask is B plus two. A one-tick improve on the bid posts at B plus one. That price is still strictly below the ask, so it does not cross. The new spread is one cent. The new mid is B plus one and a half cents. You created a new best bid. You did not trade. Joining the old bid at B would have left the spread at two cents. The difference between join and improve, on this book, is exactly one cent of spread, and it is a difference in where the order rests, not in a print.

On the one-tick book the same improve does not have a free cent to occupy. The bid is B and the ask is B plus one. One tick tighter than the bid is the ask itself. Posting there does not go past the ask, so it is not a cross in the strict sense of a bid above the ask. It does lock the book: the best bid and the best ask would be the same price, and the spread would be zero. The slide says a locked or crossed book is a state you must define, and that this lab refuses to post through the touch. Read those as instructions to you, not as a silent boolean you may guess. A crossed book would be a bid strictly above the ask, a negative spread. A locked book is a zero spread. Through the touch is the thing the lab refuses. You owe the definitions before you claim an improve was accepted. What you can say without guessing the boolean is that a one-tick book has no integer price strictly between the bid and the ask, so an improve cannot insert a new interior best. Join is still available. Join does not need a hole. It only needs a price that already exists.

You might ask whether the mid is the fair price, and therefore whether a print should happen there. The mid is not a fair price in any sense this block defined, and it is not a place a print has to happen. It is the average of two resting prices. Nobody has agreed to trade there. There may not even be a tick there, as the one-tick book showed: the mid sat half a cent inside the hole, and nothing rests at half cents in a book of integer cents. A trade happens when a marketable order crosses against resting size. Until that cross, the spread is a hole and the mid is a summary of the edges of the hole. Calling the summary fair adds a model this block did not give you.

What the claim does not mean is several refusals. It does not mean you can trade at the mid just because you can average the touch. It does not mean a positive spread is a forecast. It is a difference of two prices. It does not mean a locked book cannot exist in the world. It means you must define that state, and that this lab refuses to post through the touch. It does not mean time priority is false at a real exchange. It means that inside this lab a level is one aggregated size, so time order within the level has nothing to act on. It does not mean a take that leaves the best price unchanged left the book unchanged. The remaining size changed. Price summaries are silent about that, and the next block builds the summary that is not silent. It does not mean improve is a way to cross more cheaply. Improve is refused when it would cross. It is a new resting price, one tick tighter, or it is not posted.

Work one take against the two-tick book so the price priority rule has a size attached. Let the ask at B plus two cents have size four, a size chosen so the divisions are small integers, not a size from a tape. Lift one. One is less than four, so you print one unit at B plus two, and three remain at that same price. The spread stays two cents. The mid stays at B plus one. Lift the remaining three and the level deletes. You still have not been given the next ask, so you stop. The four units you lifted all printed at one price, because they never exceeded the level in a single step that ran past it, and because the second step only finished the level. Now change the one input that matters for a walk: lift five against the original size of four, in one take. Four print at B plus two. One remains to be filled at the next ask, which you do not know. The average fill is not B plus two. It is four fifths of the way from that unknown next price back to B plus two, which you cannot evaluate until the next price is real. Price priority told you the order of the prints. It did not invent the second price. That is the whole of the rule.

The idea in this block is that the microprice leans on size. In words, the microprice equals the ask times the bid size, plus the bid times the ask size, all divided by the bid size plus the ask size. A thin ask pulls the microprice up toward the ask. The weighting is inverted: large bid size pulls toward the ask, not toward the bid. The microprice equals the mid when the sizes match. It is undefined when the touch is empty. It is a one-line model of where the next tick is slightly more likely, not a forecast you should size.

Read the weights slowly, because the inversion is the whole content. The ask price is multiplied by the bid size. The bid price is multiplied by the ask size. The side with more resting size contributes its weight to the opposite price. A large bid size, with a thin ask, puts most of the weight on the ask price, so the result sits closer to the ask than the mid does. The story you are allowed to say is the one the lecture gives, and no more. The thin side is easier to exhaust, so the next print is slightly more likely to be against that thin side, which is a print toward the thin side's price. That is a one-line model. It is not a distribution you have estimated, and it is not a size.

Put the equal-size case down first, with symbols, then with the smallest integers. Let the bid price be B and the ask be A, with A above B, and let both sizes equal some positive number s. The microprice is A times s, plus B times s, divided by two s. The s cancels, top and bottom, and you get A plus B, divided by two. That is the mid. The lecture's sentence is checked: equal sizes, microprice equals mid. The smallest positive integer sizes are one and one. Same cancellation. Nothing about the distance A minus B entered, because the weights were equal. A wide book and a one-tick book have the same conclusion when the sizes match. The microprice collapses to the average. Size, in this special case, dropped out.

Now make the ask thin. Let the bid size be two and the ask size be one. These are the smallest integers that make the bid twice the ask, and they are a check of the formula, not a print from the lab. The microprice is A times two, plus B times one, divided by three, which is two A plus B, all over three. The mid is still A plus B, over two. Subtract. Use the common denominator six. Two times the quantity two A plus B is four A plus two B. Three times the quantity A plus B is three A plus three B. Subtract the second from the first and you get A minus B. Divide by six. The microprice sits above the mid by the spread divided by six. On a one-tick book the spread is one cent, so the microprice is one sixth of a cent above the mid. On the two-tick book the spread is two cents, so the microprice is one third of a cent above the mid. Both sit strictly above the mid and strictly below the ask. Check the distance to the ask. The ask minus this microprice is A minus the quantity two A plus B, over three. That simplifies to A minus B, over three, the spread over three. For the one-tick book, the microprice is one sixth of a cent above the mid and one third of a cent below the ask. The mid itself is half a cent below the ask. One third is less than one half, so the microprice is closer to the ask than the mid is. That is the thin ask pulling up.

You can do the subtraction once, in general, and then every later case is a plug-in. The microprice minus the mid has numerator two times A times the bid size, plus two times B times the ask size, minus the quantity A plus B, times the sum of the sizes. Expand and cancel. The terms that survive are the spread, times the bid size minus the ask size. The denominator is two times the sum of the sizes. So the gap equals the spread, times the bid size minus the ask size, divided by twice the sum of the sizes. When the bid size exceeds the ask size, the gap is positive and the microprice is above the mid. When the ask size exceeds the bid size, the gap is negative and the microprice is below the mid. When the sizes match, the gap is zero, which you already computed. The inverted weighting is this sign. Large size on the bid pushes the microprice up, toward the ask.

Change one input. Keep the ask size at one and change the bid size from two to three. The ask is now thinner relative to the bid. The microprice is three A plus B, over four. The same general gap formula says the gap is the spread times three minus one, divided by twice four, which is the spread times two over eight, which is the spread over four. On a one-tick book that is a quarter of a cent above the mid, larger than the one sixth of a cent you got when the bid size was two. The distance from this new microprice up to the ask is the half cent from the mid to the ask, minus that quarter cent, which is a quarter of a cent. Earlier, with bid size two, the distance to the ask was a third of a cent. A quarter is less than a third, so tripling the bid size, relative to an ask of one, pulled the microprice closer to the ask than doubling did. The direction matches the sentence. A thinner ask pulls harder toward the ask.

The empty touch is the case the formula refuses. If both sizes are zero, the denominator is zero, and the microprice is undefined. Do not replace the undefined ratio with the mid out of habit. The mid of an empty touch is itself undefined, because there is no best bid and no best ask to average. The slide says the microprice is undefined when the touch is empty. That is a stop sign, not an invitation to fill in a zero. A one-sided book is a different mess. If the ask size is zero and the bid size is positive, the algebraic expression collapses to the ask price, but an ask size of zero means you do not have that ask. Feeding a missing side into a two-sided formula is how you print a confident number on a touch that is gone. Say the touch is empty, or say one side is missing, and stop. The one-line model needs two prices and two positive sizes.

You might ask why a large bid should pull the microprice toward the ask. It feels backwards if you think of size as a vote for its own price. The formula is not that vote. It multiplies each price by the opposite size. The lecture's reading is the inverted one: the heavy side is harder to exhaust, so the next tick is slightly more likely to print against the thin side, and a print against the thin ask is a print up at the ask. The weight on the ask is the bid size for that reason. You do not have to believe the story in order to compute the line. You do have to avoid selling the line as something it is not. It is not a forecast you should size. The lecture says that in the same breath as the definition. A model of where the next tick is slightly more likely is not a position.

What the claim does not mean needs the same care as the algebra. It does not mean the next trade will occur at the microprice. The microprice is a weighted average of the bid and the ask. On integer cents it will often fall between ticks, as it did when it sat a sixth of a cent above the mid. Nothing rests there, and nothing has to print there. It does not mean you should choose a size from it. The lecture forbids that use in one clause. It does not mean depth behind the touch has been consulted. Only the two touch sizes entered. It does not mean the outside world revised its view. This block has not yet let you trade. The number moved only when you changed the sizes in the formula. It does not mean equal sizes make the microprice a fair value. They make it equal to the mid, and the mid is still only an average. It does not mean a zero denominator can be patched with the last price you remember. Undefined means you do not quote the model.

Work the gap formula on the other side, so a thin bid is not left as a mirror you only imagine. Let the bid size be one and the ask size be two. The gap is the spread times one minus two, over twice three, which is negative spread over six. The microprice sits below the mid by a sixth of the spread. On the one-tick book that is a sixth of a cent below the mid, and the distance down to the bid is a third of a cent. The thin bid pulled the microprice down toward the bid, by the same arithmetic that pulled it up when the ask was the thin side. Change the ask size from two to three, one input, and leave the bid size at one. The gap is the spread times one minus three, over twice four, which is negative spread over four. On the one-tick book the microprice is a quarter of a cent below the mid, closer to the bid than the sixth-of-a-cent case was. Same formula, opposite sign, same lesson. The thin side attracts the microprice. The heavy side does not get to drag the average toward itself.

The idea in this block is that imbalance is a ratio. In words, top-of-book imbalance equals the bid size minus the ask size, divided by the bid size plus the ask size. It lives in the closed interval from negative one to one, with the endpoints only as a side disappears. It ignores everything behind the touch. It is a ratio, so it does not grow just because the book is busier. Positive means more size on the bid. Depth is a different feature. Say so when you use the touch ratio as a feature, or the deep book will make a fool of the touch.

Compute the equal case and the thin cases you already used, so the ratio is not a new object with a new book. Sizes one and one: the numerator is zero, the denominator is two, the imbalance is zero. Sizes two on the bid and one on the ask: the numerator is one, the denominator is three, the imbalance is one third. Sizes three and one: the numerator is two, the denominator is four, the imbalance is one half. Sizes one on the bid and two on the ask: the imbalance is negative one third. Sizes one and three: negative one half. The sign matches the slide. More size on the bid is positive. More size on the ask is negative. None of these interior cases reached the ends of the interval. With both sizes positive, the absolute value of the numerator is strictly smaller than the denominator, so the ratio is strictly inside negative one and one.

The endpoints are limits, and you should treat them as limits. Let the ask size approach zero while the bid size stays a positive number s. The ratio approaches s over s, which is one. Let the bid size approach zero while the ask size stays positive, and the ratio approaches negative one. The interval stated in the lecture is the closed interval, and those ends are what a vanishing side does to the algebra. A touch that has actually lost a side is the empty-side problem from the microprice. You do not quietly plug a zero size into a feature and keep talking as if both sides were present. You say the side is gone. The number one is the boundary of the ratio, not a healthy two-sided book.

The ratio does not grow just because the book is busier. Double both sizes in the one-third case. Bid size four, ask size two. The numerator is two, the denominator is six, and two over six is still one third. Double again: bid size eight, ask size four, numerator four, denominator twelve, still one third. The difference of the sizes did grow. It went from one, to two, to four. The feature the lecture defined did not grow. That is what it means for a ratio to ignore a common scale. A busier touch, in the narrow sense that both sides were multiplied by the same constant, leaves imbalance unchanged. If your feature is the raw difference, bid size minus ask size, that feature doubled and doubled again. The lecture did not define that difference. It defined the ratio. If you wanted the difference, you would be building a different feature, and you would have to say so.

Change one input so the scale is not common. Keep the ask size at one and change the bid size from two to four. You did not scale both sides. Imbalance goes from one third to four minus one, over four plus one, which is three fifths. Three fifths is larger than one half, and larger than one third. Check three fifths against one half: three fifths is zero point six, one half is zero point five. The bid got busier and the ask did not, so the ratio moved. The earlier invariance was never a claim that size does not matter. It was a claim that a common scale does not matter. One side changing is not a common scale.

There is a direct link to the microprice, and it falls out of the gap you already derived. The gap between microprice and mid equals the spread times the bid size minus the ask size, divided by twice the sum of the sizes. The imbalance is that same difference of sizes, divided by the sum, without the two. So the gap equals the spread times the imbalance, divided by two. In words, the microprice equals the mid plus half the spread times the imbalance. Check it on the numbers you have. Imbalance one third, half of that is one sixth, times the spread is the spread over six, which matched the microprice when the sizes were two and one. Imbalance one half, half of that is one quarter, times the spread is the spread over four, which matched the sizes three and one. Imbalance zero gives microprice equal to the mid. Imbalance negative one third puts the microprice a sixth of the spread below the mid. You do not need a new derivation for each book. You need the mid, the spread, and this ratio. That also tells you the limit. As imbalance approaches one, the microprice approaches the mid plus half the spread, which is the ask. As imbalance approaches negative one, the microprice approaches the bid. The thin side pulls, and a missing side would pull all the way to the price that disappeared, which is another reason not to evaluate the model on a one-sided touch.

You might ask whether you can add the size at the next price into the ratio and still call it top-of-book imbalance. You cannot. The moment the next price's size enters the numerator or the denominator, you are computing a different feature. The lecture calls depth a different feature. Top-of-book imbalance ignores everything behind the touch. If the deep book is large on the ask and the touch is large on the bid, the touch ratio is positive and the depth may say something else. Using the touch ratio as if it were the whole book is how the deep book makes a fool of the touch. You may build a depth feature. You may not rename it to this one. Say which one you used.

What the claim does not mean is a set of trading conclusions the ratio does not support. A positive imbalance does not mean the price will rise. The lecture never said that. It said positive means more size on the bid, at the touch, now. It does not mean the bid is informed. Nothing in the ratio is a label for information. It does not mean the book is busy or quiet. The common-scale check showed a busier touch with the same ratio. It does not mean the microprice has become a forecast you should size. The link between them is algebraic. Half the spread times the imbalance shifts the mid. That shift is still the one-line model, with the same prohibition. It does not mean a value near one is a stronger signal in the sense of a tested prediction. It means one side of the touch is almost gone. It does not mean two books with the same imbalance have the same sizes. They have the same ratio. The sizes themselves can differ by any common scale, and the spread can differ too. The ratio throws that scale away on purpose.

Work a join as a size change that is not a common scale, so the feature moves for a reason you chose. Start from bid size two and ask size one, imbalance one third. Join the bid with two more units. Join adds size at the current best, so the bid size becomes four and the ask size stays one. You just computed that ratio: three fifths. The spread did not change, because join does not move the price. The mid did not change. The imbalance did, from one third to three fifths. The microprice, by the link, moves from a sixth of the spread above the mid to three tenths of the spread above the mid, because half of three fifths is three tenths. On a one-tick book that is a move from about seventeen hundredths of a cent above the mid to three tenths of a cent above the mid. No take occurred. No news was required. You added resting size on the bid, and both features moved. If a log of the mid is your only log, this join is invisible. The lecture's state is not the mid alone.

Change the join to the other side, one changed input. From the same start, sizes two and one, join the ask with two units instead. The ask size becomes three. The bid size stays two. Imbalance is two minus three, over two plus three, which is negative one fifth. The sign flipped. You began with more size on the bid and, by adding two to the ask, finished with more size on the ask. The microprice crosses from above the mid to below it: half of negative one fifth is negative one tenth, so the microprice sits a tenth of the spread below the mid. On the one-tick book that is a tenth of a cent below the mid. The same verb, join, on the other side, moved the ratio from positive one third to negative one fifth. That is a large move relative to the interval from negative one to one, and it came from posting two units, not from a trade. Posting is part of the state. A feature that claims to describe the touch has to notice it.

The idea in this block is that the lab stores cents as integers. Floating point is a bad representation of a tick. Dollars are how those integers are shown.

Separate the two representations before you do arithmetic. The identity of a price is the integer count of cents. Two prices are the same price when those integers are equal, and not otherwise. The display takes that integer and divides by one hundred, and shows a dollar amount. One hundred cents display as one dollar. One cent displays as one hundredth of a dollar. Two cents display as two hundredths of a dollar. The display can look like a decimal. The thing you compare, cancel, and hash is the integer. A match on the display string is a match only insofar as the string is a faithful picture of the integer. The lab's choice is to keep the integer as the thing itself.

Work the conversions that the later shortfall will assume you can do without a calculator, using the one-tick and two-tick books. The mid of the two-tick book was an integer cent, B plus one, and it displays by dividing that integer by one hundred. The mid of the one-tick book was B plus one half cents, which is not an integer. You can still display it, as the dollar picture of B cents plus half a cent. You should not store that half cent back into the book as a price identity. The book does not have a tick there. Improve and join, restated in integer cents, are edits to integers and to sizes. On the two-tick book the ask integer is the bid integer plus two. Improve the bid by one tick and the new bid integer is the old bid integer plus one. Compare it to the ask integer. The two operations are easy to confuse in a user interface that shows dollars, because one hundred point zero one and one hundred point zero zero look like a small visual step. Change one input in the improve. On the two-tick book, try to improve the bid by two ticks instead of one. The new bid integer would equal the ask integer. That is the lock you already met on the one-tick book when you improved by one. The spread would become zero. The lab refuses to post through the touch, and the notes allow an improve only if it does not cross. Landing on the opposite price is the boundary the slide told you to define. You do not get a new interior level. The integer arithmetic is what makes the boundary obvious: the candidate price minus the opposite best is zero, so there is no positive spread left. A three-tick improve from the same bid would make the candidate greater than the ask, a negative spread, a cross, and the notes refuse it. One extra tick, as a single changed input, took you from a legal one-tick-tighter improve, through the boundary you must define, to a cross. The display in dollars does not make that sequence clearer than the integers do. The integers are the sequence.

You might ask why the lab does not store dollars as decimals with two places and compare those. Because the identity the lab chose is the integer cent, and a decimal display is a view. Two-place decimals are a picture of cents only when every price really is a whole number of cents and the picture has not been produced by a binary floating-point operation. The lecture's warning is specific. Floating point is a bad representation of a tick. If production uses doubles for price identity, you will eventually fail to match an order that is equal. The failure is an equality failure: two values that were meant to be the same tick do not compare equal, so a match, a cancel, or a level aggregation misses. This lesson does not give you a particular bit pattern, so do not invent one. The claim you have is the one printed: doubles are the wrong identity, and the miss eventually happens. Integer equality does not have that miss. Ten thousand cents equals ten thousand cents. There is no second way for that sentence to be almost true.

What the claim does not mean is that the market's tick is always one cent. It means this lab's price identity is an integer cent. Another market can have a different tick, and you would store that tick as the integer, not store dollars in a double. It does not mean floating point cannot display a price. Display is allowed. Identity is the thing that must not be a double. It does not mean two averages, such as two microprices, are forbidden to be floating-point numbers. The microprice often falls between cents. It is a summary. You do not use it as the key that decides whether two orders share a level. It does not mean a locked book has been ruled impossible by the integer type. The integer type can represent a bid integer equal to an ask integer. The lab's refusal to post through the touch is a rule about orders, not a property of the number type. It does not mean time priority returns just because prices are integers. A level is still one aggregated size. Integer prices make the level's key exact. They do not split that level back into a time-ordered queue. The notes say time priority inside a level does not matter here for that reason.

Work an equality check the way a matching engine has to, on cents, with one near miss. Suppose one order rests at ten thousand cents, which displays as one hundred dollars exactly. A second order arrives with a price that was computed as one hundred dollars plus one cent minus one cent, and the computation was done in integers: ten thousand plus one minus one is ten thousand. The integers match. The second order joins the level. Size adds. The price key does not change. Now suppose the same verbal expression, one hundred dollars plus one cent minus one cent, was evaluated in a double and did not land back on the same value as the resting price. The lecture says you will eventually fail to match an order that is equal. In that failure the second order does not join the level. It opens a second level at a price that was supposed to be the same tick, or it fails to find the order it meant to cancel. The book is then wrong in a way that no later average will announce. The spread, the mid, and the microprice can all look ordinary while two levels that should be one level sit apart. That is why identity comes before the summaries. A summary of a split level is a summary of a bug.

The hole that remains is one cent. Nothing in that display required a binary fraction. Every price in the book is still an integer, and every comparison is still integer equality. The microprice, if the sizes are two and one with the bid the larger size, sits a sixth of a cent above the mid. The mid of ten thousand and ten thousand two is ten thousand one cent, which happens to be an integer. A sixth of a cent above that mid is not an integer. You display it if you want a summary. You do not round it back to ten thousand one and then claim it is an order. The order at ten thousand one is the improved bid you posted, a real level with a real size. The microprice is a weighted average sitting a fraction of a tick away from it. Same neighborhood in the display. Different jobs. The integer is the only one of the two that can match.

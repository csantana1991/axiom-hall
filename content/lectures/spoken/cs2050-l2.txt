Two counters on one line are enough to make a fast algorithm look slow. In the book you are building, the producer writes head and the consumer writes tail. Those are different variables, written by different sides, and if the atomics that hold them share a cache line, each side invalidates the other on every update. The algorithm can be lock-free and still slow, because the coherence protocol is the lock you claimed not to take.

Name the actors before you name the penalty. One side publishes new work by moving head. The other side retires work by moving tail. Nothing in that division requires the two addresses to be neighbors. They are neighbors only if you laid them out that way. A line is commonly sixty-four bytes. Two counters that sit a few bytes apart fall inside one such line. Every store to head then threatens the consumer’s copy of that line, and every store to tail threatens the producer’s copy. The logical protocol may be wait-free of locks. The line is still a single piece of coherence state.

Use the lab’s model so the invalidation has a number you are allowed to quote. The cache lab is not a processor. It charges one when two writers sit on different lines, and eight when they share one line. The eight is a teaching penalty so the toggle does something visible. Head and tail are the two counters the brief tells you to keep in mind. When they share, you quote eight. When padding puts them on different lines, you quote one. Eight divided by one is eight, which matches the brief’s sentence that a shared line costs eight times two private lines. You say “model” in the same breath as eight. You do not publish eight as a cycle count.

Run the model on a concrete number of updates. Suppose the producer and the consumer each complete one thousand updates, and you score the pair the way the lab scores the pair: one charge when the counters sit apart, eight when they share. The apart case totals one thousand model units. The shared case totals eight thousand. The extra seven thousand is not extra messages in your protocol. It is the teaching penalty for co-location. Change one input, the update count, from one thousand to two hundred fifty. The apart case costs two hundred fifty. The shared case costs two thousand. The ratio is still eight. Change the penalty instead, from eight down to two, and leave the updates at one thousand. The shared case costs two thousand, the apart case costs one thousand, and the gap shrinks to one thousand. The layout problem does not shrink with it. Only the classroom constant moved.

You might ask whether lock-free means the updates no longer interfere. It does not. Lock-free, in the sense this lecture uses, means someone is always making progress if the operating system lets a thread run. It does not mean uncontended. It does not mean wait-free. A push can fail and retry. Two threads can be lock-free and still bounce a line on every store, because the coherence protocol does not consult your progress theorem before it invalidates. The theorem is about forward progress in the algorithm. The bounce is about who owns the line. You can have the first and still pay the second.

What the slowdown claim does not mean is that every pair of atomics must be padded, or that the number eight is what your chip will report. The eight exists so the toggle is obvious. On metal, the related experiment is two threads incrementing adjacent counters, with and without padding, under a proper harness. The course numbered 6.172 assigns versions of that experiment. Your capstone still has to be timed on the machine you claim. What the claim also does not mean is that head and tail are the same variable. They are different writes. The failure is that different writes landed on one line. Distinct names in the source did not create distinct lines.

Look at the fix the capstone actually writes, so the counters are not an abstract pair. The cell is aligned to sixty-four. head and tail are each aligned to sixty-four. That is the false-sharing fix for the counters. Alignment to sixty-four makes the address a multiple of sixty-four, and for an aligned object the language makes sizeof a multiple of the alignment, so the next object can start on a fresh line. If head begins at address zero, a sizeof of sixty-four puts tail’s start at sixty-four. Those two starts are different lines of the common width. The model then charges one, not eight. If you forgot the alignment and the two counters sat at offset zero and offset eight, both offsets live in the line that runs from zero through sixty-three, and the model charges eight. The source difference is a layout difference of eight bytes versus sixty-four. The charge difference, in the model, is one versus eight.

Take a smaller update count and include a failed push, so retry does not stay verbal. Suppose the producer attempts one hundred twenty pushes and twenty of them fail and retry, while the consumer completes one hundred successful pops. The lesson does not give you a separate charge for a failed attempt. It does tell you that a push can fail and retry, and that failure is part of not being wait-free. The stores that do reach head and tail are what hit the line. If those stores share a line, each of them pays the shared tariff in the model, whether or not the logical operation ultimately committed. You do not get to call the bounce free because the algorithm had a retry loop. The retry loop is evidence that progress is conditional. The line is evidence that the counters were neighbors.

Hold the boundary in one place before you move on. Sharing a line is a layout fact. Lock-free is a progress fact. The lab maps the layout fact onto one and eight so you can see it. The capstone separates head and tail by aligning each to sixty-four. None of that separation is a claim about payload bytes inside a cell, which is the next question, and none of it authorizes you to print the teaching eight as if a harness had produced it.

Pad the indexes, not the sermon. Head and tail go on separate lines because both are hot and both are written, by different cores, under the same coherence protocol. The cell is a different pattern. Its sequence and its payload may share a line. The protocol has one writer of the payload at a time, then a release, then the other side reads. That is not two hot counters. Do not pad the payload until a profile says the cell is hot.

Keep the two patterns in separate sentences so you do not “fix” the quiet one with the medicine meant for the loud one. Head is written by the producer on the cadence of new work. Tail is written by the consumer on the cadence of retirement. Those two writes interleave for the whole run. A cell’s payload is written by one side, published, and later read by the other side. The line that holds the payload changes hands across a release, rather than being stored by both cores on every operation. Sharing that line is ordinary publication. Sharing the line that holds head and tail is false sharing. The capstone struct follows the distinction: head and tail are each aligned to sixty-four, and the cell is aligned to sixty-four as well, so a cell starts on a line boundary. Alignment of the cell is about where consecutive cells land. It is not an invitation to split sequence from payload before a profile asks you to.

Check the consecutive-cell hazard with arithmetic, because a correct alignment on one cell is not the end of the story. If sizeof of the cell were not a multiple of sixty-four, adjacent sequence numbers would share a line. Suppose the cell started at address zero and sizeof were forty. The next cell would start at forty. Both starts lie in the span from zero to sixty-three, so the two sequence numbers would sit on one line. The language does not leave you in that state for an aligned struct: it makes sizeof a multiple of the alignment. You check that size in the build anyway. A multiple you should be willing to see is sixty-four, or one hundred twenty-eight, or any other positive multiple. Sixty-four times two is one hundred twenty-eight. If sizeof is one hundred twenty-eight, the cell at zero is followed by a cell at one hundred twenty-eight, and their sequence numbers do not share the line that begins at zero. If sizeof is sixty-four, the next cell begins at sixty-four, which is the next line of the common width. Either multiple separates adjacent cells. Forty does not, and forty is the size you hope the build does not print.

Change one input. Keep the start at zero and change the imagined sizeof from forty to ninety-six. Ninety-six is not a multiple of sixty-four, because sixty-four times one is sixty-four and sixty-four times two is one hundred twenty-eight, and ninety-six is strictly between them. The next cell would start at ninety-six. Does it share the first line? The first line covers offsets zero through sixty-three, and ninety-six is past that, so the starts themselves are on different lines. The danger is the span of the object. A cell of ninety-six bytes beginning at zero occupies the line at zero and continues through offset ninety-five, which includes the line that begins at sixty-four. The following cell begins at ninety-six, inside the line that begins at sixty-four, because the next line boundary after sixty-four is one hundred twenty-eight, and ninety-six sits between them. So the tail of the first cell and the head of the second cell share a line. That is why “the starts differ” is not enough. The stride has to be a multiple of the line, which is what the language’s sizeof rule gives you, and what a stray ninety-six does not.

You might ask why the payload is allowed to sit with the sequence at all, if lines are so fragile. Because the sharing pattern is different from two hot counters. One writer fills the payload, then a release publishes the sequence ticket, then the other side acquires and reads. You will meet the order words in the next lesson. For layout, the point is narrower: there is not a pair of cores storing into that payload on every update the way the producer stores head and the consumer stores tail. Padding the payload splits a line that the protocol was using as a single published record. You pay extra lines on every cell of the ring. The lecture tells you not to do that until a profile says the cell is hot. A sermon about purity is not a profile.

What padding the indexes does not mean is that the ring has become uncontended, or that every byte in the program now deserves sixty-four bytes of private space. Lock-free still does not mean uncontended. The coherence protocol still runs. A push can still fail and retry. Someone is still making progress only in the sense that, if the operating system lets a thread run, someone moves forward. Separate lines remove a particular self-inflicted invalidation between head and tail. They do not remove the work, and they do not make a failed push succeed. What the permission to share a line inside the cell also does not mean is that any two fields may share. It means this pattern, one writer then a release then a reader, is not the two-writer pattern. A second hot counter hidden in the cell would put you back on the first pattern, and you would pad that counter, not recite the exception.

Count the lines the index padding actually buys, using the capstone’s own alignments. One line for head, one line for tail, each because each is aligned to sixty-four and sizeof will be a multiple of that alignment. If both had shared a single line, the model would charge eight for the writer pair. Separated, the model charges one. The ratio is eight, and it is still a teaching penalty. Over five hundred paired updates the model says five hundred versus four thousand. The cell array is extra lines beyond those two, one multiple of sixty-four per cell, because the cell itself is aligned. That cost is the cost of keeping adjacent sequence numbers off the same line. It is not a reason to insert still more padding between sequence and payload inside the cell. The profile, not the lecture’s adjective, would be the reason.

Suppose the ring holds sixteen cells and sizeof of each cell is sixty-four. Sixteen times sixty-four is one thousand twenty-four bytes, which is sixteen lines. Add the line for head and the line for tail and you are looking at eighteen lines of aligned objects before you have counted anything else in the process. If you had split every payload off its sequence onto a second line, those sixteen cells would become thirty-two lines, and the total with the two indexes would be thirty-four. You would have spent sixteen extra lines to separate fields the lecture told you may share. That spend is what “do not pad the payload until a profile says the cell is hot” is protecting you from. The number sixteen for the ring length is an illustration of the multiplication, not a capacity the lesson fixed. Change it to eight cells and the same sizeof: eight times sixty-four is five hundred twelve bytes, eight lines of cells, plus two index lines, ten lines, versus eighteen lines if you had split each cell in two. The comparison scales. The rule does not change.

A profile that does say the cell is hot is allowed to overturn the economy. The lecture’s sentence is conditional. Until that measurement exists, the hot pair is head and tail, and the pad goes there, written as alignas in the language of the capstone. After that measurement exists, you pad what the measurement names, and you say so. You do not pad in advance to win an argument.

The build check is the boring half of the same beat, and it is the half that catches the forty and the ninety-six. You print sizeof of the cell and sizeof of the aligned index objects. You want multiples of sixty-four. You do not accept a comment that claims the multiple. The notes are blunt: check it in the build anyway, even though the language makes sizeof a multiple of the alignment. Adjacent sequence numbers share a line only when that check would have failed. Passing the check is the layout corresponding to the toggle in the “on” position for the indexes, and to a cell stride the line can respect.

Leave this beat with the two patterns labeled. Head and tail are two writers, so they take two lines, and the model’s one versus eight is how the lab shows the difference. Sequence and payload are a publish-then-read pair, so they may share until a profile says otherwise. Sizeof must be a multiple of sixty-four or the next cell collides with this one. The language helps, and you still read the number.

Pinning is a last-mile concern. A thread that migrates cores loses its warm cache. The lines it had resident on the old core are not a gift the new core keeps. Pinning can help a measured hot loop, and it can hurt a machine that was doing something else. It is an operating-system setting. It is not a line of the language you sprinkle beside alignas and then forget.

Start from the loss, because the loss is the part you can reason about without pretending to know the scheduler’s mood. Suppose the consumer has been popping on one core long enough that the lines for tail and for the cell it is reading are warm there. The operating system then moves that thread to another core. The next pop must warm those lines again on the new core. The protocol did not change. Head and tail are still on separate lines if you aligned them. The cell is still a publish-then-read pattern. What changed is which core holds the warm copies. Migration spends the warmth. That is the entire content of the sentence about losing the warm cache. It does not, by itself, tell you to pin.

The decision needs a measurement and a cost, and the lecture gives you the shape of both without giving you a timer reading. Pinning can help when the hot loop is the thing you measured, and the migration shows up in that measurement. Pinning can hurt when the machine was doing something else with the core you just reserved. A reserved core is a core some other runnable work does not get. If that other work was the producer, you may have pinned the consumer onto a core and left the producer to migrate, which recreates the warmth problem on the other side. If that other work was unrelated, you have purchased a steady handoff by shrinking the machine. Neither purchase is free, and neither is implied by a correct alignas.

You might ask whether a pin belongs in the same header as the sixty-four-byte alignment, so that every run of the ring is pinned. It does not belong there. Alignment is a fact about addresses in the type. Pinning is a fact about the operating system’s placement of a thread onto a core, for a particular process, on a particular machine, while other work exists or does not. Writing it into the type confuses a layout invariant with a scheduling policy. The lecture’s wording is deliberate: an operating-system setting, not a line you sprinkle. You apply it, if you apply it, outside the struct, after you have a hot loop in hand, and you are prepared to remove it when the machine’s other work matters more.

What pinning does not mean is that a pinned thread is correct, or that an unpinned thread is false-sharing. False sharing is two cores writing the same line. You can pin both threads and still place head and tail on one line, and the model will still charge eight rather than one. You can leave both threads free to migrate and still have head and tail on distinct lines, and the model will still charge one, with extra warmth losses whenever a migration actually happens. Pinning does not repair a stride that is not a multiple of sixty-four. It does not publish the payload. It does not turn a lock-free ring into a wait-free one. A push can still fail and retry on a pinned core. Someone makes progress, if the operating system lets a thread run, whether or not you pinned, and pinning is one way of asking the operating system to keep letting that thread run on the same core. The ask can be refused or overridden by a policy you do not control. You do not get to describe the setting as a property of the algorithm.

Work a small numeric picture of warmth without inventing a miss penalty the lesson does not state. Take the two index lines and one cell line, three lines, warm on the consumer’s current core. After a migration those three lines are the ones that must be fetched again before the consumer is back where it was. The lesson does not give you a cycle cost for that refetch, so you do not invent one. You do say the count of lines that went cold: three, in this picture. Change one input. Let the consumer also have the next cell warm, so the cold set is head’s line if it was reading head, tail’s line, and two cell lines, four lines rather than three. The migration still costs the warmth of whatever was hot, and the only number you are entitled to report is the count of those lines, not a fable about nanoseconds. If your harness later times the migration, the harness number replaces the picture. Until then, the picture is a count.

There is a second last-mile temptation in this lecture, and it is vectorization. The slide says it briefly, and briefly is the right length if you do not promote it. A width such as the one named by five hundred twelve in the vector extension is a width, not a personality. It tells you how many bytes a wide register can hold. Five hundred twelve bits divided by eight is sixty-four bytes. That is the same count as the common line. The coincidence of the two counts is not an identity of mechanisms. A wide load moves values into a register file. A cache line is the unit of coherence and of fill from memory. Using a wide load does not pad head away from tail. Refusing a wide load does not separate them either.

The ring index is a bad place to spend that width. A dependent ring index does not vectorize into a metaphor. The next slot depends on the ticket, the mask, and the protocol’s full and empty tests. Those dependencies are scalar on purpose. The slide tells you where wide loads do belong: on the cold feature path, not on the sequence number. The sequence number is a publication ticket. Loading it wide, in the hope that a neighboring ticket is useful, pulls neighbors you may not be allowed to treat as data, and it does not make the dependency shorter. The cold path is allowed to be wide because it is not the handoff. If you cannot point at a cold path whose inner loop streams a field, you do not have a vectorization target yet. You have a slogan.

You might ask whether, since five hundred twelve bits equal sixty-four bytes, a single vector load is “the same as” touching one line and therefore always a good idea on the ring. It is not the same, and it is not always a good idea. Equality of byte counts is arithmetic. Sameness of purpose would require that the bytes you want are exactly a contiguous vector-width of payload you are allowed to read together. The sequence number fails that test. Two counters on two lines fail it as well: they are sixty-four bytes apart when each is aligned, so one sixty-four-byte vector that starts at head does not contain tail if tail begins at the next line, and a vector that somehow covered both would be evidence they were not separated. Wide loads are for the cold feature path the slide already named. They are not a third way to express alignas.

What the vector sentence does not mean is that the extension is useless, or that mentioning a width commits you to a rewrite. It means you will not talk about the ring index as if dependence disappeared in a wide register. It also does not mean the cold path is obliged to use that width. Often a cold path is cold enough that the width does not move the program. The lecture’s warning is against the metaphor, not a quota. And it does not mean five hundred twelve bits changed the line size. The line is still commonly sixty-four bytes, still not a law of nature, still something you confirm for the chip. The register width is a fact about instructions. You keep the two facts in different sentences.

Put the last-mile pair side by side with the layout you already fixed. Separate lines for head and tail remove false sharing between the counters. A cell stride that is a multiple of sixty-four keeps adjacent sequence numbers off one line. Payload may share its cell’s line with the sequence until a profile says the cell is hot. After that layout is true, a migrating thread can still lose the warm copies of those lines, and pinning is the operating-system setting you may use on a measured hot loop, knowing it can hurt other work. After that layout is true, a wide register can still be the wrong tool on the sequence number and a possible tool on a cold feature path. None of the last-mile choices revise the model charges. Shared counters still map to eight in the lab. Separated counters still map to one. You still do not publish those charges as the chip.

If you want a single numeric check you can do on the board before you leave, do the pin-and-pad combination the wrong way and then the right way. Wrong way: head at offset zero, tail at offset eight, both threads pinned to two cores. The pin is present and the line is shared, so the model still charges eight, and the pin has not bought the missing stride. Right way: head at a multiple of sixty-four, tail sixty-four bytes later, threads unpinned. The model charges one, and you accept migration only if the measurement says the warmth loss matters. Then, and only then, you consider the operating-system setting. The order is the lecture. Layout first. Measurement next. Pinning last, and not in the header.

A dependent index stays scalar in both the wrong way and the right way. Suppose the head ticket is nineteen and the capacity is a power of two so the mask is the right address calculation, a fact the mask lab is for and this beat does not redo. The slot you compute depends on that ticket. A wide register does not compute sixteen next slots unless the protocol actually has sixteen independent tickets, which a single producer head does not. One ticket, one slot, one consumer matching it. Width does not multiply the protocol. That is what “does not vectorize into a metaphor” is protecting. You may use the width where the slide allows, on the cold feature path, after the handoff has already moved a record you are free to reread in bulk.

The claim you take out is modest, which is the tone the last mile deserves. Migration drops warm lines. Pinning can keep a measured loop on one core and can crowd out other work. It is not C++ in the struct. A vector width, even one whose byte count equals the common line, is a width. The sequence number is not where you swing it. The counters are still the false-sharing problem, and padding them apart is still the toggle, written as alignment, scored by a model of one and eight that you quote as a model and nothing else.

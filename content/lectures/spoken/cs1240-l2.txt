The board in front of you is titled flow is a feasible assignment, not a path. A flow does not pick one route and stop. It assigns a number to every edge. On the studio graph the edges are these, and you should keep the capacities in view for the whole hour. S to A has capacity 10. S to B has capacity 10. A to B has capacity 4. A to T has capacity 8. B to T has capacity 10. There is no direct edge from S to T, and there is no edge from B to A in the original drawing. Four nodes, five edges. Call the nodes V equal to 4 and the edges E equal to 5 when we need those letters later. They are not needed to say what a feasible assignment is.

Three conditions make an assignment a flow. First, on every edge the assigned value f of u v sits between 0 and the capacity c of u v, inclusive. You do not send a negative amount on the original edge, and you do not send more than the edge can carry. Second, conservation: at every node other than the source S and the sink T, inflow equals outflow. Material does not appear or disappear at A, and it does not appear or disappear at B. Third, the value of the flow, written v of f, is the net flow out of the source. On this graph that is the sum of what leaves S along S to A and S to B, minus anything that returns to S. At the start nothing returns, so the value is just those two edge values added.

A path is one way to build an assignment, and it is not the assignment itself. Suppose you send 6 units along the path S, A, T, and 5 units along the path S, B, T, and you send nothing on A to B. Then f of S A equals 6, f of A T equals 6, f of S B equals 5, f of B T equals 5, and f of A B equals 0. Check capacity. 6 is at most 10, 6 is at most 8, 5 is at most 10, 5 is at most 10, and 0 is at most 4. Check A. Inflow is 6 from S. Outflow is 6 to T and 0 to B. They match. Check B. Inflow is 5 from S. Outflow is 5 to T. They match. The value is 6 plus 5, which is 11. This is a feasible flow of value 11. It is not a single path. It is two paths superimposed, and the superposition is still just five numbers, one per edge.

Eleven is feasible and not maximum, and you can see the room without a theorem. A to T still has 2 units of unused capacity, but S to A has 4 units unused, so you could push 2 more along S, A, T. B to T has 5 unused, and S to B has 5 unused, so you could push 5 more along S, B, T. Doing both would raise the value from 11 to 18. I am not claiming yet that 18 is the maximum. I am claiming that 11 was not forced on you by feasibility. Feasibility is a filter. Optimality is a later claim, and it will need a certificate.

Watch a near miss that fails conservation, because students often repair capacity and forget the nodes. Suppose f of S A equals 8, f of A T equals 5, and every other edge is 0. Capacities hold, since 8 is at most 10 and 5 is at most 8. At A the inflow is 8 and the outflow is 5. Three units arrived and never left. That assignment is not a flow, even though every edge looks legal in isolation. The value you might have tried to read off the source is 8, and the amount arriving at T is 5. Those two numbers disagree because A is a leak. On a real flow they cannot disagree. Every original edge adds its flow to the head and subtracts it from the tail, so if you sum net outflow over all four nodes, every edge contributes 0 to the grand total. S and T are the only nodes allowed a nonzero net outflow. Therefore the net outflow of S plus the net outflow of T equals 0, once A and B contribute nothing. Net flow out of S equals net flow into T. On the feasible example, both sides equal 11. On the leaky assignment they do not, and that is how you catch it.

Watch a miss that fails capacity and nothing else. Set f of S A equal to 9 and f of A T equal to 9, with the other edges 0. A is balanced, 9 in and 9 out. S sends 9. T receives 9. The edge A to T has capacity 8, and 9 is larger. The assignment is not a flow. Capacity is not a suggestion that the algorithm may bend on the last edge into the sink. The inequality is 0 less than or equal to f of u v, which is less than or equal to c of u v, on every edge, including the ones that feel like the goal.

You might ask whether the value could be defined as the flow into T instead of the flow out of S, and whether that would change the problem. On any assignment that already conserves material at A and B, the two definitions are the same number, by the grand-total argument you just used. On an assignment that does not conserve, the two definitions can split, and the object is not a flow anyway. So you may compute the value on either end when you are auditing a real flow. If the two ends differ, you do not have a tie to break. You have a broken node.

Build one more feasible flow that actually uses the decoy, so you do not leave this board thinking every legal assignment ignores A to B. Send 3 along S, A, B, T, and send 4 along S, A, T, and send 2 along S, B, T. Then f of S A equals 7, because 3 plus 4 both leave through A. f of A B equals 3. f of A T equals 4. f of S B equals 2. f of B T equals 5, because 3 arrived from A and 2 arrived from S. Capacities hold: 7 is at most 10, 3 is at most 4, 4 is at most 8, 2 is at most 10, and 5 is at most 10. At A, inflow 7 equals outflow 3 plus 4. At B, inflow 3 plus 2 equals outflow 5. The value out of S is 7 plus 2, which is 9. The value into T is 4 plus 5, which is 9. This is a feasible flow, it uses every edge, and its value is smaller than the earlier flow of 11 that left A to B idle. Using more edges did not produce a larger value. That is another reason a flow is an assignment of numbers and not a collection of paths you score by how many of them you managed to draw.

Here is what feasibility does not mean. It does not mean the assignment is a maximum, and the value 11 is the witness. It does not mean every edge is in use. A to B carried 0 in the feasible example, and nothing about conservation required it to carry more. It does not mean the flow is a path you can highlight with one finger. Two paths were packed into one assignment, and a later maximum will also be an assignment of five numbers, whether or not you bother to decompose it. It does not mean a large capacity out of S decides the value. S can emit up to 20, because 10 plus 10 is 20, and T can accept up to 18, because 8 plus 10 is 18. The smaller of those two cuts is only an upper hint. Edges in the middle can force the value lower still, or they can turn out not to bind. You do not get to announce 18 because T's doors sum to 18, not until a feasible flow actually attains 18 or a cut argument says nothing larger fits. The doors are a candidate. The flow is a different object.

The board in front of you is titled residual edges are how you change your mind. A flow is a commitment of numbers. A residual graph is the record of how you are still allowed to edit those numbers. The edit has two directions, and the sentence to memorize uses one edge. If you send 8 along an edge of capacity 10, the residual graph keeps 2 forward and gains 8 backward. Forward is unused capacity. Backward is the amount you could cancel if you decide the 8 was partly a mistake.

Speak the forward formula the way the slide writes it. c sub f of u v equals c of u v minus f of u v. That is the forward residual capacity. On the edge that carried 8 out of a capacity of 10, c sub f equals 10 minus 8, which is 2. You may still increase the flow on that edge by as much as 2 without breaking capacity. The backward residual is the flow itself, f. You may decrease the flow by as much as 8, which is the same as sending material along a residual edge that points the other way. If the original drawing had no reverse edge, the backward residual is not a new pipe in the warehouse. It is permission to revise the assignment.

Apply that to the whole studio graph on the first augmentation you will actually run, not only to a lone edge. Order the adjacency the way the edge list was given. From S, the neighbors are A and then B. From A, the neighbors are B and then T. From B, the neighbor that matters is T. A breadth-first search from S in the initial residual graph, which is just the original graph because every flow is still 0, reaches A and B at distance 1 and reaches T at distance 2. The first time T is reached is through A, by the path S, A, T. That path has two edges. Its bottleneck is the minimum of the residual capacities along it, which are 10 and 8, so the bottleneck is 8. Augment by 8. Set f of S A to 8 and f of A T to 8. Leave the other flow values at 0. The value of the flow is 8.

Now write every residual, because a partial update is how feasible flows get corrupted. Forward residual on S to A is 10 minus 8, which is 2, and the backward residual from A to S is 8. Forward residual on A to T is 8 minus 8, which is 0, and the backward residual from T to A is 8. S to B is untouched, forward residual 10, backward 0. A to B is untouched, forward residual 4, backward 0. B to T is untouched, forward residual 10, backward 0. A is conserved: 8 in from S, 8 out to T. B is conserved: nothing in, nothing out. The value out of S is 8, and the value into T is 8.

The backward edges are already useful as a concept even if the next search does not need all of them. Suppose, after this augmentation, you wished you had sent only 3 into A to T. You would push 5 along the backward residual from T to A and on back to S, canceling 5 of the flow. Forward residual on A to T would become 5, and f of A T would fall from 8 to 3. Nothing in that edit requires a physical stream from T toward A in the original network. You are lowering numbers you raised a moment ago. That is the entire meaning of the backward residual.

An augmenting path is a source-to-sink path in the residual graph, not in the original drawing. After the push of 8, A to T has forward residual 0, so the original path S, A, T is no longer augmenting. S, B, T is still there, with residual capacities 10 and 10. S, A, B, T is also still there: residual 2 on S to A, 4 on A to B, and 10 on B to T. Its bottleneck would be 2. You are allowed to use any of these. Edmonds–Karp will insist on a shortest one, and that rule is the next board. Residual capacity by itself does not rank them. It only says which edits are legal, and how large each edit can be.

You might ask whether a backward residual can create flow on an edge that ends above capacity. It cannot, if you only augment by the bottleneck. The bottleneck is the minimum residual capacity on the path. Along a forward edge, residual capacity is remaining room under the capacity, so increasing the flow by at most that room keeps f at most c. Along a backward edge, residual capacity is the current flow, so decreasing the flow by at most that amount keeps f at least 0. Conservation is preserved because the same amount enters and leaves each intermediate node on the path, whether the edge is traversed forward or backward. A backward traversal at an intermediate node cancels one unit of some old outflow or inflow and the rest of the path balances the node. The edit stays inside the definition of a flow. The value rises by the bottleneck, because the path begins at S on a forward edge out of S, or, if it ever left S backward, that would be a different and usually irrelevant shape. In the augmentations we run today, each path leaves S forward and enters T forward, so the value increases by exactly the bottleneck.

Recompute the forward formula on every edge of the value-11 example from the previous board, so the notation is not tied to a single memorable 8. There f of S A was 6 on a capacity of 10, so c sub f of S A equals 10 minus 6, which is 4, and the backward residual from A to S is 6. f of A T was 6 on a capacity of 8, so the forward residual is 2 and the backward residual from T to A is 6. f of S B was 5 on a capacity of 10, so the forward residual is 5 and the backward residual is 5. f of B T was 5 on a capacity of 10, so the forward residual is 5 and the backward residual is 5. f of A B was 0 on a capacity of 4, so the forward residual stays 4 and the backward residual stays 0. An augmenting path S, A, T now has bottleneck the minimum of 4 and 2, which is 2. Pushing 2 raises the value from 11 to 13 and drops the forward residual on A to T from 2 to 0. A different augmenting path, S, B, T, has bottleneck the minimum of 5 and 5, which is 5. You could take that one instead and raise the value from 11 to 16. Residual capacities are a menu of legal edits. They are not yet a policy about which edit to take first.

Here is what a residual edge does not mean. It does not mean you built extra capacity into the studio graph. The capacities are still 10, 10, 4, 8, and 10. The residual 8 from A back to S is not an eighth pipe. It is the right to undo. It does not mean every residual edge will be used. Several will sit untouched in the short run we are about to finish. It does not mean the flow is still 8 once further augmentations exist. Eight is only the value after one edit. The residual graph is how you notice that the edit is unfinished: T is still reachable from S. When T stops being reachable, you will have a different claim, and that claim is a cut, not a mood about being tired of searching.

The board in front of you is titled shortest residual path. Edmonds–Karp always augments a breadth-first path, so the path has the fewest edges, not the fattest bottleneck. Fewest edges is recomputed after every augmentation. You do not keep the old distances. You run breadth-first search again on the residual graph you just edited.

On the opening residual graph the distances from S are small enough to write in one breath. S is at distance 0. A is at distance 1. B is at distance 1. T is at distance 2, and it can be reached in two hops either through A or through B. The path S, A, B, T has three edges. Its bottleneck is the minimum of 10, 4, and 10, which is 4. It carries less than the two-edge paths, and it is longer. The slide's warning is visible here even though the fat path is not the long one. Fat paths can be shorter in value and longer in hops. Edmonds–Karp ignores the temptation to optimize the bottleneck. A three-edge path is simply not a candidate while a two-edge path exists.

There are two shortest paths, and breadth-first search will return one of them according to neighbor order, not according to width. S, A, T has bottleneck minimum of 10 and 8, which is 8. S, B, T has bottleneck minimum of 10 and 10, which is 10. The fatter shortest path is S, B, T. With the edge list that puts A before B among the neighbors of S, the search reaches T first through A. Edmonds–Karp therefore augments S, A, T by 8, even though a shortest path with bottleneck 10 was also present. Shortest does not mean best among the shortest. It means fewest edges, and ties are an artifact of the adjacency order. You should not spend the hour trying to break those ties by hand. The bound does not depend on it.

After that augmentation the residual distances are the next thing to recompute, not to remember. Forward residual S to A is 2, so A is still at distance 1. S to B is still 10, so B is still at distance 1. A to T is now 0, so T is no longer reached through A. B to T is still 10, so T is at distance 2 through B. The bottleneck is the minimum of 10 and 10, which is 10. Augment by 10. Set f of S B to 10 and f of B T to 10. The value of the flow is 8 plus 10, which is 18.

The slide states the complexity in three lines, and you should say them as a product rather than as a promise about this picture. There are order V E augmentations under the shortest-path rule. Each breadth-first search is order E. The total is order V E squared. On the studio graph V is 4 and E is 5, so V times E squared is 4 times 25, which is 100. That 100 is not a prediction that you will augment one hundred times. You augmented twice. It is not a cycle count. It is what the expression inside the bound becomes when you substitute the size of this graph. Order of 100 and the number 2 can describe the same run, because big-O is an upper shape, not a stopwatch.

The notes separate two reasons the picture ends. With integer capacities the bottleneck is a positive integer, so the value climbs by at least 1 each time. Ours climbed by 8 and then by 10. Both steps are integers, and both are larger than 1. The at-least-1 fact is enough to know that an integer-capacity run cannot continue forever if the value is also bounded above, which it is, because no flow can exceed the capacity of the edges into T. That integer climb is not the Edmonds–Karp proof. The notes say the hop argument holds more carefully, and that the integer observation is only why a four-node picture ends quickly. The hop argument is the reason you are allowed to write order V E augmentations rather than something that depends on the numeric size of the capacities. If capacities were enormous integers, climbing by 1 could take an enormous number of steps. The shortest-path rule is what prevents the bound from following those integers.

You might ask whether the first augmentation contradicted the claim that each augmentation strictly increases the source-side distance of some node. On the numbers you just wrote, the distances of S, A, B, and T were 0, 1, 1, and 2 before the push of 8, and they were 0, 1, 1, and 2 after it. No finite label on this drawing got larger. The slide you can check by eye is the neighboring sentence: distances from S in the residual graph never decrease. They did not decrease. The strict increase, the one that powers the order V E count, is a statement about the hop argument's bookkeeping, and the notes tell you that bookkeeping holds more carefully than the quick integer story. What this particular picture shows you without that bookkeeping is the second search. After the push of 10, T is unreachable. Treat that as the distance of T leaving the finite board. The run stopped because breadth-first search failed to reach T, not because you guessed that 18 felt large.

Put the same shortest-path habit on the modified capacity, the one where B to T carries 6 instead of 10, and record the distances explicitly so the second picture is not only a value. Here is what the shortest-path rule does not mean. It does not mean you augmented the fattest path. The first bottleneck was 8 while a two-edge path of bottleneck 10 was sitting in the same graph. It does not mean a long fat path would have been preferred if one had existed. The three-edge path of bottleneck 4 was ignored because it had more edges, not because 4 was compared with 8 and found wanting. It does not mean the expression V E squared is the number of searches you will perform. Substituting 4 and 5 produced 100, and the algorithm performed 2 successful augmentations plus one search that failed. It does not mean some other algorithm is illegitimate. The slide says Dinic is faster, and that Edmonds–Karp is the one you can still see. Seeing is the point of this graph. You can write every residual by hand. A faster algorithm that you cannot audit on a napkin is a different class session. And the rule does not mean the value 18 has been certified by the fact that you are bored with searching. The certificate is the cut, which is the next board. Until you name the cut, 18 is the value of a particular feasible flow, and a feasible flow is only a lower bound on the maximum.

The board in front of you is titled the cut is the certificate. When no residual path remains, the nodes reachable from the source form the source side of a minimum cut. Its capacity equals the flow you have already pushed. That equality is the max-flow min-cut theorem, and the notes say it is the only proof you should trust. You should not trust a feeling that you cannot see another path, unless that feeling is a finished breadth-first search in the residual graph.

After the two augmentations, the reachable set is S, A, and B. T is not in it. The cut is the pair of sets, source side S, A, B, and sink side T. The capacity of a cut is the sum of the original capacities of edges that leave the source side and enter the sink side. The edges that do that are A to T, capacity 8, and B to T, capacity 10. The sum is 18. There is no edge from S to T. A to B does not cross, because B is on the source side. The flow you pushed has value 18. The cut capacity is 18. They match. Write the theorem in words. The maximum of v of f equals the minimum of c of S, T. Here one flow attains 18, and one cut has capacity 18, so both are optimal. No feasible flow can be larger than 18, because every unit of value must cross every cut, and this cut has only 18 to give. No cut can be smaller than 18, because this flow already pushes 18, and a cut cannot be smaller than a feasible value.

Price the other source sides so 18 is not an uncompared accident. The source side containing only S has leaving edges S to A and S to B, capacities 10 and 10, total 20. The source side S and A has leaving edges S to B, A to T, and A to B, capacities 10, 8, and 4, total 22. The source side S and B has leaving edges S to A and B to T, capacities 10 and 10, total 20. A to B points into this source side, so it is not part of the cut capacity. The source side S, A, and B totals 18, and it is the smallest of the four. On this graph the minimum cut is unique. The reachable-set rule found it without you having to list the other three, but listing them is how you see that the rule and the definition agree.

A to B is a decoy, and the notes mean that precisely. A maximum flow need not use every edge. B to T is full. If your algorithm saturates the cut, it is done whether or not the decoy moved. Idleness on A to B is not a bug in the search. You might ask whether you are required to return the paths, or whether the minimum cut is the answer the caller wanted. The theorem certifies a number, the maximum value. It also hands you one source side, the reachable set, whose capacity equals that number. It does not hand you a unique flow. You just built two different assignments with the same value. If a caller wants a routing, you still have a flow in hand, the edge values, and more than one of them can be correct. If a caller wants a proof that no larger value exists, the paths are the wrong object to wave around. Another path might be hiding in a residual you did not draw.

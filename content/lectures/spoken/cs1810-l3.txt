The idea in this block is that a penalty is part of the objective. Ridge adds lambda over 2 times the squared length of the parameter. You are no longer minimizing the original loss. Saying the penalty only stabilizes the numerics is sometimes true and often a way to avoid writing the new minimizer. This block writes the new objective. The next block solves it. The distinction has to be in place before the algebra, or the algebra looks like a printing trick.

The original lab function, with the penalty left at zero, is the square of x minus 1, plus 3 times the square of y plus 0.4. Its minimizer is x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.4, and the value there is 0. The gradient is 2 times x minus 1, and 6 times y plus 0.4. Every path you trusted in the step-size discussion was a path on that function. Turning on a penalty does not tweak those paths. It replaces the function.

The lab adds lambda over 2 times the sum of the squares of x and of y. In words, the penalized function is the old bowl plus lambda over 2 times x squared plus y squared. Lambda equals 0 recovers the unpenalized minimizer, because the added term is zero everywhere and the gradient of a zero term is zero. A large lambda makes the origin win, because the added term charges every point in proportion to its squared distance from the origin, and the charge grows without bound as lambda grows, except at the origin where the charge stays zero. Between those two extremes the minimizer is a compromise. You do not get to describe the compromise as the old answer with a smaller font.

Look at the origin and at the old minimizer as two candidates, before you solve anything, so the penalty is visible as a number. At the origin the old bowl equals 1.48, and the penalty term is zero, so the penalized value is 1.48 for every lambda. At x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.4 the old bowl equals 0, and x squared plus y squared equals 1 plus 0.16, which is 1.16. The penalty term is lambda over 2 times 1.16. For lambda equals 2 that term is 1.16. The penalized value of the old minimizer is 1.16, which is already better than the origin's 1.48, so lambda equals 2 has not crowned the origin. For lambda equals 4 the penalty term is 2 times 1.16, which is 2.32, and 2.32 is worse than 1.48. The old minimizer has become a worse candidate than the origin on the penalized objective, even though it is still the best point on the original bowl. That reversal is the penalty doing its job. You are ranking points by a different number.

The gradient changes whether or not you have located the minimum. Differentiating lambda over 2 times x squared adds lambda times x to the x partial. Differentiating lambda over 2 times y squared adds lambda times y to the y partial. The new gradient is 2 times x minus 1, plus lambda x, and 6 times y plus 0.4, plus lambda y. At the origin those extra terms vanish, so the first step away from the origin matches the unpenalized first step. That coincidence is a frequent source of false comfort. The first step looks familiar, and the destination is not. At the old minimizer, x equals 1 and y equals negative 0.4, the old partials vanish and the new gradient is lambda times 1 and lambda times negative 0.4. For lambda equals 2 the new gradient is 2 and negative 0.8. It is not the zero vector. The old minimizer is not stationary for the new objective. If your iteration sits there after you raised lambda, the iteration is not minimizing the function you claim to be minimizing.

You might ask why anyone adds a term that makes the old best point illegal as a stationary point. The honest answer in this lesson is small. The estimate you get is a different estimate. It is biased for the unpenalized minimizer, which means it does not target that point. The lesson also says that bias can lower risk, and that it does not lower training loss. Training loss, here, means the original bowl, the function you were minimizing before the penalty. Risk means the expected loss under the distribution you will be scored on, from the first lecture of this sequence. Those are not the same number, and the penalty does not automatically improve either of them by existing. What it automatically does is change the objective. Any claim that risk fell is a separate computation you have not done by writing lambda. What you can already see is that the original bowl, which was 0 at its own minimizer, will not be 0 at a different point. The penalty does not lower that training loss. If you report only the penalized value, you are hiding the purchase.

What the claim does not mean is worth pinning down before the closed form makes it look obvious. It does not mean the penalty is a debugger flag with no effect on the answer. You just computed a gradient of 2 and negative 0.8 at the old minimizer when lambda is 2. A flag that changes the gradient changes the problem. It does not mean every positive lambda makes the origin the minimizer. At lambda equals 2 the old minimizer still beats the origin on the penalized scale, 1.16 against 1.48. It does not mean the original bowl's step-size ceiling survives unchanged. The new second derivatives are 2 plus lambda in x and 6 plus lambda in y, because each penalty term contributes lambda to the curvature. The stiff curvature is now 6 plus lambda, so a safe step lies below 2 over the quantity 6 plus lambda. At lambda equals 0 that ceiling is one third, the number you already know. At lambda equals 2 the ceiling is 2 over 8, which is 0.25. A step of 0.3 was legal on the unpenalized bowl and is past the new ceiling. Stabilizing the numerics, if that is what you meant, can require a shorter step, not a longer one, because you made the bowl steeper. It does not mean an L1 penalty is the same object with a different letter. An L1 penalty has a kink at zero and a subgradient, and this lab does not show it. Do not import that geometry into a quadratic penalty. The quadratic penalty is differentiable, and its stationarity condition is an ordinary equation, which is what we solve next.

Change one input, the weight inside the penalty, and recompute the value at the old minimizer so the charge is not tied to the single factor one half that the formula already chose. The lesson's penalty is specifically lambda over 2 times the squared length, and the one half is the same cosmetic choice you have seen on a quadratic, arranged so the derivative carries lambda rather than lambda over 2 or 2 lambda. If you forgot the one half and charged lambda times 1.16 at lambda equals 2, you would report 2.32 instead of 1.16, and you would wrongly say the old minimizer already loses to the origin. The derivative would also be wrong: you would add 2 lambda x instead of lambda x. At lambda equals 2 and x equals 1 that mistaken partial is 4, not 2. The one half is not decoration. It fixes the scale of both the value and the gradient. Use the formula as written. Lambda over 2 times 1.16 at lambda equals 2 is 1.16. The gradient contribution is lambda x, which is 2. The old point is not stationary, and it is not yet beaten by the origin. Both facts come from the same term, read carefully.

The idea in this block is where the bowl moves. The penalty pulls toward the origin, the gradient gains lambda x and lambda y, and the minimizer leaves the point 1 and negative 0.4. That slide is the bias you bought. You should be able to name the new point without running the iteration, and then you should use the iteration only as a check that the code is aimed at the same point.

Set the new gradient to zero and solve the two coordinates separately. They do not couple. In x, 2 times x minus 1, plus lambda x, equals zero. That is x times the quantity 2 plus lambda, minus 2, equals zero, so x times the quantity 2 plus lambda equals 2, so x equals 2 divided by the quantity 2 plus lambda. In y, 6 times y plus 0.4, plus lambda y, equals zero. That is y times the quantity 6 plus lambda, plus 2.4, equals zero, because 6 times 0.4 is 2.4. So y times the quantity 6 plus lambda equals negative 2.4, and y equals negative 2.4 divided by the quantity 6 plus lambda. The fixed point solves this linear equation, not the old one. The old one was the special case lambda equals 0, which returns x equals 2 over 2, which is 1, and y equals negative 2.4 over 6, which is negative 0.4. The formula includes the unpenalized answer. It does not stop there.

The original bowl at these new points is the training loss you gave up. At lambda equals 2 the point is 0.5 and negative 0.3. Check stationarity at that point so the formula is not a black box. The x partial is 2 times 0.5 minus 1, plus 2 times 0.5, which is 2 times negative 0.5, plus 1, which is negative 1 plus 1, which is 0. The y partial is 6 times negative 0.3 plus 0.4, plus 2 times negative 0.3, which is 6 times 0.1, plus negative 0.6, which is 0.6 minus 0.6, which is 0. The gradient is the zero vector. Do lambda equals 4 the same way, because a second row is what makes the pattern a method. The point is one third and negative 0.24. The iteration is the check, not the derivation. Start at the origin with lambda equals 2 and eta equals 0.1. The ceiling is 0.25, so 0.1 is legal. The gradient at the origin is still negative 2 and 2.4, because the penalty terms lambda x and lambda y are zero there. The first landing is 0.2 and negative 0.24, the same first landing as the unpenalized walk. The second step is where the penalty becomes visible. At 0.2 and negative 0.24 the x partial is 2 times negative 0.8, plus 2 times 0.2, which is negative 1.6 plus 0.4, which is negative 1.2. The y partial is 6 times 0.16, plus 2 times negative 0.24, which is 0.96 minus 0.48, which is 0.48. If your late iterates sit near 0.5 and negative 0.3, the code and the formula agree. If they sit near 1 and negative 0.4, you are still minimizing the old function, which means the penalty is not in the update. Those are different bugs. A path that is still moving has not failed the formula yet. A path that has settled in the wrong place has.

A step past the new ceiling is the other bug, and it should look familiar with new numbers. Lambda equals 2, eta equals 0.3, and 0.3 is above 0.25. Start at the origin. The same step size that converged on the unpenalized bowl, where the ceiling was one third, diverges once lambda equals 2 has lowered the ceiling to one quarter. If the path explodes after you raise lambda, the step is a suspect before the formula is.

You might ask whether y and x slide at the same rate, since both are being pulled to the origin. They do not. The formula is the reason. The x coordinate is 2 over the quantity 2 plus lambda, so half the distance from 1 to the new x is closed when 2 over the quantity 2 plus lambda equals one half, which means 2 plus lambda equals 4, so lambda equals 2. You already computed that point: x equals 0.5, halfway from 1 to 0 in the sense of the coordinate itself. The y coordinate at lambda equals 2 is negative 0.3, and the old y was negative 0.4, so you have only moved from negative 0.4 to negative 0.3, a quarter of the way from the old value toward zero, not halfway. y gets halfway to zero when negative 2.4 over the quantity 6 plus lambda equals negative 0.2, so 2.4 over the quantity 6 plus lambda equals 0.2, so 6 plus lambda equals 2.4 over 0.2, which is 12, so lambda equals 6. At that same lambda, x is already 0.25, which is three quarters of the way from 1 to 0. The y curvature of the original bowl was three times the x curvature. The stiffer direction resists the penalty longer. Bias is not a uniform shrink of the vector. It is the solution of two different linear equations.

What the claim does not mean, on this slide, is a list you should be able to say back. It does not mean the new point is the old point rescaled by a constant you can pull out of both coordinates. At lambda equals 2 the x coordinate went from 1 to 0.5, a factor of one half, and the y coordinate went from negative 0.4 to negative 0.3, a factor of three quarters. Two factors, one lambda. It does not mean you have lowered the original loss. You raised it, from 0 to 0.28 at lambda equals 2, and from 0 to about 0.521 at lambda equals 4. It does not mean a late iterate that is still creeping is a wrong formula. It does not mean the origin is the answer at lambda equals 100. The answer there is about 0.0196 and negative 0.0226. Close is not the same sentence as equal, and the formula will give you the difference for any finite lambda you are willing to plug in.

The idea in this block is that an L1 penalty is a different geometry, and that the slide you just computed is not a soft shrink under another name. An L1 penalty has a subgradient, a kink at zero, and coordinates that can become exactly zero. This lab does not show L1. Weight decay, in the sense of the quadratic penalty on this board, and a map that snaps a coordinate to exactly zero, are different maps. If you describe one with the vocabulary of the other, you will report a zero that the formula cannot produce, or a smooth slide that a kink does not make.

Stay with the formula you can solve, and read what it refuses to do. For any finite lambda, x equals 2 divided by the quantity 2 plus lambda, which is positive, and y equals negative 2.4 divided by the quantity 6 plus lambda, which is negative. Neither coordinate is zero. Raise lambda from 18 to 30. At lambda equals 30, x equals 2 over 32, which is 0.0625, and y equals negative 2.4 over 36, which is negative 0.0667. Both coordinates are small. Neither is zero. The original bowl at that point is the square of 0.0625 minus 1, plus 3 times the square of negative 0.0667 plus 0.4. The x error is negative 0.9375, and its square is about 0.8789. The y error is about 0.3333, and its square is about 0.1111, times 3 is about 0.3333. The bowl is about 1.212. You have nearly returned to the origin's bowl value of 1.48, and you still have not set a coordinate to zero. A quadratic penalty spends its effort on shrinking. Exact zeros are a different purchase, and this objective does not sell them.

The kink is the geometric reason, said without pretending the lab ran an L1 solver. A squared penalty is smooth at the origin. Its derivative contribution is lambda times the coordinate, which is zero when the coordinate is zero, so the origin does not exert a pull unless you are away from it, and the pull then grows with distance. A penalty built from absolute values has a corner at zero. The subgradient at that corner is a set of slopes rather than a single slope, and zero can be a stationary coordinate when the unpenalized partial at zero sits inside that set. That is how a coordinate becomes exactly zero and stays there. This lesson names that fact so you do not confuse it with the slide. It does not give you the lab path, the threshold formula, or a numeric L1 solution to memorize. If you need a number, the number you are allowed to compute is the ridge fixed point. If you need an exact zero, you are in a different objective, and you should say so before you quote this board.

Bias, named, is the comparison between the two fixed points, not a comment on the printer. The unpenalized minimizer is 1 and negative 0.4. The penalized minimizer is 2 over the quantity 2 plus lambda, and negative 2.4 over the quantity 6 plus lambda. The difference is the bias relative to the unpenalized minimizer. At lambda equals 2 the difference in x is 1 minus 0.5, which is 0.5, and the difference in y is negative 0.4 minus negative 0.3, which is negative 0.1. At lambda equals 10 the difference in x is 1 minus one sixth, which is five sixths, and the difference in y is negative 0.4 minus negative 0.15, which is negative 0.25. The bias grows as lambda grows. That can lower risk. It does not lower training loss. You already measured the training loss. At lambda equals 2 the original bowl rose from 0 to 0.28. At lambda equals 4 it rose to about 0.521. At lambda equals 10 it is the square of one sixth minus 1, plus 3 times the square of negative 0.15 plus 0.4. The x piece is the square of negative five sixths, which is 25 over 36, about 0.6944. The y error is 0.25, its square is 0.0625, times 3 is 0.1875. The bowl is about 0.8819. Training loss went 0, then 0.28, then 0.521, then 0.882 as lambda went 0, then 2, then 4, then 10. The direction is up. Anyone who tells you the penalty improved the training loss is reading a different column.

The column they might be reading is the penalized objective, and you should be able to separate it out loud. At lambda equals 10 the penalty term is 10 over 2 times the sum of the square of one sixth and the square of 0.15. The square of one sixth is one over 36, about 0.02778. The square of 0.15 is 0.0225. The sum is about 0.05028. Times 5 is about 0.2514. The penalized value is the bowl about 0.8819 plus about 0.2514, about 1.133. At the old minimizer the penalized value would be lambda over 2 times 1.16, which at lambda equals 10 is 5 times 1.16, which is 5.8, plus a bowl of 0, so 5.8. The new point wins on the penalized objective, 1.133 against 5.8, and loses on the original bowl, 0.882 against 0. Report both, or the win hides the loss. Risk is neither of those two numbers unless your scoring distribution happens to make it so, and nothing in the lab estimates that expectation. The sentence "that can lower risk" is permission to go compute a risk, not a receipt that you already lowered it.

You might ask whether a coordinate can hit exactly zero if the step is coarse, even though the fixed point does not. A finite number of gradient steps can land near zero, and a sloppy printout can round a small coordinate to zero. Neither event is the fixed point. Take lambda equals 2 and the exact point 0.5 and negative 0.3. A step that has not converged might pass through y equals negative 0.299 or y equals negative 0.301. Those are not zeros. Take a much larger lambda, 100, where y is about negative 0.0226. Printing one digit after the decimal would show 0.0 and would be a display choice, not a stationarity result. The equation y times the quantity 6 plus lambda equals negative 2.4 has the solution negative 2.4 over 106, and 106 times that solution is negative 2.4, not zero. If your logs show exact zeros in both coordinates for a moderate lambda, you are not looking at this penalty. You may be looking at a thresholding map, or at a bug, or at a format string. Those are different maps, and the fix is different.

What the claim does not mean, for the whole lesson, is the last distinction to keep. It does not mean the quadratic penalty and an L1 penalty are two implementations of one shrink. One of them leaves a smooth fixed point at 2 over the quantity 2 plus lambda and negative 2.4 over the quantity 6 plus lambda. The other can place a coordinate at exactly zero and is not the update this lab runs. It does not mean bias is a defect you should always drive back to zero by taking lambda to 0. Lambda equals 0 is available, and it returns the unpenalized minimizer, and the lesson's point is that any other lambda is a chosen target, not a numerical convenience wrapped around the same target. It does not mean the step-size story from the unpenalized bowl transfers without editing. The stiff curvature becomes 6 plus lambda, and the safe step drops from one third at lambda equals 0 to one quarter at lambda equals 2, and to 2 over 16, which is one eighth, at lambda equals 10. A step you liked before the penalty can be an unstable step after it. It does not mean a path that fails to match the fixed point has a unique cause. If the late iterates are still moving, you may simply not have arrived. If they have settled somewhere else, the penalty may be missing from the gradient, or the step may be past the new ceiling and only looks settled because you stopped looking. Those are different bugs. Match the last iterate to the fixed point before you leave the board. If it matches, you minimized the objective you wrote down. You did not, by that match, minimize the original bowl, and you did not, by that match, produce an exact zero.

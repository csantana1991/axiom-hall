The idea in this block is a hedge, and then a financing constraint. Hold one option and short the S derivative of the option, that many shares. The dW terms cancel. The hedged portfolio is instantaneously riskless, so it must earn the financing rate r, or there is an arbitrage of the static style under the model’s assumptions. You replace mu with r in the dt coefficient and rearrange. Your forecast of the drift is irrelevant inside this argument. The forecast still drives the Monte Carlo skeletons under the physical measure. It does not drive the price that falls out of the hedge.

Name the book before you differentiate it. Let f be the price of the option, a function of time and of S. Let f sub S be the partial with respect to S. The hedged book is one option and a short position of f sub S shares. In words, Pi equals f minus f sub S times S. Over an instant you treat the share holding as frozen, which is the usual instantaneous reading of this hedge, and you apply the differential only to f and to S. The change in the book is df minus f sub S times dS.

Use the formula from the previous lecture on f, and use the geometric Brownian motion on S. The differential of f is the quantity f sub t plus mu S f sub S plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS, times dt, plus sigma S f sub S dW. The differential of S is mu S dt plus sigma S dW. Multiply the second by f sub S and subtract. The dW piece of df is sigma S f sub S dW. The dW piece you subtract is f sub S times sigma S dW. They are the same term. The difference has no dW. The mu S f sub S dt piece inside df is canceled by the mu S dt piece you subtract. What remains of d Pi is the quantity f sub t plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS, times dt. The mu is gone. The noise is gone. The book moved by a pure dt term. That is what instantaneously riskless means in this model. It does not mean the option was riskless. It means the net book, option against the shares, shed its dW over the instant.

A riskless book must earn the financing rate, or the model admits an arbitrage that does not depend on a forecast. The financing statement is that d Pi equals r times Pi times dt. Pi is f minus f sub S times S, so the right-hand side is r times the quantity f minus f sub S S, times dt. Set the two expressions for the dt coefficient equal. Then f sub t plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS equals r f minus r S f sub S. Move every term to one side. You get f sub t plus r S f sub S plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS minus r f equals 0. Look at where r entered and where mu did not. The r in front of S f sub S is the financing of the share position, brought across from the other side. The r in front of f is the financing of the option premium. The one half sigma squared term is the Itô correction, inherited from the previous lecture, untouched by the financing. Mu was canceled by the hedge before financing was imposed. Replacing mu by r is a description of the end result: the dt coefficient that used to contain mu S f sub S now contains r S f sub S, and an extra minus r f sits in the equation because the premium itself is financed. The mechanism was cancellation, then a financing identity, not a belief that the stock’s drift became r.

The assumptions are part of the claim, and this lesson states them narrowly. The equality is required or there is a static-style arbitrage under the model’s assumptions. The lab pays no dividend. Dividends change the financing. If the share threw off cash, the dt term of the short stock would include that cash, and the equation you just wrote would be the wrong equation. Do not add a dividend yield that the lab does not have, and do not drop the assumption when you leave the lab for a name that pays one. The argument also used a share holding equal to f sub S, frozen over the instant, and a diffusion of the geometric Brownian motion type so that the dW coefficients matched. A different diffusion would still cancel if you short the right number of shares, and the quadratic term would carry that diffusion’s b squared. This lecture keeps b equal to sigma S.

You might ask where the cash account is, because a book that is long an option and short shares has a cash residual that the symbol Pi compresses. Pi is that residual. If Pi is positive, you are holding a positive amount of value in the hedged book, and the financing constraint says that value must grow at r. If Pi is negative, you are borrowing, and the same equation charges you r. Nothing in the derivation required Pi to be positive. The algebra is the same with the sign. What the derivation required is that the rate on that residual is r, the same r you will use in the discount factor of the closed form. If your financing rate and your discount rate are two different numbers, you are no longer inside this argument.

What the claim does not mean should kill the most natural misreading. It does not mean the stock’s expected return is r. Mu is still the drift in the physical skeleton. The lab will let you move mu and watch the paths fan up or down. The hedge did not edit those paths. It removed mu from the price equation by canceling it between the option and the shares. It does not mean the option has no risk to an unhedged holder. The dW coefficient of f, sigma S f sub S, is still there if you do not short the shares. The cancellation is a property of Pi, not of f. It does not mean any forecast is equally good for trading the unhedged option. The lecture’s sentence is narrower. Inside this hedge argument, the forecast is irrelevant to the price. Outside the hedge, the previous lecture still says the mean of S grows with mu. Both sentences stand. It does not mean the derivation survived a dividend, a jump, or a rate that is not the r in the cash account. Those are different models. The equation you earned is the one with r in the two financing spots and with mu absent.

Change only the verbal slot that people fill with mu, and watch the algebra reject it. Suppose someone keeps the canceled equation, f sub t plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS, and sets it equal to mu times Pi instead of r times Pi, on the grounds that the stock earns mu so the book should too. The book does not hold the stock’s risk. That was the point of canceling dW. A riskless residual earns the riskless financing rate. If you nevertheless write mu, you get f sub t plus mu S f sub S plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS minus mu f equals 0, which is the Itô drift of f set equal to mu f. That equation still contains mu, and it is not the equation the financing argument produced. At a numerical level you will see the damage in the next blocks: the closed form built with r equal to 0.05 and the same closed form built with a drift of 0.10 in the r slot are different prices, about 10.45 against about 13.27 on the at-the-money case defined there. Using mu as if it were a financing rate is not a philosophical variant. It is a different number. The hedge argument picks r.

The idea in this block is the partial differential equation itself, read as a financing identity and not as a forecast. The equation is the time partial of f, plus r S times the first S partial, plus one half sigma squared S squared times the second S partial, minus r times f, equals 0. The terminal condition for a call is f at T and S equals the maximum of S minus K and 0. Mu does not appear. A forecast of the drift does not enter this equation, and moving a forecast slider must not move the price that solves it.

Read each term as a job. The time partial is the explicit clock inside the option price, the part that would change even if S were held fixed. The term r S times the first S partial is the financing adjustment on the share hedge, the piece that replaced mu S times the first S partial. The term one half sigma squared S squared times the second S partial is the Itô correction, the same curvature term that produced minus one half sigma squared in the log. The term minus r f is the financing of the option premium. Set their sum to zero and you have said that the hedged book earns r and nothing more. If your written equation is missing the minus r f, you have priced an object that is not charged for the premium. If it still contains mu, you have undone the cancellation from the previous block.

The terminal condition is not a detail you add after the price looks reasonable. It is the other half of the problem. At expiry the call pays the maximum of S minus K and 0, and it pays it once, with no remaining optionality. Any solution of the differential equation that ends somewhere else is a solution for a different contract. A contract that pays S at expiry, rather than the maximum of S minus K and 0, solves the same differential equation with a different terminal condition, and its value is not the call value. On this model that pure claim is worth S, if there is no dividend, because holding the share itself replicates a payoff of S and the share price is S. The call pays less than S whenever S is below K, and pays S minus K when S is above K. Its value lies below the share price. The equation does not by itself know this. The terminal condition tells it.

Mu’s absence is worth a second pass, because the slider in the lab is built to test it. The physical skeleton is still dS equals mu S dt plus sigma S dW. The paths fan up when mu rises and fan down when mu falls. The mean of S at a horizon t is still S at 0 times e to the mu t, from the previous lecture. None of that was revoked. What was revoked is the right of mu to appear in the hedged price. The derivation canceled mu between df and the short shares before r was imposed. There is nowhere left for a forecast to sit. If you move mu and the call price moves, the lab is wrong. That is the exercise. The bug, if it appears, is a wiring bug, not a subtlety of the Wiener process. A correct implementation can show a violent change in the fan and a call price that does not change in the fourth digit.

Dividends are the nearby modification the lecture bothers to name, so you do not learn the equation as a law of nature. The lab pays none. If the underlying paid a continuous dividend, the cash thrown off by the short shares would alter the dt coefficient of the hedge, and r in front of S f sub S would not be the whole story. This lecture does not write the dividend equation, and you should not pretend a verbal “plus a yield” fixes the formula without re-deriving the financing. The honest statement in the lab is the equation as written, with no dividend term. When you leave the lab, the absence of a dividend is a hypothesis you have to restate.

The same narrowness applies to the terminal contract. The maximum of S minus K and 0 is a call. A put would be the maximum of K minus S and 0. The lab only prices the call. Put-call parity, with no dividend, says the call minus the put equals S minus K times e to the minus r T. That identity is the test you will use if you add a put later, not another copy of the same bug. If both your call and your put are wrong by the same additive amount, their difference can still match the parity value, so parity is not a complete test. If their difference fails to match S minus K e to the minus r T, at least one of them is wrong. The lecture gives you the identity so the test is available. It does not ask the lab to display the put.

You might ask whether the equation determines a unique price, or whether many functions satisfy it. With the terminal condition, and with the usual growth restrictions this lecture does not spell out as a separate theorem, the closed form in the next block is the solution the course wants you to use. Two different functions cannot both be the call unless they agree. If a numerical scheme and the closed form disagree, one of them is not solving this problem. The usual failures are a terminal condition applied at the wrong time, a discount rate that is not r, a volatility that entered as variance in one place and as standard deviation in another, and a mu that was allowed into the drift of the price. The equation itself is a short sentence. The closed form is the solution of that sentence for the call. You should be able to point at a term in the equation and at the corresponding habit in the closed form. The r in front of S f sub S becomes the carry inside d1. The minus r f becomes the discount on the strike. The one half sigma squared term becomes the one half sigma squared inside d1 and the gap between d1 and d2. The next block computes those pieces. This block’s job is to refuse any solution procedure that still asks for mu.

What the claim does not mean is a list of neighboring models you have not solved. It does not mean every derivative contract satisfies this equation regardless of payoff. Change the terminal condition and you change the contract. It does not mean the equation prices the stock’s drift. The stock’s mean path can be anything mu says, and the call price does not consult it. It does not mean a low price from the equation is a bargain. The equation is a hedging price under the stated assumptions. A market price that differs from it is a different object, and this lecture does not give you a trading rule that converts the difference into a position. It does not mean the fan of paths is irrelevant to a person who cares about the physical distribution of S. The fan is the previous lecture. The equation is this one. Keeping them in different sentences is the discipline. It does not mean you may drop the curvature term if the option is far from the strike and “almost linear.” Far from the strike the second derivative of the call price becomes small, and the term shrinks for that reason, which you can see later when a deep in-the-money call approaches S minus the discounted strike. You do not get to drop the term at the start of the derivation because you hope you are far from the strike. The derivation did not assume a region. The curvature term is in the equation everywhere. It is merely small in places, after you have solved it.

Change one coefficient in the equation and recompute the identity, so r is not a decoration. Suppose the financing rate on the premium and on the hedge were 0. The equation would say the time partial plus one half sigma squared S squared times the second partial equals 0, because every term that carried r drops. That is a different problem. Its call price is not the r equal to 0.05 call price. You will compute both in the closed form: with S and K equal to 100, sigma equal to 0.2, and T equal to 1, the zero-rate call is about 7.966, and the five-percent call is about 10.451. The differential equation notices the 0.05. A lab that does not is solving the zero-rate equation by accident. Conversely, leaving mu equal to 0.10 inside the r slots produces the price associated with r equal to 0.10, about 13.270 on those same inputs, which is the wrong financing. The equation’s silence on mu is not vagueness. It is a specific omission, next to a specific presence of r, sigma, S, and the terminal maximum of S minus K and 0.

The idea in this block is the closed form. The call price is S times Phi of d1, minus K times e to the minus r T, times Phi of d2. d2 equals d1 minus sigma times the square root of T. d1 carries the log moneyness, the carry, and half the variance. In words, d1 equals the quantity log of S over K, plus the quantity r plus one half sigma squared, times T, all divided by sigma times the square root of T. Phi is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The lab uses a standard normal approximation. It is a desk calculator, not a special-function library. Move mu. The call must sit still. Move sigma or r, and it should move.

Build d1 on the base inputs before you touch Phi. Let S equal 100, K equal 100, r equal 0.05, sigma equal 0.2, and T equal 1. The log of S over K is the log of 1, which is 0. One half sigma squared is one half of 0.04, which is 0.02. r plus that half variance is 0.05 plus 0.02, which is 0.07. Times T, which is 1, the numerator is 0 plus 0.07, which is 0.07. The denominator is 0.2 times the square root of 1, which is 0.2. d1 equals 0.07 divided by 0.2, which is 0.35. d2 equals 0.35 minus 0.2, which is 0.15. The gap between them is the total volatility, sigma times the square root of T, and on a one-year option with sigma 0.2 that gap is exactly 0.2. If your d2 is 0.35 minus 0.04, you subtracted the variance instead of the standard deviation over the life, and every later digit is wrong.

Phi of 0.35 is about 0.636831. Phi of 0.15 is about 0.559618. Those are values of the standard normal cumulative, the kind of approximation the lab is allowed to use, and you should treat them as computed values rather than as rounded folklore. The discount factor e to the minus r T is e to the minus 0.05, about 0.951229. The discounted strike is 100 times 0.951229, about 95.1229. The call is 63.6831 minus 53.2325, about 10.451. More digits from the same approximation give about 10.4506. A physical drift of 0.10 would have contributed mu T equal to 0.20 if someone forced it into the r slot, and the numerator would have been 0.20 plus 0.04, which is 0.24, and d1 would have been 0.24 over 0.28284, about 0.848, a different number and a different price. Leaving mu at 0.10 in the path generator and out of d1 leaves d1 at about 0.495 and the call at about 16.127. The two-year mean of S, from the previous lecture, would be 100 times e to the mu times 2. At mu equal to 0.10 that is 100 times e to the 0.20, about 122.14. The call does not become a discount of 122 minus something. It stays 16.127. The longer horizon makes the temptation worse, because the physical mean and the spot diverge more, and the formula still ignores the divergence.

Now an out-of-the-money spot, so Phi is not stuck near one half and the price is not a two-digit habit. Let S equal 70, K equal 100, r equal 0.05, sigma equal 0.2, and T equal 1. The log of 70 over 100 is the log of 0.7, about negative 0.356675. The carry piece is still 0.07. The numerator is about negative 0.286675. Divided by 0.2, d1 is about negative 1.4334. d2 is about negative 1.6334. Phi of negative 1.4334 is about 0.07588. Phi of negative 1.6334 is about 0.05120. Both tails are thin. The first term is 70 times 0.07588, about 5.311. The discounted strike is still about 95.1229, times 0.05120 is about 4.870. The call is about 0.441. The forward intrinsic value is 70 minus 95.123, about negative 25.123, and the call does not follow it below zero. A call is at least zero because the terminal maximum of S minus K and 0 is at least zero, and the closed form respects that. Parity then puts the put at the call minus the forward intrinsic value, which is 0.441 minus negative 25.123, about 25.564. The put is the valuable contract when the spot is 70 and the strike is 100. The lab does not display it. Parity does, once you trust the call. If a later put solver says 10 on these inputs, it has failed the test by about 15 points, and you do not need a new theory to see the failure. The difference should be S minus the discounted strike, and it is not.

Change only the rate by one percentage point, from 0.05 to 0.06, on the original at-the-money one-year contract, so a small financing edit has a small, fully visible price edit. Sigma is 0.2, S and K are 100, T is 1. One half sigma squared is 0.02. r plus that is 0.08. d1 equals 0.08 over 0.2, which is 0.4. d2 equals 0.2. Phi of 0.4 is about 0.655422. Phi of 0.2 is about 0.579260. The discount factor is e to the minus 0.06, about 0.941765. The first term is about 65.542. The second term is about 94.1765 times 0.579260, about 54.553. The call is about 10.990. The move from r equal to 0.05 to r equal to 0.06 raised the call from about 10.451 to about 10.990, a difference of about 0.54 points for one percentage point of rate, on this contract. That difference is not a constant you may carry to the two-year option or to the spot of 70. It is one subtraction of two prices. The equation said r would enter. The closed form shows the size, here, of about half a point. Mu moving by the same one percentage point, from wherever it sat in the path generator, moves the call by zero.

One check remains on the two-year d2, because the subtraction is where total volatility is lost. d1 was about 0.49497 and the square root of T times sigma was about 0.28284. Subtract: 0.49497 minus 0.28284 equals 0.21213, which matches the d2 that went into Phi. If the subtraction is skipped, Phi of d1 gets used twice, and the call becomes 68.969 minus 90.4837 times 0.689691. That second product is about 62.406, and the fake call is about 6.563, far from 16.127, and far from the forward intrinsic value in the wrong direction relative to a careless story. Using one Phi for both terms is not a conservative approximation. It is a different formula. d2 is d1 minus sigma times the square root of T every time, including when that gap is 0.2 on the one-year base case and when it is about 0.283 on the two-year case.

The numbers you should be able to recompute without the script are the base call, the effect of mu, and one edit. Base: d1 0.35, d2 0.15, call about 10.45, with S and K at 100, r at 0.05, sigma at 0.2, T at 1. Mu from 0.1 to 0.3: paths and the mean of S move, the call stays.

The idea in this block is a Taylor expansion in which you keep the term ordinary calculus drops. For a function f of time and of a price S, the second derivative in S, multiplied by the square of dS, does not vanish when S has a diffusion. If dS equals mu S dt plus sigma S dW, then the square of dS equals sigma squared S squared dt, once you use the rule that the square of dW equals dt and you drop what is of higher order. You substitute that square back into the expansion and you collect terms in dt and in dW. The multiplication table is the whole license for the dropping. It is a calculation rule for integrals, not a claim that a derivative of W exists.

Write the table before you use it, in words, because the notation is easy to misuse. The product dt times dt is 0. The product dt times dW is 0. The product dW times dW is dt. Nothing in that table says W has a time derivative. The previous lecture forbade that derivative, and this table does not sneak it back in. The table tells you which products of increments survive when the mesh goes to zero inside an integral, given that the sum of squared Wiener increments tends to the length of the interval. You use the table inside a second-order expansion, and then you drop what the table kills. If a term contains dt times dW, it goes. If a term contains dt squared, it goes. If a term contains dW squared, it becomes a dt, and it stays.

Start from the ordinary second-order expansion so the new piece has a place to sit. For a smooth function of time and S, the change in f is the time partial times dt, plus the S partial times dS, plus one half the second S partial times the square of dS, plus higher-order remainders. I am suppressing the mixed partial and the pure second time partial in that sentence because the table will kill them, and I want their death to be a calculation rather than a shrug. The mixed partial produces a product dt times dS. The pure second time partial produces dt squared. Expand dS itself as mu S dt plus sigma S dW. Then dt times dS contains dt squared and dt times dW. Both of those are 0 under the table. So the mixed partial does not survive. The second time partial does not survive. What can survive, besides the first-order pieces, is one half the second S partial times the square of dS.

Now square dS with the table in your hand. dS equals mu S dt plus sigma S dW. The square expands into three pieces: the square of mu S dt, the cross term 2 mu S sigma S dt dW, and the square of sigma S dW. The first piece has dt squared in it and dies. The cross term has dt times dW in it and dies. The last piece is sigma squared S squared times dW squared, and dW squared becomes dt. So the square of dS equals sigma squared S squared dt, in the sense of this calculation rule. The drift mu entered the square and then left, because every place it entered was a higher-order product. The diffusion coefficient is the one that sets the quadratic term. For geometric Brownian motion the diffusion coefficient b, in the form dS equals a dt plus b dW, is sigma S, and b squared is sigma squared S squared. The same sentence in the general diffusion is that the square of dS equals b squared dt. Geometric Brownian motion is the case a equals mu S and b equals sigma S.

A numerical step shows what was dropped, so the phrase higher order is a size and not a slogan. Let S equal 100, mu equal 0.1, sigma equal 0.2, and dt equal 0.01. Let Z equal 1, so dW equals the square root of 0.01 times 1, which is 0.1. The drift piece mu S dt is 0.1 times 100 times 0.01, which is 0.1. The diffusion piece sigma S dW is 0.2 times 100 times 0.1, which is 2. So dS equals 2.1, and the raw square of dS is 4.41. The rule says the square of dS should be replaced by sigma squared S squared dt, which is 0.04 times 10,000 times 0.01, which is 4. The difference between 4.41 and 4 is 0.41, and that difference is what the table tells you to drop. Where did 0.41 come from? The drift piece 0.1 squared is 0.01. The cross term 2 times 0.1 times 2 is 0.4. Together they are 0.41. They are real on this finite step. They are the terms the limit will kill as dt shrinks, relative to the dt that you keep. The piece you keep, 4, is sigma squared S squared times dt, and it is the same order as dt. The piece you drop is smaller order in the limit, even though on a width of 0.01 the number 0.41 is not tiny next to 4. A width of 0.01 is a coarse illustration, not the limit. The rule is the limit’s rule. You should see the dropped piece once, so you know what you are discarding, and then you should discard it when you invoke the formula.

Shrink the same step and watch the dropped fraction fall. Change only dt, from 0.01 to 0.0001, and keep Z equal to 1. Then dW equals 0.01. The drift piece is 0.1 times 100 times 0.0001, which is 0.001. The diffusion piece is 0.2 times 100 times 0.01, which is 0.2. So dS equals 0.201, and the raw square is 0.040401. The rule’s replacement is sigma squared S squared dt, which is 0.04 times 10,000 times 0.0001, which is 0.04. The absolute gap is 0.000401. Relative to the kept term 0.04, the gap is about one percent, whereas on the width 0.01 the gap 0.41 relative to 4 was about ten percent. Refining the step shrinks the relative size of what the table drops. That is the sense in which the replacement becomes accurate. It is not accurate because 4.41 and 4 were close on the coarse step. They were only roughly close. It is accurate in the limit the quadratic variation lecture already prepared.

Put the kept square back into the second-order piece. One half times the second S partial, times the square of dS, becomes one half times the second S partial, times sigma squared S squared, times dt. That term is a dt term. It joins the other dt terms. It is not a curiosity off to the side of the drift. It is part of the drift of f. If your expansion stops at the first derivative, you have omitted a dt term of exactly the order you intended to keep. The omission is not a small numerical leak on a fine grid. It is a wrong coefficient in the limit.

You might ask whether the table could have kept dt times dW, since a sum of those products might conceivably accumulate. The rule used here sets that product to 0, and the justification is the same mesh limit that sets dt squared to 0 and dW squared to dt, not a separate aesthetic. On the numerical step, dt times dW is 0.01 times 0.1, which is 0.001, while dW squared is 0.01. The product dt dW is an order smaller than the dW squared term you are promoting into a dt. On the finer step, dt times dW is 0.0001 times 0.01, which is 10 to the minus 6, while dW squared is 0.0001. Again the cross time-noise product is negligible next to the square you keep. If a derivation you are reading retains dt times dW as a dt term, it is using a different multiplication rule from the one in this lesson. This lesson’s table is dt times dt equals 0, dt times dW equals 0, and dW times dW equals dt.

What the claim does not mean is as important as the table, because the table looks like algebra and people treat it like algebra that applies to every product in sight. It does not mean W is differentiable and dW over dt is an ordinary random function you may cancel. The previous lecture’s difference quotients blew up as the mesh shrank. This table is a substitute for that derivative, not a computation of it. It does not mean every square of every process equals its variance. The replacement of the square of dS by sigma squared S squared dt used the diffusion coefficient of this S, and it used the table on dW. A process with a different diffusion coefficient keeps a different quadratic term. If the diffusion coefficient were 0, the square of dS would be killed entirely and ordinary calculus would return. The second-order term survives because there is a diffusion, not because Taylor series always keep second derivatives in time. It does not mean the finite-step square 4.41 was illegal. It was the square of a real increment on a real grid. The formula replaces it by 4 when you pass to the limiting integral. Inside a coarse simulation you still have 4.41 if you square the realized increment. Do not “correct” a simulated increment by editing its square down to the limit expression. The limit expression is for the calculus, not a post-processing step on a single draw.

Change one input, the normal draw, and recompute the dropped piece so the drift’s absence from the quadratic term is checked rather than trusted. Keep S equal to 100, mu equal 0.1, sigma equal 0.2, and dt equal 0.01, and set Z equal to negative 0.5. Then dW equals negative 0.05. The drift piece is still 0.1. The diffusion piece is 0.2 times 100 times negative 0.05, which is negative 1. So dS equals negative 0.9. The raw square is 0.81. The rule's replacement is still sigma squared S squared dt, which is 0.04 times 10,000 times 0.01, which is 4, and it does not depend on Z. The raw square 0.81 is not close to 4. That is a useful shock. On a single step with a small Z, the realized square can be far from sigma squared S squared dt, because sigma squared S squared dt is the quadratic variation contribution, an order-dt object that a single draw estimates very noisily. The cross and drift pieces are 0.1 squared plus 2 times 0.1 times negative 1, which is 0.01 minus 0.2, which is negative 0.19, and  negative 1 squared is 1, and 1 plus negative 0.19 is 0.81. The table still tells you to replace the square by 4 inside the limiting calculus, not because 0.81 is near 4, but because sums of such squares concentrate, the way sums of squared Wiener increments concentrate on the clock. One step is allowed to look rude. The rule is about the accumulated integral. Hold that distinction when the formula in the next block writes one half sigma squared S squared times the second derivative as if it were a sure dt term. It is the sure term in the limit, not a claim about your last increment.

The idea in this block is the formula you will actually use. For geometric Brownian motion, the differential of f is the time partial, plus mu S times the S partial, plus one half sigma squared S squared times the second S partial, all of that times dt, plus sigma S times the S partial times dW. In words, df equals the quantity f sub t, plus mu S f sub S, plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS, times dt, plus sigma S f sub S dW. The drift of f is not the time partial plus mu S times the S partial. The second-derivative correction is the content of the course. If you remember a shorter drift, you have remembered ordinary calculus, and ordinary calculus is the wrong tool on this path.

Assemble it from the previous block rather than memorizing the parentheses first. The first-order contribution of dS is the S partial times dS, and dS is mu S dt plus sigma S dW, so this contribution splits into mu S times the S partial, times dt, and sigma S times the S partial, times dW. The time partial contributes its own dt. The second-order contribution that survived is one half the second S partial times sigma squared S squared dt. Add the dt coefficients. They are the time partial, the mu S times the first S partial, and the one half sigma squared S squared times the second S partial. Add the dW coefficient. It is sigma S times the first S partial. There is no second-derivative piece in front of dW. The second derivative joined the drift. That placement is the entire novelty.

The general diffusion statement is the same pattern with names for the coefficients. If dS equals a dt plus b dW, then df picks up one half b squared times the second S partial, times dt, on top of the first-order pieces f sub t dt plus f sub S times dS. For geometric Brownian motion, a is mu S and b is sigma S, so one half b squared is one half sigma squared S squared, and you recover the formula above. When you change models later, you change a and b and you keep the pattern. You do not re-derive the multiplication table unless the noise itself has changed. This course’s noise is a Wiener process, and the table from the previous block stays.

Read the drift of f as a function you could code, so the correction cannot hide inside notation. The drift coefficient is f sub t plus mu S f sub S plus one half sigma squared S squared f sub SS. The diffusion coefficient of f is sigma S f sub S. Both coefficients can depend on time and on S, through f and through the coefficients of S. Nothing in the formula requires f to be the identity function. If f is the identity, f of S equals S, then the time partial is 0, the first S partial is 1, and the second S partial is 0. The drift coefficient collapses to mu S, and the diffusion coefficient collapses to sigma S. You get dS back. That check is cheap and you should do it every time you write a general formula, because a general formula that cannot reproduce the process you started from has a partial wrong.

Do a second check on a function whose second derivative is not zero, but whose answer you can see without stochastic calculus, so the correction is forced to earn its place. Let f equal S squared. Then the time partial is 0, the first S partial is 2 S, and the second S partial is 2. The drift coefficient is mu S times 2 S, plus one half sigma squared S squared times 2. That is 2 mu S squared, plus sigma squared S squared. The diffusion coefficient is sigma S times 2 S, which is 2 sigma S squared. So the formula says the differential of S squared equals S squared times the quantity 2 mu plus sigma squared, times dt, plus 2 sigma S squared dW. Notice the sigma squared inside the drift. Ordinary calculus, which keeps only mu S times 2 S, would have reported 2 mu S squared and would have missed sigma squared S squared. The miss is not a change of notation. It is a different drift. At S equal to 100 and sigma equal to 0.2, the missed term sigma squared S squared is 0.04 times 10,000, which is 400. The kept ordinary term 2 mu S squared, at mu equal to 0.1, is 0.2 times 10,000, which is 2,000. The correction 400 is not a rounding error next to 2,000. It is a fifth of the ordinary piece, at these parameters, and it is present for every hour the process runs, in the drift coefficient, not only on volatile days. Days do not enter. The dt coefficient is a rate.

You might ask where the one half went in that square example, because the second derivative is 2 and one half times 2 is 1, so the one half disappeared into the arithmetic and the correction looked like a full sigma squared S squared. The one half is still there. One half times sigma squared S squared times the second derivative 2 equals sigma squared S squared. If the second derivative had been 4, the correction would have been 2 sigma squared S squared, and the one half would have remained visible as the factor that turns 4 into 2. The one half is the Taylor coefficient in front of the second-order term. It is not a volatility convention, and it is not optional. Dropping it would change the drift of S squared from 2 mu plus sigma squared into 2 mu plus 2 sigma squared, in units of S squared. At the numbers above, the correction inside the drift of S squared would become 800 instead of 400. That is a different process.

Change one input, sigma, and recompute that correction so the dependence on volatility is obvious. Keep S equal to 100 and mu equal to 0.1, and replace sigma equal to 0.2 by sigma equal to 0.4. Then sigma squared is 0.16, and sigma squared S squared is 0.16 times 10,000, which is 1,600. The ordinary piece 2 mu S squared is still 2,000, because mu and S did not change. The correction is now 1,600, which is almost as large as the ordinary piece. The drift coefficient of S squared, multiplied back by the structure we derived, is S squared times 2 mu plus sigma squared, and 2 mu plus sigma squared is 0.2 plus 0.16, which is 0.36, whereas at sigma equal to 0.2 it was 0.2 plus 0.04, which is 0.24. The diffusion coefficient 2 sigma S squared becomes 2 times 0.4 times 10,000, which is 8,000, against the old 2 times 0.2 times 10,000, which is 4,000. Doubling sigma doubled the diffusion coefficient of S squared and multiplied the quadratic correction by 4, because the correction carries sigma squared. If a sensitivity analysis of yours moves sigma and the drift of f does not move, you have lost the second-derivative term.

What the formula does not mean should be nailed to specific wrong sentences. It does not mean the drift of f is the time partial plus mu S times the first S partial. That shorter expression is the ordinary chain rule, and it is missing one half sigma squared S squared times the second derivative. On the square example the missing piece was 400 at sigma equal to 0.2 and S equal to 100, a rate in the drift of S squared, not a one-time adjustment. It does not mean mu is irrelevant to f. Mu is still in the drift, multiplied by S and by the first derivative. The quadratic correction is additional, not a replacement for mu. Later, in a hedging argument, mu will leave a pricing equation for a different reason. It has not left this formula. It does not mean the dW coefficient acquired a second derivative. The second derivative sits in the dt term. The dW term is the first derivative times the diffusion coefficient of S. If you are matching a simulated increment of f to the formula, the random piece should line up with sigma S times the first derivative times the realized dW, and the expected piece, over many draws, should line up with the full drift including the second derivative. Matching only the random piece can look convincing and still hide a wrong drift, because one path’s noise dominates a small dt. It does not mean the formula is a multiplication you may rearrange as if dW were a number with dW over dt finite. You may collect coefficients of dt and of dW. You may not divide the equation by dt and then take an ordinary derivative of W.

A third function, linear in S, is worth thirty seconds because it tells you when the correction is allowed to be zero. Let f equal 5 S plus 7. The time partial is 0, the first S partial is 5, the second S partial is 0. The drift coefficient is mu S times 5, and the diffusion coefficient is sigma S times 5. There is no quadratic correction, because there is no curvature. Affine functions of S obey the ordinary chain rule even when S is a diffusion. The surprise lives in curvature. If your f is a call payoff, the curvature is not zero, and the correction will not be zero, but the payoff itself is not a smooth function, so you apply the formula to the pricing function before expiry, where the smoothness holds under this model, rather than to the kink at expiry. This lecture’s lab is not that pricing function yet. The formula you are responsible for now is the differential, and the check you are responsible for now is the logarithm, which has curvature, a clean one, and a closed integral. That check is the next block. Before you turn to it, write the drift once more and underline, in the only way speech can underline, the term ordinary calculus does not have: one half sigma squared S squared times the second derivative with respect to S, sitting in the dt coefficient, beside the time partial and beside mu S times the first derivative.

The idea in this block is a check you can finish on paper. Apply the formula to the logarithm of S. The second derivative of the log is negative 1 over S squared, so the correction is negative one half sigma squared. You recover the statement that d log S equals the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times dt, plus sigma dW. The lab’s exponential step is this integral, not a guess. The mean of S at time t is S at 0 times e to the mu t, not S at 0 times e to the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t. The exponential of a normal is lognormal, and Jensen’s inequality sits between those two expressions. Individual paths wander. The call price is not the average of a few pictures.

Write every partial, as the notes insist. Let f equal log S, the natural logarithm. The time partial is 0, because the log of S does not depend on t except through S. The first S partial is 1 over S. The second S partial is negative 1 over S squared. Now multiply one half sigma squared S squared by the second partial, negative 1 over S squared. One half sigma squared S squared times negative 1 over S squared equals negative one half sigma squared. The S squared cancels. If an S survives, a partial is wrong. That cancellation is the check. Do it at a number so a leftover S cannot hide in the letters. Let S equal 100 and sigma equal 0.2. Then one half sigma squared S squared is one half of 0.04 times 10,000, which is 0.02 times 10,000, which is 200. The second partial is negative 1 over 10,000, which is negative 0.0001. The product is 200 times negative 0.0001, which is negative 0.02. And negative one half sigma squared is negative one half of 0.04, which is negative 0.02. Same number, no S left. Change S to 2, same sigma. One half sigma squared S squared is 0.02 times 4, which is 0.08. The second partial is negative 1 over 4, which is negative 0.25. The product is negative 0.02 again. Change sigma to 0.5 and S to 100. Sigma squared is 0.25, one half sigma squared is 0.125, one half sigma squared S squared is 0.125 times 10,000, which is 1,250. Times negative 1 over 10,000 is negative 0.125. The S canceled, and the correction moved because sigma moved. The cancellation is algebraic. The value of the correction is one half sigma squared, with a minus sign, and it does not depend on the price level.

The other pieces of the drift are short. The time partial is 0. Mu S times the first partial is mu S times 1 over S, which is mu. The S cancels there too. So the full drift coefficient of the log is mu plus the correction negative one half sigma squared. The diffusion coefficient is sigma S times the first partial 1 over S, which is sigma. Therefore d log S equals the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times dt, plus sigma dW. At mu equal to 0.1 and sigma equal to 0.2, the drift of the log is 0.1 minus 0.02, which is 0.08. Ordinary calculus would have said the drift of the log is mu, which is 0.1, because it would divide dS by S and forget the curvature. The difference is 0.02, and 0.02 is one half sigma squared. Over a year that is a shift of 0.02 in the log, which is not a cosmetic change in the exponent.

Integrate the differential. The right-hand side has constant coefficients if mu and sigma are constant. From time 0 to time t, the log of S at t minus the log of S at 0 equals the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, plus sigma times W at t, using W at 0 equal to 0. Exponentiate. S at t equals S at 0 times the exponential of the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, plus sigma times W at t. That is the exact solution. The lab’s exponential step on a grid is this formula on each step, with t replaced by the step width and W at t replaced by the Wiener increment over the step. It is not an Euler step on the level of S, and it is not a guess added to make a plot look random. If your code updates S by multiplying by 1 plus mu dt plus sigma times the square root of dt times Z, you have discretized the level. If your code multiplies S by the exponential of the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times dt, plus sigma times the square root of dt times Z, you have used the integral. Those are different updates. On a fine grid they are close. On a coarse grid they are not, and the exponential one matches the distribution of geometric Brownian motion at the grid times.

Put numbers on both updates for a coarse step, so the gap is not theoretical. Let S equal 100, mu equal 0.1, sigma equal 0.2, and let the step be a whole year, dt equal to 1, so the square root of dt is 1 and dW equals Z. The exact multiplier is the exponential of 0.08 plus 0.2 Z. The Euler multiplier is 1 plus 0.1 plus 0.2 Z, which is 1.1 plus 0.2 Z. If Z equals 0, the exact new level is 100 times the exponential of 0.08. The exponential of 0.08 is about 1.0833, so the level is about 108.33. The Euler level is 100 times 1.1, which is 110. If Z equals 1, the exact exponent is 0.08 plus 0.2, which is 0.28, and the exponential of 0.28 is about 1.3231, so the level is about 132.31. The Euler level is 100 times 1.3, which is 130. If Z equals negative 1, the exact exponent is 0.08 minus 0.2, which is negative 0.12, and the exponential of negative 0.12 is about 0.8869, so the level is about 88.69. The Euler level is 100 times 0.9, which is 90. The gaps are about 1.67, about 2.31, and about 1.31 on these three draws. A one-year Euler step is a blunt instrument. The lab is not required to be blunt, because the log integral is available in closed form. Use it.

The mean is the fact that makes the minus one half sigma squared feel paradoxical, so derive it instead of memorizing the paradox. S at t is S at 0 times the exponential of a normal random variable. The normal is the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, plus sigma times W at t. Its mean is the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, because W at t has mean 0. Its variance is sigma squared times t, because W at t has variance t. The exponential of a normal is lognormal, and the mean of the exponential of a normal with mean m and variance v is e to the m plus one half v. Here m is the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, and v is sigma squared t. So m plus one half v equals mu t minus one half sigma squared t plus one half sigma squared t, which is mu t. The one half sigma squared terms cancel. Therefore the expected value of S at t is S at 0 times e to the mu t. The correction that lowered the drift of the log is exactly undone, in the mean of the level, by the variance of the log. If you had forgotten the correction in the exponent and also forgotten to restore it through the lognormal mean, you might land on the right mean by a double error. The disciplined route is to keep the correction in the path and to watch it cancel in the expectation.

Jensen’s inequality is the sentence that sits between the two expressions. The exponential is a convex function. The expectation of the exponential is at least the exponential of the expectation. The expectation of the log of S at t is the log of S at 0 plus the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t. Exponentiate that, and you get S at 0 times e to the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t. That number is the exponential of the expected log. It is not the expected level. The expected level is larger, unless sigma is 0, in which case the two expressions agree because there is no variance and Jensen’s gap closes. Convexity of the exponential pushes the mean of S above the exponential of the mean of the log. The gap is not a simulation bias. It is the inequality, and for this lognormal law you even know both sides in closed form.

Compute both sides on the running example. S at 0 is 100, mu is 0.1, sigma is 0.2, t is 1. The expected level is 100 times e to the 0.1. The exponential of 0.1 is about 1.10517, so the mean is about 110.517. The exponential of the expected log is 100 times e to the 0.08, about 100 times 1.08329, about 108.329. The gap is about 2.188. The path formula spends most of its time, in the median sense associated with W at t equal to 0, near 108.329, while the mean is pulled up by the right tail to 110.517. Change only sigma, to 0.4. One half sigma squared is one half of 0.16, which is 0.08. The log drift is 0.1 minus 0.08, which is 0.02. The exponential of the expected log is 100 times e to the 0.02, about 102.020. The expected level is still 100 times e to the 0.1, about 110.517, because the mean does not depend on sigma. The gap widens from about 2.188 to about 8.497. More diffusion, same mean of S, lower median-type center, fatter right tail doing the lifting. If your simulated average level moves when you change sigma and you claim to be estimating the mean, you are seeing Monte Carlo noise or a bug, not a sigma dependence in the formula. The formula’s mean does not contain sigma.

Change only mu, to 0.2, and put sigma back to 0.2 and t back to 1. The expected level is 100 times e to the 0.2, and e to the 0.2 is about 1.22140, so the mean is about 122.140. The log drift is 0.2 minus 0.02, which is 0.18, and e to the 0.18 is about 1.19722, so the exponential of the expected log is about 119.722. The gap is about 2.419. Both expressions moved when mu moved. Only the mean expression is the expectation of S. Change only t, to 2, with mu 0.1 and sigma 0.2. The mean is 100 times e to the 0.2, the same number about 122.140, because mu t is 0.2 again. The log correction over two years is one half sigma squared times 2, which is 0.04, so the exponent of the expected log is 0.2 minus 0.04, which is 0.16. The exponential of 0.16 is about 1.17351, so that center is about 117.351. The mean is about 122.140. The ordering is forced by Jensen: 117.351 is smaller than 122.140. The lab’s closed-form mean is the higher one, S at 0 times e to the mu t.

A few pictures do not estimate that mean, and this is the place the lab’s warning becomes arithmetic. Take W at time 1 equal to negative 1, 0, and 1, with S at 0 equal to 100, mu 0.1, sigma 0.2. The three levels are about 88.692, about 108.329, and about 132.313. Their average is 88.692 plus 108.329 plus 132.313, which is 329.334, divided by 3, which is about 109.778. The true mean is about 110.517. Three equally spaced Wiener values are not the Gaussian distribution, and their average level is not the expectation. Add the draws W equal to negative 2 and W equal to 2. The level at W equal to 2 is 100 times the exponential of 0.08 plus 0.4, which is the exponential of 0.48. The exponential of 0.48 is about 1.6161, so the level is about 161.61. The level at W equal to negative 2 is 100 times the exponential of 0.08 minus 0.4, the exponential of negative 0.32, about 0.7261, so the level is about 72.61. Average all five pictures: 72.61, 88.692, 108.329, 132.313, and 161.61. The sum is about 563.554, divided by 5 is about 112.711, now above the mean. A symmetric set of W values does not produce a symmetric set of S values, because the exponential bends. The average of the pictures moves around the true mean as you add points, and it is not the true mean. The call price, likewise, is not the average of a few pictures. The lab says to compare the path fan with the closed-form mean. The fan wanders. The mean is  S at 0 times e to the mu t. Quote the mean from the formula. Do not quote it from three polylines.

You might ask why the mean of S grows at rate mu if every path’s log grows at rate mu minus one half sigma squared. The answer is the cancellation you already did, said in the other order. Paths that go up contribute more than linearly to the average, and paths that go down contribute less than linearly, with a floor at zero because the exponential is positive. The asymmetry adds exactly one half sigma squared t back into the exponent of the mean, canceling the subtraction inside the typical path. At our numbers, the typical-path exponent 0.08 and the variance adjustment one half of 0.04 together rebuild 0.10. There is no leftover and no extra term in mu. If your derivation of the mean still contains sigma at the end, an S failed to cancel or the lognormal half-variance was applied with the wrong sign. The target expression is short enough to police: S at 0 times e to the mu t.

What the claim does not mean needs a hard boundary, because this block sits next to a pricing lecture that will remove mu for a different reason. It does not mean mu is irrelevant to the path or to the mean. The mean is exactly the place mu appears as the growth rate of the expectation. Changing mu from 0.1 to 0.2 moved the mean from about 110.517 to about 122.140. It does not mean the log drift is a more correct mean. The log drift is the drift of a different process, the log. Reporting 108.329 as the expected price is the Jensen mistake in numerical form. It does not mean an Euler step on the level is always wrong enough to matter. On a small dt the two updates are close, and the exact exponential is still the one that matches the law at the grid time without a local truncation error. It does not mean a simulated fan that averages to 109.8 has disproved the mean 110.5. Three paths are not an expectation. It does not mean the call price should track the fan or the mean. You have not priced the call in this block. You have priced nothing. You have identified the distribution of S. The lab can show a call off to the side and tell you it must not be confused with the average of the pictures. Believe that instruction. The pictures are skeletons. Their average is a noisy functional of a few draws. The mean of S is a formula. The mean of a call payoff would be yet another formula, and it is not the next sentence of this derivation.

One last numerical case, with the starting level changed, so every symbol in the solution has been edited once. Let S at 0 equal 50, mu equal 0.05, sigma equal 0.3, and t equal 1. One half sigma squared is one half of 0.09, which is 0.045. The log drift is 0.05 minus 0.045, which is 0.005. The expected level is 50 times e to the 0.05. The exponential of 0.05 is about 1.05127, so the mean is about 52.564. The exponential of the expected log is 50 times e to the 0.005. The exponential of 0.005 is about 1.00501, so that center is about 50.251. The gap is about 2.313, on a starting level of 50. Check the partial cancellation at this new sigma and a new S, say S equal to 50. One half sigma squared S squared is 0.045 times 2,500, which is 112.5. Times the second derivative negative 1 over 2,500, which is negative 0.0004, the product is 112.5 times negative 0.0004, which is negative 0.045. Again no S remains, and the correction equals negative one half sigma squared. The solution formula does not care that the start was 50 rather than 100. S at t equals 50 times the exponential of 0.005 t plus 0.3 times W at t, at general t, and at t equal to 1 with W equal to 0 the level is about 50.251, while the mean is about 52.564. Carry both numbers, and the reason they differ, into the pricing lecture. The path has a log drift with a minus one half sigma squared. The expectation of the level does not. A hedge that replaces mu will be a third object. Do not collapse the three into one exponent.


Work one more path in two exact steps, because a single annual draw hides the way the integral adds. Keep S at 0 equal to 100, mu equal to 0.1, and sigma equal to 0.2. Split the year into two half-years, so each width is 0.5 and the square root of the width is the square root of one half, about 0.7071. The log drift per unit time is still 0.08, so each half-year contributes 0.08 times 0.5, which is 0.04, to the exponent, before the noise. Suppose the first standard normal is 1 and the second is negative 0.5. The first Wiener increment is 0.7071 times 1, about 0.7071. The noise piece in the exponent is sigma times that increment, 0.2 times 0.7071, about 0.1414. The first exponent is 0.04 plus 0.1414, about 0.1814. The exponential of 0.1814 is about 1.1989. The level after the first half-year is about 119.892. The second Wiener increment is 0.7071 times negative 0.5, about negative 0.3536. The noise piece is 0.2 times negative 0.3536, about negative 0.0707. The second exponent is 0.04 plus negative 0.0707, about negative 0.0307. The exponential of negative 0.0307 is about 0.9698. Multiply 119.892 by 0.9698 and the level at the end of the year is about 116.266.

Check that product against the closed form on the whole year, which is the point of having an integral rather than a ritual of steps. The total Wiener increment is 0.7071 plus negative 0.3536, about 0.3535. The year-long exponent is 0.08 plus 0.2 times 0.3535, which is 0.08 plus about 0.0707, about 0.1507. The exponential of 0.1507 is about 1.1627, and 100 times 1.1627 is about 116.27. The two half-steps and the one year formula agree. They agree because the exponents add and the Wiener increments add, which is what constant coefficients allow. If a lab step and the closed form disagree on the same normals, the step is not implementing this integral.

The Euler update on the same two halves does not agree, and the gap is the discretization, not a new model. Start at 100. The first Euler increment is mu S dt plus sigma S dW, which is 0.1 times 100 times 0.5, plus 0.2 times 100 times 0.7071, which is 5 plus 14.142, which is 19.142. The level becomes 119.142, against the exact 119.892. The second Euler increment uses the new level. Drift piece: 0.1 times 119.142 times 0.5, about 5.957. Diffusion piece: 0.2 times 119.142 times negative 0.3536, about negative 8.425. The net Euler change is about negative 2.468, and the ending level is about 116.675. The exact ending level was about 116.266. On this particular pair of draws the year-end gap is only about four tenths of a point, while the half-year gap was about three quarters of a point. Gaps can shrink or grow depending on the path. The exact method does not need the gap. The Euler method is a different recursion, accurate at these parameters to a fraction of a percent on this one path and not identical.

This path ended near 116.3. The mean at t equal to 1 is about 110.5. One path sitting above the mean is not a bias in the formula and not a failure of the half-step arithmetic. W ended near 0.35, which is a positive draw, and the exponential turns a positive draw into a level above the W equals 0 center, which was about 108.3. The path at 116.3 is above both the center and the mean. A single path is allowed to be. The mean is not a magnet that each skeleton must approach. It is an average over the Gaussian measure on W. Your one W was 0.35. That is the entire explanation of 116.3.

Set mu to 0 and recompute the three reference levels, so a zero drift cannot be confused with a zero log drift. Sigma stays 0.2 and t stays 1 and S at 0 stays 100. The log drift is 0 minus 0.02, which is negative 0.02. The W equals 0 level is 100 times the exponential of negative 0.02, about 100 times 0.9802, about 98.020. The W equals 1 level is 100 times the exponential of negative 0.02 plus 0.2, the exponential of 0.18, about 119.722. The W equals negative 1 level is 100 times the exponential of negative 0.22. The exponential of negative 0.22 is about 0.8025, so the level is about 80.252. The average of these three pictures is 80.252 plus 98.020 plus 119.722, which is 297.994, divided by 3, about 99.331. The true mean, S at 0 times e to the mu t, is 100 times e to the 0, which is 100. The three-picture average came out about 99.3, close to 100 and not equal to it, and the center 98.020 is visibly below 100. With mu equal to 0 the expected price does not move, and the typical log path still decays at one half sigma squared. If a desk tells you a zero expected return means the fan is centered at the starting price, they are describing the mean and not the W equals 0 path. The W equals 0 path is at about 98.02. The mean is 100. Both sentences fit in the same model because the right tail, the W equals 1 picture at about 119.7 against a left tail at about 80.3, is asymmetric.

Shorten the horizon instead of the drift. Return mu to 0.1 and sigma to 0.2, and set t equal to 0.5. The mean is 100 times e to the 0.05. The exponential of 0.05 is about 1.05127, so the mean is about 105.127. The log contribution is 0.08 times 0.5, which is 0.04, and e to the 0.04 is about 1.04081, so the W equals 0 level is about 104.081. The gap has shrunk to about 1.046, from about 2.188 on the full year, which is what you expect when both the mean exponent and the variance of the log are proportional to t. Half the time, a smaller Jensen gap. None of this required a new theorem. It is the same pair of expressions, S at 0 times e to the mu t, and S at 0 times e to the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, evaluated at a different t.

Take the partial test to one more awkward price so the cancellation is not a trick of round hundreds. Let S equal 7 and sigma equal 0.1. One half sigma squared is 0.005. One half sigma squared S squared is 0.005 times 49, which is 0.245. The second derivative of the log is negative 1 over 49. The product is 0.245 times negative 1 over 49. But 0.245 is 0.005 times 49, so the product is 0.005 times 49 times negative 1 over 49, which is negative 0.005. The 49 cancels. Negative one half sigma squared is negative 0.005. If a leftover factor of 7, or of 49, is sitting in your correction, you differentiated 1 over S into something other than negative 1 over S squared, or you multiplied by sigma S rather than by one half sigma squared S squared. The notes are blunt about this because the algebra is short enough to audit. Write every partial. Multiply. Watch the price disappear. Then, and only then, integrate.

The audit leaves you with three numbers you should not mix when you look at the lab. The skeleton is S at 0 times the exponential of the quantity mu minus one half sigma squared, times t, plus sigma times the realized W. On the two-step year that number was about 116.3 for one particular W. The mean is S at 0 times e to the mu t, about 110.5 on the base parameters for one year, about 105.1 for a half year, and exactly the starting price when mu is 0. The exponential of the expected log is the lower one, about 108.3, about 104.1, and about 98.0 in those same three settings. The fan illustrates the skeleton. The closed-form mean is the middle sentence. A call price, when you meet it, will be none of these three, and averaging a few skeletons will not produce it. If the fan and the mean disagree, that is the model working. If the exact step and the closed form disagree on the same W, that is a bug.
